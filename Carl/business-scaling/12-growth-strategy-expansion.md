# Growth Strategy & Expansion Decisions

## TL;DR

- **Only ~1 in 5 growth initiatives succeeds** (Bain/Zook, ~2,000-company study). The base rate is against every expansion idea; the job is to pick moves where the odds are structurally better — which almost always means moves adjacent to a strong core, not leaps.
- **Use the Ansoff matrix as a risk-classifier, not a menu.** Most expansion failures are misclassification failures: teams call a diversification move "product development" and price the risk wrong (Quaker/Snapple lost $1.4B+ in 27 months this way). Sequence from low-risk to high-risk quadrants; let penetration fund development.
- **Size markets bottom-up.** Market size = (# of realistically reachable customers) × (annual revenue per customer). For a niche business, a defensible near-term obtainable share is typically **5–15% of your serviceable market** — anyone projecting more needs a structural reason.
- **Don't expand a facility until location #1 is boring**: 12+ months of positive cash flow, churn <5%, 85–100% capacity with waitlists, documented SOPs, and a manager who runs it without the owner. Most second locations lose money for at least a year — the first location must be able to carry that.
- **For media brands, treat content as distribution, not the destination.** The modern creator playbook (MrBeast/Beast Industries, Alex Cooper/Unwell) uses audience attention as the top of a funnel into owned products, formats, and IP. Expand along *adjacent audiences* and *owned economics*, not just "more content."
- **Buying beats building more often than founders assume** — ~80% of acquired small businesses survive year one vs ~50% of startups surviving five years — but M&A itself fails to create value ~70% of the time. The difference is buying cash flow + audience you already know how to operate vs buying a business you have to learn.
- **Focus is the highest-ROI growth strategy for a small team.** Buffett's 25/5 rule, Jobs's "focus means saying no": for a company under ~20 people, every new growth bet cannibalizes an existing one. Cap concurrent expansion bets at one, maybe two.

## 1. The core question: where does the next dollar of growth come from?

Every growth conversation reduces to four sources, in rising order of risk:

1. **Sell more of what you have to who you already serve** (penetration / retention / pricing / frequency).
2. **Sell new things to who you already serve** (product development — leverages trust and distribution you own).
3. **Sell what you have to people you don't yet serve** (market development — new geography, segment, channel, platform).
4. **Sell new things to new people** (diversification — you know neither the product nor the customer).

The advisor's first job is not to generate growth ideas — clients always have too many. It's to (a) classify each idea honestly by risk, (b) check whether the *core* is actually maxed out (it almost never is), and (c) force sequencing so cheap, high-odds growth funds expensive, low-odds growth.

**Opinionated default for a small operator-led company:** exhaust quadrant 1, run one quadrant-2 or quadrant-3 bet at a time, and treat quadrant 4 as a separate company decision (new P&L, new capital, possibly new entity) — never a side project.

## 2. The Ansoff Matrix in practice

Igor Ansoff, "Strategies for Diversification" (HBR, 1957). Two axes: products (existing/new) × markets (existing/new). Risk increases as you move away from the core — first along one axis, then both.

### The four quadrants, with what they actually look like

| Quadrant | Risk | What it looks like in real life |
|---|---|---|
| **Market penetration** | Lowest | Pricing, retention, upsell, frequency, capacity utilization, better conversion, loyalty programs, "more stores closer together" |
| **Product development** | Moderate | New offers to the existing customer base — a membership tier, a course, a merch line, a premium program |
| **Market development** | Moderate | Same offer, new geography/segment/channel — second location, new platform, new demographic, B2B version of a B2C product |
| **Diversification** | Highest | New offer + new customer — a content company opening a training facility qualifies |

### How practitioners actually use it (StrategyU's applied guidance)

1. **Map initiatives precisely.** List every live and proposed growth effort and classify each honestly. Vague goals ("grow the brand") can't be classified and therefore can't be risk-managed. The hard call is deciding whether something is *genuinely* new or a variant — a premium tier of an existing membership is penetration/development gray area; a facility in a new city is unambiguously market development.
2. **Check portfolio balance.** Over-indexed on penetration → vulnerable to disruption of the core (Kodak: penetration looked "low risk" right up until film died). Over-indexed on diversification → expensive failures in unfamiliar territory.
3. **Sequence risk.** Amazon is the canonical staircase: books (penetration) → broad catalog (product development) → international + marketplace (market development) → AWS (diversification). **Each phase funded the next.** Small companies should copy the sequencing logic, not the endpoint.
4. **Pressure-test classifications.** The Quaker–Snapple failure: Quaker classified Snapple as product development (another beverage, like Gatorade). In reality the distribution networks, brand identity, and marketing model were all new — a diversification move priced as an adjacency. Bought for $1.7B, sold 27 months later for $300M.
5. **Know the counter-example.** Starbucks generated most of its growth from the "boring" quadrant — market penetration ("more stores, closer together," loyalty, pricing) — while its ventures outside it (Teavana, the Kraft partnership) underperformed. Penetration is unglamorous and systematically underrated.

### Limitations Carl should keep in mind

- Binary "new/existing" hides a spectrum; the honest question is "what % of the required capabilities do we already have?"
- The matrix tells you *what kind* of risk you're taking, not *how* to execute.
- Competitive dynamics can invert it: penetration in a dying market is riskier than development into a growing one.

**Mayday/Neptune note:** Neptune Performance is a true diversification move relative to Mayday Media (new product, new customer). That's fine — but it should be run with diversification discipline: own P&L, own launch criteria, own kill criteria, and no assumption that media skills transfer automatically. The one real synergy — Mayday's audience as customer-acquisition channel for Neptune — is an *asset to be tested*, not a given.

## 3. Adjacency growth: the Zook/Bain doctrine

Chris Zook & James Allen (*Profit from the Core*, *Beyond the Core*, Bain & Company; ~2,000 companies studied):

- **Only 1 in 5 growth strategies succeeds.** This is the single most important base rate in growth advising.
- Sustained profitable growth comes overwhelmingly from **adjacency moves**: expansions related to, but not part of, the core — new product lines, new channels, new customer segments, new geographies — chosen because they "stem directly from what the company already knows and does best."
- **Distance from core kills.** The further a move sits from existing customers, capabilities, and economics, the lower the odds. A useful mental scorer: count the "steps away" (new customer? new product? new channel? new geography? new cost structure? new competitors?). One step = adjacency. Two steps = risky. Three+ = effectively a startup.
- **Repeatability wins.** The best growers ("successful repeaters") found *one* adjacency formula and ran it again and again (e.g., Nike entering sport after sport with the same playbook: shoes → apparel → equipment → athlete endorsement, per sport). Two traits: extraordinary discipline with rigorous screens before every move, and formulas built from **deep study of their customers and their customers' economics**.
- Practical implication: a small company's best growth question is not "what else could we do?" but "**what move have we already done once successfully, and where can we run the identical play again?**" For a media brand that has launched one successful format, the repeatable play is launching the next format for the same audience — not launching a different business.

## 4. Market sizing for niche businesses

### TAM / SAM / SOM, defined tightly

- **TAM** — total revenue if you had 100% of the market. Vision number.
- **SAM** — the slice your actual model and geography can serve. Strategy number.
- **SOM** — what you can realistically capture near-term given competition and your sales/marketing capacity. Operating-plan number. It's the only one that should drive hiring and spend.

### Bottom-up is the only method that matters for niche businesses

**Formula: Market size = # of potential customers × annual revenue per customer (ACV).**

Top-down ("the sports training market is $X billion, we need 1%") reads as lazy to investors and is useless for operating decisions. Bottom-up builds from countable units: customers you can name, prices you actually charge. It produces a *smaller* number than analyst reports — and a defensible one. Best practice: do both and use top-down only to sanity-check the bottom-up.

**Benchmark:** for an early-stage niche business, a realistic near-term **SOM is 5–15% of SAM**, depending on competitive concentration and category maturity. Anyone modeling 30%+ needs a structural advantage (only facility in town, exclusive audience, locked-in channel).

### Worked example — local training facility (the Neptune shape)

1. Define the catchment: families realistically drive **≤25–30 minutes** for weekly training. Count youth baseball/softball players in that radius (Little League + travel + HS rosters — countable from league registrations).
2. Segment by willingness to pay: travel-ball families are the premium segment. Industry data: combination packages (cage time + video analysis + strength work) for the travel demographic average **$2,200–$4,800 per member per year** in premium markets; general memberships run $75–$150/mo basic to $250–$500/mo mid-tier bundles; private lessons $60–$125/hr.
3. SAM = (# players in radius who train year-round) × (blended annual spend). SOM = 5–15% of that in years 1–2, more if competition is thin.
4. Sanity check against facility economics: a 10,000–15,000 sq ft facility (4–6 cages, 2 tunnels, small turf infield) typically generates **$250K–$400K/year**; 20,000–50,000 sq ft climate-controlled facilities report **$1.2M–$3.5M**. Indoor sports facilities overall: revenue ~$100K–$1M+, **net margins typically 10–20%**, rent the largest cost, **utilization the #1 profit driver**. If your bottom-up SOM exceeds what a facility that size has ever produced, the model is wrong.
5. Add non-membership revenue lines explicitly (they're not rounding errors): 50–100 unlimited members = $7K–$17.5K MRR; winter leagues $18K–$30K per 8–10 week season; weekend tournaments/rentals $6K–$15K; camps, parties, pro shop. Memberships average only ~28% of facility revenue industry-wide, coaching/training ~35%.

### Worked example — media/audience business

Bottom-up for a media brand is audience math, not industry math:
- Sponsorship SAM = (realistic annual views/impressions) × (achievable CPM for the niche) + (# integratable videos × integration rate).
- Product SAM = (audience size) × (plausible conversion to buyers, usually 0.5–3% for merch/courses off free content) × (AOV × purchases/yr).
- Growth in this SAM comes from either more attention (audience growth) or more revenue per unit of attention (better monetization) — sizing forces you to say which lever a given initiative pulls.

### Market-sizing mistakes (recurring)

- Inflating past the true target audience ("everyone who likes baseball" vs "travel-ball families within 30 min").
- No geographic boundary on a physically-bounded business.
- Top-down citation with no original research.
- Indefensible pricing assumptions (using aspirational price, not the price customers currently pay someone).
- Static TAM — not showing whether the market itself is growing or shrinking.

## 5. Geographic expansion for facilities

### The readiness gate (do not pass without all of these)

Synthesized from gym/fitness operator literature (Gymdesk, Zen Planner, Glofox, Motion Mentors):

**Financial readiness**
- **12+ consecutive months of positive cash flow** at location #1
- Monthly churn **<5%**
- **6 months of operating expenses in reserve**
- Consistently hitting revenue goals; one benchmark used by gym-scaling advisors: **~$20K/month profit** at location one before contemplating two — "if not, you haven't cracked the model"

**Demand readiness**
- 85–100% of member capacity; waitlists on popular classes/slots; full peak hours. Expansion should relieve real, measured excess demand — not go hunting for hypothetical demand.

**Operational readiness (the one that actually kills expansions)**
- Documented SOPs for every major function
- A management layer that runs the facility **without the owner's daily presence** ("wait until your current gym feels boring — boring means the systems are doing the work, not you")
- Software for billing/membership/reporting already standardized
- Key roles filled and a bench: GM, assistant manager, head trainer/coach, front desk lead, maintenance

**Budget reality:** a modest second fitness location runs roughly **$120K–$130K** (lease deposit $10–12K, renovation $25–30K, equipment $45–50K, staffing ramp $20K, marketing $5–7K, working capital $15K). Baseball facilities skew higher (turf, cages, HVAC for large clear-span space). Timeline: 1–3 months if buying an existing facility, 6–12 months for a new build-out.

**Expect the second location to lose money for at least a year.** Most do. Location one's cash flow must cover that without starving itself.

### Density beats dispersion

The two most instructive operators in American physical expansion:

- **In-N-Out**: expands only within a **~300–350 mile distribution radius** of its supply/training infrastructure; concentrates markets to strengthen training density and reduce variability. Explicit company position: "our comparatively slow rate of expansion helps us keep an intense focus on quality and friendliness." It has left enormous demand unserved for decades — deliberately.
- **Chick-fil-A**: ~3,000 US locations but only ~150 new units/year; deep penetration of existing regions before entering new ones; the payoff is the highest sales-per-restaurant in fast food.

**Principle for any facility business:** the second location should be close enough that your best people, your brand reputation, and your operating attention transfer. A second facility 20 minutes away shares coaches, marketing, tournaments, and word-of-mouth; one in another metro shares nothing but the logo. Cluster, then jump — never jump first.

### Expansion-failure pattern

Multi-location fitness postmortems agree: failures are **almost never site-selection failures — they're operational**. Systems that couldn't handle 2x volume, undertrained staff, member experience that didn't match the original. And the third location, not the second, is where systems truly get tested (billing consistency, staff coordination, quality drift).

Before-you-sign checklist for a candidate site: population density and youth-sports participation within a 3–10 mile radius, median household income, drive-time patterns, parking, competitor mapping (and specifically what competitors *don't* offer), lease escalation terms, ceiling height/clear-span suitability.

## 6. Audience expansion for media brands

The physical-expansion analog for a media company is *audience* expansion, and the same adjacency logic applies.

### The modern creator playbook: content as distribution

- **MrBeast / Beast Industries**: the defining reframe — "the videos are no longer the destination; they are the distribution." A 500M+ subscriber base functions as owned distribution for Feastables, Beast Games, licensing, and physical products. The flywheel: content grows fanbase → fanbase buys products → product revenue funds bigger content → repeat. By 2025–26 the strategy shifted toward narrative/emotional formats and longer videos to deepen engagement, not just widen reach.
- **Alex Cooper**: *Call Her Daddy* → Unwell Network (a talent/format portfolio) → branded beverage → live events — a deliberate build of assets designed to outlast any single format.
- Creators now present **content slates at upfronts** (calendars, formats, tentpoles, audience data) so brands can buy in early — behaving like networks, not channels. Colin & Samir's framing: "creators are not just making videos, they're building modern media networks."

### The two axes of media expansion (Ansoff, translated)

| | Existing audience | New audience |
|---|---|---|
| **Existing format** | Penetration: upload cadence, packaging (titles/thumbnails), retention, back-catalog optimization | Market development: new platform, new language/geo, algorithm-adjacent topics |
| **New format** | Product development: podcast, newsletter, live show, second channel, membership, merch, courses | Diversification: a genuinely different brand for different people |

Practical rules:
- **New format for the existing audience is the highest-odds move** — you're monetizing trust you already own. This is the repeatable Zook play for creators.
- **New audience with existing format** works when the adjacency is real: the *adjacent audience* is people one topic-step away who the algorithm already co-recommends (check what else your viewers watch). Chasing a demographically different audience with the same content usually dilutes both.
- **Broadening is the classic failure mode**: going "wider" to grow often lowers click-through and watch time among the core, which suppresses distribution to everyone. Audience expansion should be additive series/formats, not a blurring of the flagship.
- **Owned economics is the strategic goal**: creator-led companies increasingly build direct relationships, first-party data (email!), subscriptions, and products explicitly to reduce platform dependency. Every audience-expansion move should be scored partly on whether it increases *owned* audience (email list, memberships, D2C customers) vs merely rented reach.

**Mayday note:** the highest-leverage version of this for a YouTube-first business with sponsors + newsletter + merch is almost always deepening monetization of the existing audience (formats, products, community) before chasing a new audience — and using the audience as the launch channel for Neptune, which is exactly the Beast Industries pattern at small scale.

## 7. Partnership-led growth

Partnerships are the cheapest form of market development: borrowed distribution, borrowed credibility, shared cost.

### The numbers

- Companies with strong alliances grow revenue **~2.5x faster** than those without (McKinsey).
- **64% of SaaS scale-ups** lean on partnerships; partnerships contribute ~**12% of new-business pipeline** on average (Insight Partners) — meaningful but rarely the whole engine.
- **~60% of business alliances break down**, usually from conflicting objectives (HBR). Assume most partnerships die; structure accordingly.
- 80% of leaders see partnerships as increasingly effective for audience reach; some companies get ~25% of revenue from them (HubSpot).

### Types that matter for a small media/facility company

1. **Distribution partnerships** — someone with your target audience carries your offer (leagues/travel clubs feeding a facility; a bigger channel/newsletter cross-promoting a media brand).
2. **Complementary-offer partnerships** — adjacent non-competitors bundle (facility × physical therapist × equipment brand; media brand × complementary creator for a co-owned series).
3. **Strategic alliances with established players** — sponsors evolving into co-built products; a facility becoming a brand's regional training partner.
4. **Content/IP partnerships** — the 2025–26 wave: Tubi × Audiochuck ($150M multi-year), Netflix × iHeart franchises, A24 greenlighting a YouTuber's film. Premium creator IP now attracts institutional capital; a strong format is a partnership asset, not just a revenue line.

### Structuring rules

- Define the mutual benefit in numbers before signing (leads, revenue share, audience delivered) — misaligned objectives are the #1 killer.
- Regular checkpoints with a named owner on each side; partnerships without an owner silently die.
- **Never depend on a single partner** for a material share of revenue or distribution — diversify or maintain an exit path.
- Start with a small, time-boxed pilot (one event, one co-branded series, one season) before any exclusivity.
- Put exclusivity, IP ownership, and audience-data ownership in writing on day one — these are the three things people assume differently about.

## 8. Acquisition of attention vs acquisition of businesses

Two distinct moves that get conflated:

### A. Buying attention (audience assets)

Buying a newsletter, channel, podcast, or media property to acquire its audience. This is the fastest-growing corner of small-cap M&A:

- Creator-economy M&A: **81 deals in 2025, +17% YoY** (52 in H1 2025 alone, +73% YoY). Targets: software 26%, agencies 21%, **media properties 16%**, talent management 14%.
- Landmark deals show the "attention as asset" logic: Paramount bought **The Free Press for $150M**; Fox bought Meet Cute and Red Seat Ventures; Publicis paid $150M for Captiv8; Bending Spoons paid $1.38B for Vimeo. Buyers explicitly value "built-in audience, cultural relevance, and native understanding of the space."
- Pricing is professionalizing: disclosed B2B creator-tool multiples of **~5.0–6.7x revenue** (SocialPilot $50M ≈ 5x ARR; Social Snowball $35M ≈ 6.7x revenue); media properties trade on audience quality + revenue durability, and "vibes-based valuations" are giving way to standardized metrics, compressing bid-ask spreads in the **$10M–$100M** lower-middle market.
- Context: creator economy ≈ **$250B** today (Goldman Sachs), projected **~$530B by 2030**; US creator ad spend ~$37B in 2025, growing 4x faster than overall media.

**When buying attention makes sense for a small operator:** a small newsletter/channel in your exact niche, priced off its (usually weak) monetization, that your existing sales/product engine can immediately monetize better. You're arbitraging your superior monetization against their audience. Diligence essentials: engagement quality over subscriber count (open rates, watch time, list decay), revenue concentration, whether the audience is attached to a *person* who's leaving, and platform-policy transferability of the account/list.

### B. Buying businesses (cash-flow assets)

- Survival asymmetry: **~80% of acquired businesses survive year one**, vs startup failure of ~20% in year one, ~50% by year five, ~70% by year ten. Buying gets you revenue, staff, systems, and product-market fit on day one.
- But value-creation asymmetry cuts the other way: **~70% of M&A fails to create value** (McKinsey; Roger Martin puts the range at 70–90%). The business survives; the *deal* still often destroys value via overpayment and integration failure.
- Reconciling the two: acquisition de-risks *existence* and re-risks *returns*. It works best when (a) you already operate in the niche and are buying capacity/customers/geography — i.e., the acquisition is itself an adjacency move — and (b) the price reflects current performance, with your improvements as upside you don't pay for.
- Facility application: buying an existing struggling cage/training facility is often the cheapest geographic expansion (1–3 months vs 6–12 for a build-out, inherited members, existing lease and fit-out) — *if* the location passes the same site checklist as a new build.

### The decision rule

Build when the asset is your differentiator (content, brand, training methodology). Buy when the asset is a commodity you need at scale (audience in an adjacent niche, a fitted-out space, a functioning back office). Partner when you need it temporarily or want to test before owning.

## 9. Saying no: focus as strategy

The counterweight to everything above. For companies under ~20 people this section outranks the rest.

- **Porter**: "The essence of strategy is choosing what not to do." A strategy that doesn't forbid anything isn't a strategy.
- **Jobs** (WWDC 1997): "Focusing is about saying no… to the hundred other good ideas." He killed dozens of promising projects to protect the few; the 1997 Apple turnaround began by cutting the product line ~70%.
- **Buffett**: "The difference between successful people and really successful people is that really successful people say no to almost everything." The **25/5 exercise**: list 25 goals, circle the top 5 — the other 20 become the *avoid-at-all-costs* list, because they're appealing enough to steal time from the top 5. Mediocre opportunities, not bad ones, are the real threat.
- **McKeown, *Essentialism***: "the disciplined pursuit of less" — if it isn't a clear yes, it's a no; trade-offs are real and pretending otherwise means the trade-off gets made for you, badly.
- **The conglomerate evidence**: diversified companies underperform focused ones in growth, return on capital, and stock performance (McKinsey). Classic failures — Anheuser-Busch into snacks, EMI into CAT scanners — shared the same shape: underestimated new competitors, overestimated capability transfer.

### Capacity math for a small team

A founder-led company has roughly one "strategic slot" per senior operator. Each concurrent growth bet needs a full-attention owner, or it becomes a zombie project that consumes 20% of everyone's time and produces nothing. Practical caps:
- **One major expansion bet at a time** (a facility launch *is* the bet; nothing else major runs concurrently).
- New bets must pass a **stop-doing test**: name what gets killed or paused to fund it. If nothing is named, the answer is no.
- Revisit quarterly: every initiative re-justifies its slot; sunk cost is not a justification.

## 10. The growth-decision playbook (run this with any client)

1. **Audit the core first.** Quantify headroom in penetration: churn, price (when was the last increase?), utilization/capacity, conversion, frequency. If the core is <80% "maxed" on these, the best growth bet is usually right there.
2. **Inventory every growth idea** on the Ansoff grid. Force honest classification (capability-gap count, not wishful labels).
3. **Bottom-up size the top 2–3 ideas.** Customers you can count × prices someone already pays. SOM at 5–15% of SAM. Kill anything whose realistic SOM doesn't move the P&L ≥15–20% within 2–3 years — too small to be worth a strategic slot.
4. **Score adjacency.** Steps from core (customer/product/channel/geography/cost-model/competitors). One step: proceed. Two: pilot only. Three+: this is a new company; decide it as one.
5. **Check the repeatable formula.** Has the client run this exact play before? What's the template? If it's a first-of-kind move, halve the projected odds.
6. **Choose build / buy / partner** per §8's rule. Get a real price on the buy option even if you expect to build — it anchors the build case.
7. **Gate it.** Define entry criteria (what must be true before committing capital — e.g., the facility readiness gate in §5), a pilot with a budget cap, and **kill criteria written down in advance** (the metric and date at which you stop). Pre-committed kill criteria are the single best defense against escalation of commitment.
8. **Apply the stop-doing test** (§9). Name the funded-from.
9. **Assign one accountable owner** with real capacity, and a review cadence (monthly against the gate metrics).

## Common mistakes

1. **Skipping penetration because it's boring.** The Starbucks lesson: the unsexy quadrant usually holds the cheapest growth. Raising prices 8% beats opening a second location for most sub-$2M businesses.
2. **Misclassifying diversification as adjacency** (Quaker/Snapple). Count capability gaps honestly; "it's all content" / "it's all sports" is not an analysis.
3. **Top-down market sizing.** "1% of a $19B market" is not a plan. If you can't count the customers, you can't have them.
4. **Expanding to escape problems at location/product #1.** Expansion multiplies whatever exists — including broken systems and owner-dependence. Fix, document, *then* replicate.
5. **Jumping geographies instead of clustering.** Distance destroys the transfer of people, reputation, and attention (In-N-Out's 300-mile rule exists for a reason).
6. **Broadening the media brand to chase scale**, diluting the flagship's appeal to its core and suppressing algorithmic distribution to everyone.
7. **Underestimating the loss year.** Second locations typically lose money for 12+ months; new formats typically underperform the flagship for a long time. Underfunded expansions die in month 8, not month 1.
8. **Partnership without a named owner and written terms** on exclusivity, IP, and audience data — the 60% alliance failure rate is mostly this.
9. **Paying for your own upside in an acquisition.** Price current performance; your improvements are your return, not the seller's.
10. **Running three bets at once with a ten-person team.** Zombie projects. One slot, one bet, one owner.
11. **No kill criteria.** Escalation of commitment turns a cheap failed pilot into an expensive failed strategy.
12. **Treating a survived acquisition as a successful one.** The business persisting (80% do) says nothing about whether the deal returned the capital (~70% don't).

## Questions Carl should ask

**On the core**
- What's your churn/retention, and when did you last raise prices? What happens to demand if you raise them 10%?
- What % of capacity (hours, cage slots, upload slots, sponsor inventory) are you actually selling?
- If you did nothing new for 12 months and just executed better, how much would revenue grow?

**On any expansion idea**
- Which Ansoff quadrant is this, honestly? What capabilities does it require that you don't currently have — list them.
- Can you count the customers? Walk me through the bottom-up: how many, at what price that someone *already pays*?
- What's the SOM if you get 10% of the serviceable market — does that number even matter to your P&L?
- Have you run this exact play before? What happened?
- What has to be true for this to work, and how will you test the riskiest assumption for under $5K / one month?

**On facility expansion specifically**
- Has location #1 been cash-flow positive for 12 straight months with churn under 5%? Is it at 85%+ capacity with waitlists?
- Could your team run it for a month without you? Where are the SOPs?
- Do you have 6 months of opex in reserve *plus* a year of losses at the new site?
- Is the new site inside your "cluster radius" — can staff, reputation, and members flow between locations?

**On media/audience expansion**
- Is this a new format for your existing audience, or your existing format for a new audience? (Different risk classes.)
- What do your viewers already watch/read that you don't make? (That's the adjacency map.)
- Does this move grow *owned* audience (email, members, customers) or just rented reach?
- What does this do to the flagship's output and quality? Who exactly is making it?

**On buy vs build vs partner**
- What would it cost to buy this capability/audience instead of building it? Have you actually looked at what's for sale?
- If the partnership ends in 12 months, what do you keep?
- Who owns the audience data / the IP / the customer relationship in this deal?

**On focus**
- What are your top 5 priorities — and what's on the avoid-at-all-costs list?
- If we green-light this, what stops? Name it.
- What's the kill metric and the kill date? Say it now, before you're invested.
