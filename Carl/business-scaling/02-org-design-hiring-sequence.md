# Org Design & the Hiring Sequence

Who to hire, in what order, in a founder-led business — and how to avoid the two classic deaths: hiring so far ahead of demand you burn cash on idle payroll, or hiring so far behind that the founder becomes the permanent bottleneck and growth caps at the founder's calendar.

## TL;DR

- **The right next hire is whichever one relieves the binding constraint.** Not the most impressive title, not what peers are hiring. Diagnose the bottleneck first; the role falls out of the diagnosis.
- **First leverage hire is almost always an assistant (EA scope), not a manager.** Until founder time is unblocked, every other hire underperforms because the founder is the review bottleneck. Dan Martell's replacement ladder puts EA at Level 1 for exactly this reason.
- **Sequence beats talent.** "The wrong sequence kills more startups than the wrong people." A great senior hire dropped into a company without an operating spine burns out in ~6 months.
- **Hire generalists until a function has stable, repeating work; then hire the specialist.** Specialists fail in chaos not because they lack skill but because the setup isn't right for them.
- **Bad hires are expensive and common:** DOL pegs the floor at 30% of first-year earnings; SHRM replacement cost is 50–200% of salary by seniority; ~74% of employers admit to at least one bad hire; average reported loss $17K, executive-level $240K+. Slow down 1–2 weeks at the decision point — it's cheap insurance.
- **Hire slightly behind demand for permanent roles, slightly ahead for capacity you can flex (contractors).** A healthy company feels ~10–20% understaffed. Hold 9–12 months of a hire's fully loaded cost before committing to a permanent role.
- **Convert a contractor to employee when the relationship already looks like employment** — set hours, day-to-day supervision, your tools, >80% of their income, business-critical work past ~12 months. Misclassification penalties can exceed 2–3 years of the cost difference, so proactive conversion wins.
- **Use a scorecard (mission / outcomes / competencies), never a job description.** Geoff Smart's A Method: Scorecard → Source → Select → Sell. An A Player = 90% chance of achieving outcomes only the top 10% of candidates could achieve.

## 1. The core principle: hire against the constraint, not the org-chart fantasy

Founder-led businesses don't fail from missing roles; they fail from mis-sequenced roles. The pattern across every credible framework (EOS, the A Method, Martell's replacement ladder, PostHog's ops-hire essay, the first-10-hires literature):

1. **Identify the binding constraint** — the thing that caps growth this quarter. It's usually one of: founder time, delivery capacity, cash visibility, or lead flow.
2. **Hire (or contract) the cheapest competent unit of relief for that constraint.** Not the most senior. A $4K/mo EA that returns 15 founder-hours a week beats a $200K COO hired to "get organized."
3. **Re-diagnose after every hire.** The constraint moves. That's the point.

Corollary: a role that would be obviously right at 25 people is often wrong at 8. The same "Head of Marketing" hire is a force multiplier when there's a team and a working funnel, and a very expensive individual contributor when there isn't.

### The founder's two failure modes

| Failure mode | Symptom | Root cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Hiring behind demand** | Founder works 70-hr weeks, turns down revenue, everything routes through them, team waits on approvals | Control addiction; "no one can do it like me"; fear of fixed costs |
| **Hiring ahead of demand** | Payroll grows faster than revenue, people invent work, senior hires with nothing to manage, runway evaporates | Optimism, competitive anxiety, "we'll grow into it" |

Startup Genome's research links premature scaling (including early team expansion before validation) to significantly higher failure rates; ~38% of startup failures tie to cash issues that include scaling teams before the model is validated. On the other side, the founder-as-bottleneck company doesn't die fast — it just stops growing and slowly exhausts the founder. Both are org-design failures.

**Operating heuristic:** aim to feel ~10–20% understaffed at all times. Busy team, clear priorities, no idle fixed cost. Flex the gap with contractors, not permanent hires.

## 2. The founder-leverage stack: EA → Ops → Chief of Staff → Integrator/COO

These four roles get conflated constantly. They are different jobs with different units of value, and there is a correct order.

### 2.1 Executive Assistant (first, almost always)

- **Unit of value: the founder's reclaimed hour.**
- Owns — actually owns, not "helps with" — inbox and calendar. Martell's Level 1: the outcome is "manage inbox and calendar," full stop. Plus meeting prep, travel, follow-through, personal/company logistics collisions.
- **Symptoms that point here:** founder spends 3+ hours/day on coordination; inbox is a backlog; meeting prep happens in the hallway outside the meeting.
- **Cost:** US in-house EA roughly $80–120K; operator-grade remote EAs start around $3K/month and show value in weeks. At creator-business scale, a strong remote EA/ops-assistant hybrid is usually the correct first non-production hire.
- Martell's stronger claim: you can't afford NOT to hire an assistant early — every founder-minute spent on admin is a minute not spent on the market, sales, or delivery. The ROI of someone pulling deals forward in the calendar pays for the role by itself.

**Buyback Rate math (Martell):** annual income ÷ 2,000 hours ÷ 4 = the hourly rate below which you should delegate everything. Example: $140K income → $70/hr → **$17.50/hr buyback rate**. Anything on the founder's plate that can be done for less than that number is a delegation candidate, and the 4× divisor bakes in the ROI requirement.

### 2.2 First Ops hire (operations manager / ops generalist)

- **Unit of value: a reliability metric that moves** (things ship on time, payroll runs, nothing catches fire).
- PostHog's framing: early-stage ops is "a 100% defensive position" — preventing disasters, payroll/benefits/expenses, fixing problems. System-building comes later.
- **Timing:** common guidance clusters around employee 8–10 (Kruze and others); PostHog says definitely before ~50. For a small media company the trigger is simpler: when recurring operational work (invoicing, sponsor deliverable tracking, scheduling, contractor payments) consumes >1 founder-day/week.
- **The three attributes that matter more than experience (PostHog):**
  1. **Extreme optimism** — anyone at any level must be able to ask them for help without fearing annoyance; no request too trivial.
  2. **Willingness to do the unglamorous** — unflappable when blamed for others' mistakes; nothing is "beneath them."
  3. **Total dependability** — they'll hold banking access, passwords, payroll data. If you don't trust them with top-level admin access to everything, don't hire them yet; wait for someone you do.
- **Anti-signals:** annoyance at routine requests, task snobbery, strict 9–5 availability for a role that owns emergencies.

### 2.3 Chief of Staff (leverage for a leadership *team*, not a solo founder)

- **Unit of value: a strategic project that ships.**
- Runs leadership cadence, decision documentation, cross-functional initiatives, board/investor prep.
- **Symptoms:** leadership meetings produce decisions that evaporate; the same strategic project has been "in progress" two quarters; departments only sync when the founder forces it.
- **Cost:** $160–250K US market. That price means most sub-15-person companies should not buy this; a strong EA plus a founder who runs a weekly cadence covers 80% of it.
- Sequencing consensus: 3–15 person founder-led companies that need more than an EA usually want CoS-type leverage before a COO — the constraint is CEO leverage, not operational ownership. 15–50 person companies with real cross-functional complexity start needing the COO.

### 2.4 Integrator / COO (the EOS "Rocket Fuel" pairing)

Gino Wickman's EOS framework (Traction, Rocket Fuel) names the two seats at the top of the Accountability Chart:

- **Visionary** (usually the founder): new ideas & R&D, creative problem solving, major external relationships, culture, closing big deals. Strong on big-picture; struggles with routine detail and follow-through.
- **Integrator**: leads/manages/holds the leadership team accountable, executes the business plan and owns P&L results, integrates the major functions, resolves cross-functional issues, owns organizational communication. Titles vary: COO, president, GM.

Key EOS claims worth carrying:

- **Under ~10–25 employees, one person can hold both seats.** Beyond that, a founder wearing both hats oscillates between chaos (visionary mode) and stagnation (integrator mode), and growth constrains.
- The pairing works only with complementary skills, mutual respect, trust within domains, and a **weekly Visionary–Integrator 1:1** to align on priorities and issues.
- **Hiring an Integrator is high-risk: up to 40% of new Integrators fail within the first 12 months** (Rocket Fuel ecosystem data). This is the single most expensive mis-hire a founder-led company makes. De-risk it: promote a proven internal operator when possible, use a fractional integrator first, and never hire an Integrator to "fix" a company that hasn't defined its operating cadence — they integrate an existing machine, they don't conjure one.

### The sequence in one line

**EA (unblock founder time) → Ops manager (make delivery reliable) → Chief of Staff (make leadership coordinated) → Integrator/COO (own the whole operating system).** Skipping levels is how you get a $200K COO doing calendar management.

The classic compression mistake: hiring one junior "ops person" to be EA + CoS + ops manager simultaneously. You get weak execution across all three instead of strength in one. Better: a senior operator with **focused scope**, and contract/fractional coverage for the rest.

## 3. Generalists vs. specialists early

### The rule

- **Generalist when:** the area needs to be figured out, built from scratch, and will change shape as the business changes. Early stage is almost all of this. Generalists ("athletes who can play any position") are cost-efficient — one hire covers what would take 2–3 specialists — and they tolerate ambiguity.
- **Specialist when:** you have a hypothesis, direction, and early traction, and need someone to operationalize and optimize a known motion. Also: any area where amateur mistakes are expensive or regulated — paid ads at real budget, complex infrastructure, legal/compliance, tax.
- **The trap:** specialists need clear goals, runway, and working systems. Drop a specialist into chaos and they underperform — not from lack of skill, but because the environment can't use them. If the role's inputs don't exist yet, the specialist will spend six months building generalist scaffolding badly.

### The T-shaped compromise

Best early hires are **T-shaped**: broad competence across areas, deep expertise in one. For a creator business that's e.g. "an editor who can also shoot and thumbnail," or "an ops person who can also handle sponsor comms." First 5 hires should nearly all be T-shaped; pure specialists earn their slot once a function has stable, repeating volume.

### The shift point

Roles stabilize → work becomes repeating → the generalist's 70%-good output starts costing real money → hire the specialist and move the generalist to the next fire. The generalist-to-specialist transition typically starts around hires 4–6 (see sequencing below) and is mostly complete by ~25 people. Handled badly, this is where early generalists quit — get ahead of it by telling generalists explicitly that their job is to keep moving to the newest, least-defined problem, and that specialists arriving behind them is success, not demotion.

## 4. Hiring ahead vs. behind demand

### Default: behind demand for permanent heads, ahead of demand with flexible capacity

- Expand permanent headcount on **validated, persistent demand** — not temporary overwhelm. Short-term spikes never justify permanent heads; they justify contractors and overtime.
- **Legitimate ahead-of-demand cases:** (a) you're declining revenue for lack of capacity — the demand exists, it's just unmet; (b) roles with long ramps (senior sales, anything needing 3–6 months to productivity) where waiting until you're drowning means recruiting badly and onboarding worse; (c) a known future event with a hard date (facility opening, program launch) — hire back-scheduled from the date by ramp time.
- **Cash rule:** hold **9–12 months of the hire's fully loaded cost** in stable reserves, separate from opex; conservative advisors say 18 months for slower-revenue businesses. Fully loaded ≈ salary × 1.25–1.4 (payroll taxes, benefits, tools, workspace).
- Revenue floor heuristic for a first hire in a small services/product business: roughly **$5–20K MRR** with acceptable churn and a repeatable acquisition motion — proof that customers pay, before adding fixed cost.

### The Startup Genome caution

Premature scaling — team expansion before the model is validated — is among the most reliable predictors of startup failure. The correction (layoff, down-round, or death) is always more painful than the delay would have been. When in doubt: contractor first, convert later (see §7).

### Facility-business note (relevant to a training facility like Neptune)

Physical facilities invert the default: some roles must be hired **ahead** of revenue because the facility can't open or legally operate without them (lead trainer, whoever holds keys/insurance-required supervision). Treat those as part of the launch capex/opening budget, not as ongoing opex to be justified by revenue — and staff everything else (front desk, additional coaches) behind demonstrated booking volume, using part-time/contract shifts to flex.

## 5. Reference org shapes: 5, 10, 25 people

Stage names from the small-business org literature: **owner-led (1–10) → function-led (10–25) → manager-supported (25–50)**. Span-of-control guide: ~5 direct reports typical, 8 max per leader.

### ~5 people: owner-led, flat

```
Founder (Visionary + Integrator, both seats)
├─ EA / ops assistant          (founder leverage)
├─ Maker #1                    (core product: editor, coach, engineer…)
├─ Maker #2
└─ Revenue/customer generalist (sales, sponsors, community — often part-founder)
```
- No managers. Everyone reports to the founder. Structure is a task board and a weekly meeting, not an org chart.
- The org-design work at this size is **role clarity, not hierarchy**: one written owner per recurring outcome (who owns publishing? who owns getting paid?).
- Creator-business flavor (per Colin & Samir / Samir Chaudry's "4 roles"): the maker seats are typically **editor first** (MrBeast's first hire was an editor), then **producer** ("ensure content excellence and make sure content meets its deadline" — guests, schedules, coordination), with **channel manager** (uploads, thumbnails, clips, distribution) and **strategist** (retention data, packaging, series pitches) arriving later or as fractional/contract roles.

### ~10 people: function-led emerging

```
Founder/CEO (still both seats, starting to strain)
├─ EA
├─ Ops/Finance generalist        → owns books, payroll, vendors, cash reporting
├─ Delivery lead (working lead)  → 2–4 makers
│   ├─ Maker
│   ├─ Maker
│   └─ Maker/contractor pool
├─ Revenue lead                  → sales/sponsors/partnerships
└─ Marketing/audience generalist
```
- First "leads" appear but they're **working leads** (player-coaches), not pure managers.
- The atdrate first-10 framework maps cleanly here — hires 1–3 are the **operating spine** (ops/CoS, finance owner, customer/sales lead), hires 4–6 are **domain specialists** (performance marketing or product, senior fulfillment, people-ops), hires 7–10 are the **scale layer** (head of growth with a team to lead, senior finance, tech lead, plus one slot held open for the bottleneck you didn't predict).
- Its central warning again: **spine before seniority.** A VP hired before the spine exists burns out in six months.

### ~25 people: manager-supported, Integrator question is live

```
CEO/Visionary
└─ Integrator/COO (or founder still holding the seat — decision point)
    ├─ Head of Delivery/Production   → 2 team leads → makers
    ├─ Head of Revenue               → AE/partnerships + account mgmt
    ├─ Head of Marketing/Audience    → 2–3 ICs
    ├─ Finance (controller or strong fractional CFO + bookkeeper)
    └─ People/Ops manager
```
- This is the EOS threshold band (10–25) where one person holding Visionary + Integrator stops working. Either the founder consciously becomes the Integrator and hires creative/vision leadership, or (more commonly) hires/promotes the Integrator and stays Visionary.
- Add manager layers **selectively, only where coaching and decision load demand it** — not uniformly.
- Every seat goes on an EOS-style **Accountability Chart**: seats defined by the 5 roles each owns, one name per seat, before you think about the people. Structure first, then people into structure — never draw the chart around incumbent personalities.

### Multi-business note (media company + facility under one roof)

Shared-services model: finance, people-ops, and founder-office (EA) are shared across both businesses; delivery and revenue functions are per-business with their own leads. Do NOT share a delivery lead across a content business and a facility — the cadences (daily publishing vs. scheduled coaching blocks) are incompatible and one side always starves. The Integrator seat, when it comes, sits above both.

## 6. Hiring machinery: the A Method (Who, Smart & Street)

The single most-adopted hiring framework in the operator world. Four steps:

### 6.1 Scorecard — not a job description

Three parts, written before sourcing anything:
- **Mission** — one sentence on why the role exists ("Own sponsor delivery end-to-end so no deliverable is ever late or renegotiated").
- **Outcomes** — 3–8 specific, measurable deliverables ("Reduce founder editing hours to zero by month 3"; "All invoices out within 48h of deliverable").
- **Competencies** — how the work must be done, including cultural (e.g., PostHog's ops triad above works as a competency list).

"You can only hire the right person with a scorecard." Vague roles produce B/C hires by default because everyone interviews against a different imaginary job.

### 6.2 Source — before you need people

Referrals beat advertising, always. Standing habit: ask every strong person you meet "who are the best people you've ever worked with?" and keep a bench list. For creator businesses, niche boards (YT Jobs) and your own audience are unusually good sourcing channels — your best future producer may already watch the channel.

### 6.3 Select — four structured interviews

1. **Screening call** (~30 min phone): kills 80–90% of the pipeline fast. What are your career goals? What are you great at? Not great at? How did your last bosses rate you?
2. **Who/Topgrading interview** (1.5–3+ hrs, chronological): for each job — what were you hired to do, what accomplishments are you proud of, what were the low points, who was your boss and what will they say when we call, why did you leave. Patterns across the chronology are the signal; single answers aren't.
3. **Focused interview(s)** (45–60 min each): one scorecard outcome or competency per interviewer, deep.
4. **Reference interviews** — you make the calls, to bosses the candidate named, asking the same questions. The tell: lukewarm references. "Formerly worked with" enthusiasm gaps are data. Treat references as among the most valuable tools in the process, not a formality.

Grade A/B/C against the scorecard; **only hire As** — defined as ≥90% likelihood of achieving outcomes that only the top 10% of candidates could achieve. A strong practical addition from the first-10-hires playbooks: a **paid 2–4 hour working session** on a real business problem before final sign-off — observing actual operating mode beats any interview answer.

### 6.4 Sell — throughout, not just at offer

The 5 Fs candidates actually weigh: **Fit, Family, Freedom, Fortune, Fun.** Small companies lose As by assuming the offer sells itself; they win by selling autonomy (Freedom), mission (Fit), and the ride (Fun) against bigger-company Fortune.

### Compensation posture for hires 1–10 (from the same literature)

- Base at 80–90% of market + 10–20% quarterly performance bonus tied to written objectives.
- Equity/upside reserved mostly for hires 1–4 (in startup form: ~1.5–3% each for hires 1–2, sliding to 0.1–0.4% by hires 8–10, 12-month cliff). For non-VC businesses like a creator company, translate to profit-share or revenue-linked bonuses — the principle (early joiners share upside) transfers even when equity doesn't.

## 7. Contractor → employee conversion

Creator businesses and facilities both start contractor-heavy (editors, thumbnail artists, coaches on per-session rates). Two forces eventually push conversion: **retention/integration** (your best contractor gets poached or drifts) and **legal exposure** (the relationship already is employment in everything but paperwork).

### When to convert

- **Tenure:** 3–12 months is the evaluation window; **business-critical, ongoing work past ~12 months is a strong conversion signal.**
- **Control red flags — you're already treating them as an employee if:** you set strict hours, supervise day-to-day tasks, require your tools/software/process, or provide benefit-like perks. The IRS reads specific instructions ("use this software, follow this 10-step process, work 9–5") as employment.
- **Exclusivity:** if you're >80% of their income or they work only for you, most jurisdictions presume employment.
- **Business logic:** you want exclusivity, priority access, or deep integration you can't demand from a contractor.

### The cost math

- Contractors carry a rate premium because they self-fund taxes, insurance, benefits. Converting, expect the **compensation package to need a 15–45% adjustment** (varies by jurisdiction) to keep the person whole on net pay once benefits/withholding shift.
- Employer adds: payroll taxes (FICA employer share), unemployment insurance, workers' comp, benefits. Rule of thumb: fully loaded W-2 cost ≈ 1.25–1.4× salary.
- **Against that:** misclassification exposure. Penalties include back FICA for all misclassified years, state unemployment and workers' comp back-payments, unpaid overtime, and liquidated damages that **double back wages** — up to ~$25K/worker in fines in the US, more in class actions. **Retroactive costs routinely exceed 2–3 years of the contractor-vs-employee cost difference — proactive conversion is almost always cheaper than getting caught.** A single unemployment claim from a terminated contractor can trigger an audit of every contractor you've used in three years.

### Conversion playbook (~6–10 weeks, per BambooHR)

1. Define the role in writing — responsibilities, reporting line, hours (1–2 wks).
2. Verify classification status and compliance; EOR if cross-border (1–2 wks).
3. Build the offer — level tenure as experience, sell stability + benefits, land inside your pay bands (1–2 wks).
4. Paperwork: I-9, W-4, employment contract, benefits enrollment (1 wk).
5. Onboard like a new hire — HR processes + what changes in the role (2–4 wks).
6. Communicate internally (manager briefing, team announcement).
7. Update IP assignment, NDAs, system access, benefits eligibility dates.
8. Set explicit expectations: what changes (hours, exclusivity, benefits, review cadence) and what doesn't.

### Keep-as-contractor list

Genuinely project-based, multi-client, own-tools, outcome-billed work should stay contract: occasional motion graphics, one-off legal, seasonal overflow editing, guest coaches running their own book. The test is the work's shape, not your budget preference.

## 8. Cost of a bad hire (the numbers to quote)

| Source | Figure |
|---|---|
| US Dept. of Labor | Bad hire costs **≥30% of first-year expected earnings** (floor) |
| SHRM | Replacement = **50–200% of annual salary**: entry 50–75%, mid 100–150%, exec 200%+ |
| SHRM 2025 benchmarking | Avg cost-per-hire **$5,475** non-exec; **$35,879** executive (up 21% since 2022) |
| CareerBuilder (2024) | **74%** of employers admit a bad hire; avg loss **$17K**, exec-level **$240K+** |
| Gallup | Disengaged employee costs **$3,400 per $10K of salary** in lost productivity |
| Management drag | Managers spend **~17% of the workweek** supervising/correcting poor performers |
| Morale | 85% of HR pros report one bad hire damages team morale/productivity; secondary departures of top performers amplify cost 50–200% each |

Detection timeline: warning signs surface at 30–90 days; by six months most bad hires are identifiable. **The discipline that matters is acting at 90 days, not 9 months** — the cost curve is roughly linear in time-to-exit, and everyone on the team already knows before the founder admits it.

At small scale the percentages understate reality: in a 6-person company a bad hire is ~17% of the org — it distorts culture, consumes the founder's coaching capacity, and blocks the seat an A player would have filled. Practical implication: taking 1–2 extra weeks to run the full A Method process is trivially cheap against a $17K–240K downside; strong onboarding (structured 30-60-90) improves retention by ~82% (Brandon Hall).

On "hire slow, fire fast": the *fire fast* half is well-supported (see detection timeline). The *hire slow* half is contested for small companies — A players won't wait through six interview rounds. Resolution: **hire rigorously, not slowly** — compress the A Method into 2–3 weeks of dense process rather than 2–3 months of drift.

## 9. Common mistakes

1. **Hiring seniority before spine.** VP/Head-of titles before ops+finance+revenue basics exist. The senior hire has no machine to run, builds scaffolding badly, burns out in ~6 months.
2. **Hiring a mini-me instead of a complement.** Visionary founders hiring another visionary; the company gets two idea generators and zero execution. Rocket Fuel exists because the pairing must be complementary.
3. **Hiring an Integrator/COO to create order that the founder won't submit to.** Up to 40% of new Integrators fail inside 12 months — usually because the Visionary keeps overriding them. If the founder won't give the Integrator real authority (and a weekly 1:1), don't make the hire.
4. **The three-in-one ops unicorn.** One junior hire asked to be EA + chief of staff + operations manager. Weak at all three. Hire focused scope.
5. **Delegating unsolved problems.** "Solve first, then delegate" — hiring someone to figure out what the founder hasn't figured out (first salesperson before any founder-led sales; marketer before any message works) fails predictably. A hire makes sense when the process works and someone needs to run it. (Exception: true specialists in domains the founder should never learn — tax, legal.)
6. **Staying the top individual contributor.** The founder remains the best editor/seller/coach, reviews everything, and caps the org at their throughput. What built the company inverts into the thing limiting it — silently.
7. **Hiring for the company you plan to be.** Over-hiring on optimism/runway confidence. The correction (layoffs) always costs more than the delay would have.
8. **No scorecard, gut-feel interviews.** Unstructured interviews are barely predictive; every interviewer assesses a different imaginary job. Scorecard + structured chronological interview + real reference calls.
9. **Skipping reference calls or treating them as formality.** Lukewarm past-boss enthusiasm is the single cheapest bad-hire detector available.
10. **Keeping long-term core contractors misclassified to save 20%.** The back-tax + liquidated-damages exposure dwarfs the savings, and one unemployment claim can audit your entire contractor history.
11. **Letting early generalists feel demoted by incoming specialists.** Lose your most loyal, most context-rich people at exactly the moment you need them redeployed to the next fire. Pre-frame their career path as "permanent first-responder to the newest problem."
12. **Drawing the org chart around people instead of seats.** Accountability chart first (what seats does the business need, what 5 outcomes does each own), then names into seats. One name per seat; one seat per major function.

## 10. Questions Carl should ask a client

**Diagnosing the constraint**
- Walk me through your last full week, hour by hour. What fraction was work only you can do?
- What revenue did you decline or delay in the last 90 days, and why?
- If you disappeared for two weeks, what breaks first? Second?
- What's the one recurring task you'd pay most to never do again? What would doing it cost per hour on the open market? (Compute their buyback rate: income ÷ 2,000 ÷ 4.)

**Testing hire readiness**
- Is this a persistent load or a spike? Show me 3 months of evidence.
- Do you have 9–12 months of this role's fully loaded cost in reserve, separate from opex?
- Has anyone (you included) done this job well here already — is there a working process to hand over, or are you delegating an unsolved problem?
- Could a contractor or fractional cover this for 6 months first? Why not?

**Testing role definition**
- Write the mission of this role in one sentence. Now list 3 measurable outcomes for month 6. (If they can't: scorecard work before sourcing.)
- What are the 3 competencies this person must have that your culture will punish them for lacking?
- Who owns this outcome today? Who will they report to — and does that person have <8 directs and time to manage?

**Founder-leverage stack**
- Do you have someone who fully owns your inbox and calendar? (If no, that's almost certainly the first hire regardless of what they asked about.)
- Do leadership decisions stick, or do the same topics resurface for months? (CoS signal.)
- Are you the Visionary, the Integrator, or trying to be both? Which five roles of each do you actually enjoy? At what headcount will both-seats stop working?

**Contractor exposure**
- List every contractor >6 months tenure. For each: do you set their hours, direct daily work, or require your tools? What % of their income are you? Which ones are business-critical?
- If your best contractor quit tomorrow with two days' notice, what happens?

**Bad-hire hygiene**
- Tell me about your last hiring miss. When did you first suspect, and when did you act? What was the gap?
- Do you call references yourself and ask "would you enthusiastically rehire?"
- What's your 30-60-90 for the next hire, written down?

## Quick-reference: hiring sequence for a ~creator-media business (Mayday-shaped)

1. **Editor(s)** — first production leverage (MrBeast pattern; almost universal for video-first businesses).
2. **EA / ops assistant** — founder inbox, calendar, scheduling, sponsor logistics. Level 1 of the replacement ladder.
3. **Producer** — owns deadlines, guests, production schedule; the "content ships on time" reliability metric.
4. **Ops/finance generalist** — books, payroll, contractor payments, cash reporting (fractional bookkeeper + internal owner works).
5. **Channel manager / distribution** — uploads, thumbnails, clips, cross-platform.
6. **Revenue lead** — sponsors/partnerships once founder-led sales has a repeatable motion.
7. **Strategist** (often fractional) — packaging, retention data, series development.
8. **Creative director** — only when the founder's taste must scale across a team they no longer personally direct.
9. **Integrator/COO conversation** — at ~10–25 total heads across business lines, or when a second business (e.g., a facility) makes the founder the only cross-business coordinator.

For the facility line: lead coach/trainer hires ahead of opening (launch budget), front-desk and additional coaches behind booking volume, coaches on contract only if they genuinely run multi-client books — otherwise W-2 part-time from day one to avoid the misclassification trap.
