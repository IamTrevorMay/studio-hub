# Financial Operations & Cash Flow for Scaling

## TL;DR

- **Cash, not profit, kills businesses.** The widely cited U.S. Bank figure: 82% of small business failures involve cash flow problems. Profitable-on-paper businesses die waiting for receivables. The fix is forecasting cash *timing*, not just P&L.
- **Run a 13-week rolling cash flow forecast, updated weekly.** Weekly granularity over a rolling quarter is the Goldilocks zone — monthly misses crunches, daily is noise. 30–60 minutes a week once built.
- **Forecast cash receipts, never revenue.** A $10K invoice on net-30 is April cash, not March cash. For a sponsorship-driven business (65% of brand deals pay net-30, 25% net-60, 10% net-90+), this distinction is the whole game.
- **Target 10–15% pretax profit after paying the owner a market wage** (Crabtree's Simple Numbers). Below 5% the business is on life support. Owner's comp buried in "whatever's left" makes the financials worthless.
- **Cash cushion: minimum 2 months of opex with zero line-of-credit draws** (Crabtree's core capital target); 3–6 months for lumpy/seasonal revenue; seasonal businesses should size reserves off *peak-season* monthly expenses.
- **Hire finance help by complexity, not just revenue:** outsourced bookkeeper immediately (any real revenue), controller-level rigor around $1–5M or multiple entities/revenue streams, fractional CFO (10–15 hrs/month to start) when big capital decisions loom — a facility buildout qualifies at *any* revenue level.
- **For a facility buildout, match financing to asset life:** equipment financing (8–16% APR, 36–72 mo, closes in days) for gear; SBA 7(a) (up to $5M, 10-yr terms, 25-yr with real estate) for tenant improvements + working capital; SBA 504 (50/40/10 structure) only if buying the building. Never bridge a buildout with merchant cash advances.
- **Negotiate 50% upfront on sponsorships.** Most brands accept it; it halves receivable exposure and is the single highest-leverage cash flow move for a creator business.

## 1. Why cash flow is the discipline that matters

The failure statistic everyone quotes — 82% of small businesses that fail do so because of cash flow problems — traces to a U.S. Bank study and is repeated by SCORE, the U.S. Chamber, and SBA-affiliated content. Read it correctly: cash flow management is *implicated* in most failures, not the sole cause. But the mechanism is consistent and worth internalizing:

1. **Timing mismatch.** Expenses (payroll, rent) are due on the business's cycle; receivables arrive on the customer's cycle. Net-30/60 terms mean two months of float the business must fund.
2. **Revenue optimism.** Founders project sales and spend against the projection before cash lands.
3. **Customer concentration.** One customer at 30%+ of revenue who pays slowly can create a working-capital crisis on *your* payroll cycle, not theirs. (Directly relevant to any business where 1–2 sponsors or one anchor client dominates.)
4. **Growth without financing.** A big new contract requires upfront labor/materials; margins are fine, cash isn't. Growth *consumes* cash before it returns it.

The punchline: businesses that close were often profitable on paper. They couldn't convert profit into cash fast enough to make payroll. So the operating system below is built around cash timing first, accrual profitability second — you need both, but cash is the one with a hard deadline every two weeks.

## 2. The 13-week cash flow forecast (the core tool)

The Thirteen-Week Cash Flow (TWCF) is the standard tool of restructuring professionals and fractional CFOs, and it scales down to a one-person shop. It is a rolling, weekly, direct-method forecast of cash in and cash out over one quarter.

**Why 13 weeks / why weekly:** monthly forecasts miss week-to-week crunches (payroll lands on the 15th, sponsor payment lands on the 28th — a monthly view says you're fine, the 20th says otherwise). Daily is too noisy to maintain. Weekly over a rolling quarter balances detail and foresight.

### Structure (direct method — always)

Three sections:

1. **Cash inflows** — customer/sponsor payments, platform payouts (AdSense, affiliate), merch settlements, loan proceeds, owner injections. Organized by *collection pattern*, not by sales category: group receivables by who pays in 30 days vs 60 vs 90, not by product line.
2. **Cash outflows** — payroll (with exact pay dates), rent, contractors, software, debt service, taxes, capex. Group by *payment control*: fixed obligations vs. variable vs. discretionary. Separating discretionary from non-discretionary lets you run "cash crunch" scenarios in minutes.
3. **Net + cumulative position** — weekly net cash flow, running bank balance, and a minimum-balance line (your floor). Conditional-format anything that dips below the floor.

### Build steps

1. **Pull 12–18 months of history** from the accounting system. Extract: average collection delay by payer type, vendor payment cycles, seasonal patterns.
2. **Set up weekly columns** — a few weeks of actuals plus 13 forward weeks.
3. **Populate inflows by expected *deposit date*.** Use aging reports and documented payer behavior, not invoice dates. If a sponsor historically pays net-60 + 2 weeks of approval delay, model 74 days.
4. **Populate outflows by actual due dates** — payroll runs, rent day, quarterly tax dates, annual insurance premiums, software renewals.
5. **Add scenario toggles** if useful: base / slow-collections / lost-client.

### Weekly maintenance ritual (30–60 min)

- Drop week 1 into actuals; add a new week 13 (the forecast always rolls).
- Reconcile last week's forecast vs. actual. Classify variances as **timing** (payment arrived late/early) vs. **amount** (sales 12% over forecast).
- Update assumptions that drifted — collection lag is the usual culprit.
- Document recurring variance patterns; forecast accuracy compounds. (Practitioner example: consistently shifting collection assumptions one week later fixed most misses.)

### Common TWCF mistakes

- Forecasting **revenue instead of cash receipts** — the #1 killer.
- Organizing categories by accounting convention instead of how cash actually moves.
- Using invoice dates instead of expected collection dates.
- Building something so elaborate nobody updates it. A forecast updated weekly at 80% fidelity beats a beautiful model updated quarterly.
- Not refreshing assumptions when terms change (a sponsor renegotiates to net-60; the model still says net-30).

**Carl's rule:** if a client has ever been surprised by a low bank balance, the 13-week forecast is the first prescription, before any other financial sophistication.

## 3. Profit First (Michalowicz) — the envelope system for businesses

**Core inversion:** Sales − Profit = Expenses (instead of Sales − Expenses = Profit). Take profit off the top mechanically; force the business to run on what's left. It's Parkinson's Law weaponized: expenses expand to consume available cash, so make less cash "available."

### The five accounts

1. **Income** — all revenue lands here first
2. **Profit** — skimmed off top, untouchable for operations
3. **Owner's Comp** — the owner's pay
4. **Tax** — quarterly/annual obligations
5. **Operating Expenses** — everything else runs from here

(Optional sixth: materials/subcontractors, or a dedicated payroll account.)

### Target Allocation Percentages (TAPs) by real revenue

"Real revenue" = total revenue minus materials & subcontractor pass-through costs.

| Real Revenue | Profit | Owner's Pay | Tax | OpEx |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| < $250K | 5% | 50% | 15% | 30% |
| $250K–$500K | 10% | 35% | 15% | 40% |
| $500K–$1M | 15% | 20% | 15% | 50% |
| $1M–$5M | 20% | 10% | 15% | 55% |
| $5M+ | 25% | 5% | 15% | 55% |

Note the shape: as the business scales, owner's pay percentage drops (the owner becomes a salaried executive) while profit percentage rises (the business becomes an asset that throws off distributions).

### Mechanics

- **Allocation rhythm:** twice monthly, typically the 10th and 25th — sweep the income account into the other four at preset percentages.
- **Start where you are:** run an instant assessment to compute Current Allocation Percentages (CAPs) vs. TAPs. If margins are tight, start profit at 1% and ratchet up quarterly. The behavior matters more than the number at first.
- **Quarterly distribution:** take 50% of the profit account balance as a distribution each quarter; leave 50% as retained earnings/emergency buffer.

### When Profit First fails (know before prescribing)

- **High fixed costs:** if opex genuinely consumes 70–80% of revenue (true of a facility with rent + coaching payroll), the standard TAPs are fantasy until the cost structure changes. Don't pretend.
- **Heavy debt service:** loan payments can eat the allocation room; prioritize the debt paydown waterfall (see Crabtree, below) over cosmetic profit accounts.
- **Irregular revenue:** the system assumes reasonably consistent deposits. With lumpy sponsorship income, allocate on *receipt* rather than on a calendar, and hold larger buffers.
- **Adherence decay:** bookkeepers who see many clients report most people set up the accounts and stop maintaining them (Kate Tyson/Wanderwell: "we rarely see people adhere to the system with much discipline"). Five fee-free, no-minimum accounts is also a banking annoyance at traditional banks.
- **It's triage, not a business model.** Tyson's sharpest critique: Profit First forces profit through aggressive spending constraint, like a wedding diet — effective short-term, unsustainable and growth-limiting as a permanent operating system. It doesn't make the *model* profitable; it just rations cash.

### The pragmatic middle path (what Carl should usually recommend)

Three accounts, not five:
1. **Operating checking** — revenue in, expenses and owner's pay out
2. **Tax savings** — fixed % of every deposit (15% of real revenue is a sane default; adjust with the accountant)
3. **Profit/reserve savings** — builds the cash cushion, funds quarterly distributions

This keeps the load-bearing idea — "every dollar has a job," and taxes/profit are physically segregated so they can't be accidentally spent — without the ceremony. Graduate to full Profit First only if the client needs the behavioral guardrails.

## 4. Simple Numbers (Greg Crabtree) — the profitability operating system

Where Profit First is behavioral, Crabtree is analytical. For a scaling business, this is the more durable framework.

### The foundational move: owner's market-based wage

"Until you pay yourself a market-based wage and plug that number into your financials, your financial data is worthless." If the owner does a $120K/year job and takes $40K, the business is showing $80K of fake profit. Fix this first; every other number depends on it.

**For creator businesses this is acute:** the founder is often the talent, the salesperson, and the CEO. Price the roles at market (what would it cost to hire your replacement on camera + a GM?) before declaring the business profitable.

### Profit targets (pretax, after market-wage owner comp)

- **10%** = acceptable baseline
- **15%** = excellent
- **< 5%** = business on life support
- Once you hit 15%, it's rational to hire (spend back down toward 10%) to fund the next growth leg.

### Labor Efficiency Ratio (LER) and the salary cap

- **LER = gross profit ÷ total labor dollars.** The single best productivity metric for a service/people business. "The teams that win get the most productivity per dollar of labor."
- **Salary cap:** Revenue − non-salary costs − target profit = maximum total payroll. Example: $1M revenue, $400K non-salary costs, 10% profit target → $500K salary cap. Every hire is checked against the cap; this prevents "labor creep" — the slow accretion of headcount that quietly eats margin.

### The four forces of cash flow (the waterfall for profits)

Profits get consumed in this order — distributions come *last*:
1. **Taxes** (set aside quarterly, always)
2. **Debt repayment** (from after-tax profit)
3. **Core capital building** (until the cushion target is hit)
4. **Owner distributions** (only after 1–3 are satisfied)

### Core capital target

**Two months of operating expenses in cash, with zero draws on the line of credit.** That's the definition of a "fully capitalized" business in Crabtree's system. Until you're there, distributions beyond tax money are premature.

### Forecasts, not budgets

"A budget is a license to spend; a forecast is your road map to profitability." Allocate financial attention 75% forward-looking / 25% reviewing actuals. Update the forecast monthly (weekly for cash). The Springfield ReManufacturing example: factory workers updating forecasts weekly understood the financials better than most accountants.

### Reporting rhythm (the small-business scoreboard)

The more frequently you look at a report, the *less* data it should contain:

| Cadence | Report |
|---|---|
| Daily | Cash balance (deposits + current balance — one email/glance) |
| Weekly | 13-week (or at least 2-week) cash forecast; sales & labor-productivity snapshot |
| Monthly | Rolling-12 P&L (each column = trailing twelve months — filters out seasonality); balance sheet review to validate the P&L |
| Quarterly | Tax estimate true-up; profit distribution decision; forecast rebuild |

The rolling-twelve P&L deserves emphasis for seasonal businesses (a training facility with winter peaks; a channel with Q4 CPM spikes): month-over-month comparisons lie; trailing-twelve trends don't.

## 5. Margin management: benchmarks and levers

### Gross margin benchmarks (know the neighborhood)

Selected ranges from public-company data (private companies typically run **5–15 points lower** due to scale and pricing power):

| Business type | Gross margin |
|---|---|
| SaaS / software | 70–85% |
| Media / publishing / broadcast | 40–55% |
| Management consulting / creative services | 40–60% |
| Legal / professional services | 35–50% |
| E-commerce / specialty retail (merch!) | 30–50% |
| Healthcare & personal services | 30–45% |
| Sit-down food service | 30–35% |
| Staffing / low-differentiation services | 20–30% |

Service-business rules of thumb: **50–75% gross margin is healthy; under 40% signals pricing problems, scope creep, or an inefficient delivery model.** Agencies specifically: target 50–60%+ delivery margin on the P&L, 70%+ on individual projects.

### The agency 55/25/20 rule

A clean top-down structure for any people-heavy service business (applies well to a content studio or a coaching-heavy facility):
- **55%** of revenue → delivery labor (salaries + contractors)
- **25%** → overhead (rent, software, insurance, admin)
- **20%** → target net profit

Healthy net margins for services: **15–20% typical, 20–30% for high performers**; for small (<$5M) service businesses, 10–15% net is respectable. Utilization for billable teams: 65–80% sweet spot per person; junior staff 80–85%, senior leadership 50–60%.

### Warning thresholds

- **5+ points below** your sub-sector benchmark → systematic diagnosis, not tinkering.
- **Declining trend** vs. peers is a red flag even if you're still above median.

### Margin improvement playbook (sequenced)

**0–30 days:** model 3–5% price increases on the top 20% of customers/offerings; renegotiate the biggest supplier/vendor contracts; kill the bottom 10% of low-margin offerings; audit services being given away unintentionally (scope creep, free revisions, comped facility time).

**30–180 days:** replace resold/pass-through products with proprietary offerings (typically +2–5 points); institutionalize annual 3–5% price increases; tighten forecasting to cut waste/idle capacity.

**180+ days:** shift mix toward recurring revenue (memberships, retainers, newsletter subscriptions) and high-margin segments; consider vertical integration where you're paying someone else's margin.

**The compounding math:** on $10M revenue, 1 gross-margin point = $100K of gross profit with zero additional sales effort. On $1M, a 5-point improvement is $50K — often more than any single new revenue initiative will net.

## 6. Managing lumpy revenue (sponsorships, seasonality, project income)

### The sponsorship cash cycle — real numbers

From an agency dataset of ~3,700 creator brand-deal campaigns:
- **65% pay net-30, 25% net-60, 10% push net-90+**
- The clock usually starts at **invoice receipt after brand approval**, not at delivery. Approval can add 2+ weeks.
- Case study: an 80K-subscriber finance creator delivered a $6,000 integration in January; cash arrived in April — net-60 + two-week approval delay + a late invoice window = **11 weeks from delivery to deposit**.
- Running four deals a quarter on net-60 means permanently floating a quarter's worth of receivables.

### Playbook for sponsorship terms

1. **Ask for 50% upfront, balance net-30 after delivery.** Framing: "We work on 50% upfront with the balance net-30 after delivery — does that work on your end?" It's stated as standard process, not distrust. The majority of brands accept.
2. **If refused, counter with net-30 from delivery** instead of net-60 from approval — a month recovered without conflict.
3. **Deals over $10K: milestone structure** — 50/30/20 (upfront / at delivery / 30 days post-launch).
4. **Contract red flags:** "payment subject to brand approval" with no approval deadline; "net-60 from invoice receipt" where the invoicing window doesn't open until approval. Cap approval windows in the contract.
5. **Late fee clause:** 1.5%/month on overdue balances is standard, widely accepted, and moves your invoice up the AP queue.
6. **Negotiate terms on a call before the contract is drafted** — terms align verbally far more easily than over email.

### Cash reserve sizing

- **Baseline: 3–6 months of operating expenses.** Formula: monthly opex × target months. ($50K/month × 6 = $300K.)
- **Lumpy/seasonal revenue → the high end or beyond.** Seasonal businesses should compute the target from **peak-season monthly expenses**, not annual averages, and hold enough to cover the full off-season plus 2–3 months of peak expenses.
- **Adjusters:** recurring-revenue models need less; net-30/60 receivable books need more; high fixed costs (facility rent, salaried coaches) need more; an established, undrawn line of credit lets you run slightly leaner.
- **Where to park it:** 1–2 months in checking; 2–4 months in high-yield savings (4–5% APY as of 2025–26); anything beyond 6 months in short-term T-bills.
- **How to build it:** automate 5–10% of every deposit into reserves; sweep 50–75% of windfalls (a big sponsorship, tax refund) in. During peak season, earmark a fixed percentage of surplus above operating needs.

### The income-smoothing pattern for creator businesses

Treat high-revenue months as funding for low-revenue months, explicitly: a separate "smoothing fund" account that absorbs peaks and pays a level monthly "salary" to the operating account. Pair with a business line of credit ($25–250K range; typical requirements: 12–24 months operating history, $30K+ monthly deposits, 600+ FICO) as the backstop for payment-timing gaps — draw when a sponsor pays late, repay on receipt. The LOC covers *timing* risk; the reserve covers *level* risk. Never let the LOC quietly become permanent working capital (that violates Crabtree's core-capital definition).

### Concentration discipline

Track revenue concentration quarterly. Any single sponsor/client >30% of revenue is a cash flow risk, not just a strategy risk — their payment delay becomes your payroll crisis. Mitigations: stricter terms (upfront %) for concentrated payers, faster diversification, larger reserves while concentrated.

## 7. When to hire finance help: bookkeeper → controller → fractional CFO

### The three roles (they are not interchangeable)

- **Bookkeeper** — records transactions, reconciles bank/credit accounts, keeps the books accurate and current. Historical and tactical. Does not analyze, advise, or forecast.
- **Controller** — produces monthly financial statements, enforces GAAP-ish consistency, builds internal controls, ensures the numbers are *trustworthy and on time*. Reporting discipline.
- **Fractional CFO** — cash flow forecasting, scenario modeling, capital structure, pricing strategy, advising the owner on financial trade-offs. Forward-looking and strategic. Typical starting engagement: **10–15 hours/month**.

### Thresholds (heuristics, then the real trigger)

- **$0 → ~$1M:** outsourced bookkeeping from day one of real revenue. The owner should never be doing categorization by month three. Priority: accurate records, clean monthly close.
- **$1–3M:** outsourced bookkeeping still fine if operations are simple; add controller-level review if statements are late, inconsistent, or nobody trusts them.
- **$5–10M:** bookkeeping + controller services become essential — multiple revenue streams and a growing team make reliable statements and internal controls non-negotiable.
- **Fractional CFO:** the commonly cited threshold is ~$2M revenue (some say $500K–$1M, some say $5–10M) — but the honest answer from practitioners is that **complexity, not revenue, is the trigger**. Some $3M businesses desperately need CFO-level leadership; some $20M businesses don't yet.

### Complexity triggers that beat any revenue number

Hire fractional CFO help when any of these are true:
- Making a **major capital decision** (facility buildout, acquisition, big equipment purchase, real estate) — this is CFO work at any revenue level
- **Cash flow surprises despite profitability**
- Multiple entities or business lines with shared costs (e.g., a media company + a facility) that need clean allocation
- Preparing for financing, fundraising, or M&A
- Investor/lender pressure for better reporting
- Founder is the bottleneck on every financial decision and can't articulate the trade-offs

### Sequencing rule

**Never hire the strategy layer before the data layer works.** A fractional CFO on top of messy books produces expensive guesses. Order of operations: clean bookkeeping → reliable monthly close (controller function, even if outsourced/part-time) → CFO-level strategy. For a two-business setup (media co + facility), get the chart of accounts and entity/class structure right *before* the buildout starts — retrofitting cost allocation is miserable.

## 8. Capital for facility buildouts (loans, leases, equipment financing)

### Cost baselines (indoor sports/training facilities)

- Full indoor facility buildout: **$150–300/sq ft** including climate control, specialized flooring, electrical. A 15,000 sq ft facility = $2.25–4.5M total investment.
- Smaller footprint works: batting-cage/training franchise models run **3,000–5,000 sq ft** (min ~20 ft clear height) at roughly **$225K total investment**.
- Commercial gym equipment package: **$150–400K** (cardio $50–200K; strength $30–100K; specialty pieces $15–25K each).
- Industrial rent is the largest recurring cost: order-of-magnitude **$7–20/sq ft/year** for industrial space (market-dependent) — a 6,000 sq ft facility ≈ $45–120K/year.
- Facility economics reality check: typical profit margins **10–20%**; many facilities cluster around modest revenue ($100K–$1M+). The governing metric is **revenue per square foot** — un-programmed space is pure cost. Revenue mix that works: rentals + recurring memberships + lessons/camps/clinics + packages, weighted toward group/team bookings over one-off public rentals (larger group sizes per hour of space).

### Financing menu — match the product to the asset

| Product | Use for | Terms/rates (2026) | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Equipment financing** | Machines, cages, turf systems, tech | 8–16% APR, 36–72 months, 0–10% down (10–20% for thin credit files), closes in **3–10 business days** | Equipment is the collateral; no long operating history required. Usually the right *first* product. |
| **SBA 7(a)** | Tenant improvements/buildout on leased space, working capital, acquisitions, refinancing stacked equipment debt | Up to $5M; up to 10-yr terms (25 with real estate); rates roughly prime + spread (2026: ~10–14% fixed range; variable lower); **60–120 days to close** | The workhorse for leased-space buildouts. Small Loan variant ($50–350K) fits boutique buildouts. Start pre-qualification early — at LOI stage if acquiring. |
| **SBA 504** | *Buying or constructing* owner-occupied real estate + major long-life equipment | 50% bank first lien / 40% CDC debenture / **10% owner equity**; 20–25 yr real estate, 10 yr equipment; below-market fixed | Lowest fixed-rate SMB real-estate money available. Only relevant if buying the building. |
| **SBA Microloan** | Startup/solo scale | Up to $50K via intermediaries | For pre-revenue or tiny footprints. |
| **Business line of credit** | Seasonal smoothing, receivable timing | $25–250K revolving; needs 12–24 mo history, $30K+ monthly deposits, 600+ FICO | Working-capital shock absorber, not buildout money. |
| **Revenue-based financing / MCA** | Almost never | Factor rates 1.1–1.5× | **Danger:** stacking MCAs to bridge a seasonal dip can consume 30–40% of gross revenue. The most common over-leverage pattern in fitness. |

Rate environment context (mid-2026): Fed funds 3.5–3.75% after three 2025 cuts; prime ~6.75%; SBA 7(a) fixed roughly 9.75–14.75% depending on size/term; median new small-business term loans high-6s to low-7s%; big-bank approval rates only ~13–15% of applications (community banks and SBA lenders approve far more).

### Buildout financing sequence (leased space — the common case)

1. **Model the full project before the lease:** buildout cost/sq ft × footprint + equipment + 6+ months of operating losses during ramp. Underestimating the ramp is the classic failure.
2. **Negotiate tenant improvement (TI) allowance into the lease** — landlords in soft industrial markets will fund part of the buildout in exchange for term. Every TI dollar is a dollar you don't borrow.
3. **SBA 7(a) for the buildout + working capital cushion** in one facility (7(a) explicitly covers leasehold improvements and working capital). Underwriters will want the lease term to meet or exceed the loan term.
4. **Equipment financing separately** for the gear — faster, collateralized by the equipment, keeps 7(a) proceeds for improvements that have no collateral value.
5. **Open an LOC once you have 12+ months of deposits**, before you need it.
6. **Bank hygiene 6+ months before applying:** dedicated business operating account, no personal/business mixing, clean deposit history. Lenders in this space underwrite on member counts, churn, revenue per member/tier, and ACH deposit consistency — instrument those metrics from day one.

### Buildout capital mistakes (from lender post-mortems)

- **Over-buying equipment at launch** — carrying $4–5K/month of equipment debt before reaching capacity. Buy for month-6 utilization, not the dream layout; add via fast equipment financing as demand proves out.
- **Underestimating SBA timelines** (60–120 days) and losing deals or paying bridge rates.
- **Stacking MCAs** through the seasonal trough.
- **Not disclosing existing leases/advances** to a new lender — kills deals late.
- **Financing long-life assets with short money** (equipment on a credit card, buildout on an LOC) or vice versa. Match term to asset life, always.
- **No working-capital reserve in the loan sizing.** The buildout loan should include the ramp-period operating cushion; going back for more money six months in is far harder.

## 9. Budgeting & review rhythms (the operating cadence)

A synthesis of Crabtree's rhythm + TWCF practice — the minimum viable finance cadence for a scaling small business:

**Daily (30 seconds):** glance at cash balance(s). Anomalies surface immediately.

**Weekly (30–60 min):** update the 13-week cash forecast; review variances; check sales pipeline vs. forecast; check labor productivity if service-heavy. This is the meeting that prevents surprises.

**Twice monthly (10 min):** allocation sweep — tax % and profit/reserve % moved out of operating on the 10th and 25th (Profit First rhythm, even in the simplified 3-account version).

**Monthly (60–90 min, within 10 days of month-end):**
- Close the books (bookkeeper delivers; owner/CFO reviews)
- Rolling-12 P&L review — trends, not single months
- Balance sheet review (validates the P&L; catches miscodings, ballooning AR)
- Margin check vs. targets (gross margin, LER/salary cap for people businesses)
- AR aging review — who's late, who gets a call

**Quarterly (half day):**
- Tax estimate true-up and payment
- Profit distribution decision (only after taxes, debt, and core capital per the four-forces waterfall)
- Rolling forecast rebuild for the next 4–6 quarters (forecast, not budget: 75% of energy forward-looking)
- Pricing review (institutionalized 3–5% annual increases live here)
- Concentration and reserve-level check

**Annually:** insurance/vendor renegotiation pass; comp/salary-cap reset; capital plan for the coming year; entity/allocation structure review if multi-business.

## 10. Common mistakes (rapid-fire list Carl can scan against any client)

1. Confusing profit with cash — no receipts-based forecast.
2. Owner pays themselves scraps, calls the business profitable (Crabtree's cardinal sin).
3. No tax segregation → the April/June/Sept/Jan estimated-payment scramble.
4. Reserves sized off average months instead of peak-expense months (seasonal killer).
5. Accepting net-60/90 by default; never asking for upfront deposits.
6. One sponsor/client >30% of revenue with no term protection or reserve offset.
7. Line of credit silently converted into permanent working capital.
8. Hiring a fractional CFO on top of messy books (strategy before data).
9. Or the inverse: making a $500K+ capital decision (buildout) with no CFO-level modeling because "we're too small for a CFO."
10. Growth spending committed against forecasted revenue, not banked cash.
11. Labor creep — headcount added without checking the salary cap; profit erodes 1% per quarter and nobody notices until the rolling-12 shows it.
12. Financing mismatch: long assets on short money (buildout on credit cards) or MCAs bridging seasonal dips (30–40% of gross revenue consumed).
13. Buying launch equipment for fantasy utilization.
14. Overly complex financial models that die from maintenance burden — the tool you update weekly beats the tool you admire quarterly.
15. Reviewing month-vs-month in a seasonal business instead of rolling-12 trends.
16. Scope creep / comped services never audited — invisible margin leak.
17. Mixing entities' cash (media co paying facility bills ad hoc) — destroys unit visibility and creates tax and lending problems later.

## 11. Questions Carl should ask (diagnostic set)

**Cash visibility**
- "What will your bank balance be in week 9? How do you know?" (Tests for a real forecast.)
- "When were you last *surprised* by a cash position? What caused it?"
- "What's your minimum cash floor, and what happens at the floor — whose payment slips?"

**Profitability truth**
- "If you hired someone to do your job(s), what would it cost? Is that number in your P&L?"
- "What's your pretax margin *after* that market wage? Above or below 10%?"
- "What's your gross margin, and what's the benchmark for your kind of business? Are you within 5 points?"

**Lumpy revenue**
- "Walk me through your last three sponsor deals: delivery date vs. deposit date. What was the real lag?"
- "What percent of deals do you get paid anything upfront on?"
- "What single payer is your biggest? What percent of revenue? What happens to payroll if they pay 45 days late?"
- "How many months could you run at zero new revenue? Is that computed off peak-month expenses or average?"

**Structure & team**
- "Who closes your books, and by what day of the month? Do you trust the numbers?"
- "What financial decision are you facing in the next 12 months that's bigger than anything you've decided before?" (If yes → CFO-level support now.)
- "Are the media business and the facility separate entities with separate books? How do shared costs get split?"

**Capital & buildout**
- "What's the all-in project number — buildout + equipment + ramp-period losses? What's the working-capital cushion inside the financing?"
- "What TI allowance did/can you negotiate from the landlord?"
- "What's the utilization assumption at month 6, and what does the debt service look like if you hit half of it?"
- "Is every dollar of financing matched to the life of the asset it's buying?"

**Rhythm**
- "Show me your weekly finance ritual. If there isn't one, what would 45 minutes a week be worth to you?"
- "When did you last raise prices? What's stopping a 3–5% increase on your top offerings this quarter?"

## Relevance notes for small media companies & training facilities

- A YouTube-first media business is a **lumpy-receivables service business**: AdSense pays monthly and reliably (forecastable), sponsorships pay net-30/60 with approval lag (the volatility source), merch settles on platform schedules. The 13-week forecast + 50%-upfront negotiation + smoothing fund is the complete prescription.
- A training facility is the **opposite cash profile**: memberships prepay (cash-favorable) but fixed costs (rent, coaching payroll) are relentless and seasonal demand (off-season indoor training peaks in winter for baseball) makes rolling-12 reporting and peak-expense reserve sizing mandatory.
- Running both under one roof makes **entity separation, class-level bookkeeping, and a shared-cost allocation policy** the first finance project — before the buildout, not after.
- The facility buildout is precisely the "complexity trigger" that justifies fractional CFO hours regardless of current revenue; the sequencing is clean books → project model with ramp losses → TI negotiation → SBA 7(a) + separate equipment financing → LOC after 12 months of deposits.
