# Founder Leverage, Delegation & Time Allocation

## TL;DR

- **The founder's calendar is the company's real strategy.** Whatever the founder actually spends hours on IS the priority list, regardless of what the plan says. Audit it quarterly; the gap between stated priorities and calendar reality is the single fastest diagnostic Carl can run.
- **Delegation is measurably worth money.** Gallup: Inc. 500 CEOs with high "Delegator talent" posted 112 percentage points higher 3-year growth and 33% more revenue ($8M vs $6M) than low-delegator peers — and only ~1 in 4 employer entrepreneurs has high delegator talent. Assume the client doesn't, and build systems that compensate.
- **Use the Buyback Rate as the delegation trigger:** annual income ÷ 2,000 ÷ 4. A founder taking $150k should delegate anything they could buy for ≤ ~$19/hr — which is nearly all admin, scheduling, inbox, basic editing, and errands. If they're still doing $10/hr work, that's the first fix, always.
- **Delegate outcomes with an explicit authority level, not tasks with vibes.** Most delegation "failures" are level mismatches (Hyatt's 5 levels): the founder thought they delegated Level 2 (research and report), the person heard Level 4 (decide and inform). Name the level out loud every time.
- **Keep only what's genuinely founder-only:** vision, values, strategy, key hires, capital allocation, and irreversible (one-way-door) decisions. Everything reversible, repeatable, or weekly should have a named owner and a written threshold (spend limit, refund limit, pricing band).
- **Follow the Replacement Ladder order:** admin assistant → delivery → marketing → sales → leadership. Founders almost always skip rung 1 and try to hire rung 5. The first $1,500–$3,000/mo assistant is the highest-ROI hire most sub-$2M businesses will ever make.
- **Energy is a filter, not a nice-to-have.** The DRIP matrix scores every task on two axes — makes money / drains or gives energy. Tasks that are low-value AND draining go first; tasks that are high-value AND energizing (Production quadrant / "zone of genius") are what the bought-back time is FOR.
- **Founder Mode (Graham/Chesky, 2024) is a correction, not a repeal.** Stay in the details of the product and quality bar ("eyes on"), while still moving execution and reversible decisions off your plate ("hands off"). The failure modes are symmetric: abdication and micromanagement both kill scale.

## 1. Why this is the constraint

A founder-led company scales exactly as fast as the founder's judgment can be either (a) applied or (b) transferred. Early on, applying it directly is the fastest path. Past a certain point — roughly when the team can't get decisions out of the founder faster than the business generates them — the founder becomes the CPU every request routes through, and throughput flatlines.

The data on how bad the default state is:

- Small business owners average **~52 hours/week**, with **20% working 60+**; 91% work more than 40 (multiple surveys via Time etc / delegation research).
- Entrepreneurs spend on average **~36% of the work week on admin** — invoicing, data entry, scheduling, supplies (Time etc, "The Big Price of Small Tasks").
- A Clutch survey: **68% of small business owners still do their own customer service, 61% do their own marketing.**
- **25% of entrepreneurs say "faster to do it myself"** even for simple repetitive tasks — the classic trap, because it compares one instance of the task against the training cost, instead of a year of instances.
- Gallup (Inc. 500 + 1,446 employer entrepreneurs): only **~1 in 4** founders has high Delegator talent; those who do grow dramatically faster (1,751% vs ~1,639% 3-year growth in the Inc. 500 sample; $8M vs $6M revenue; 21 vs 17 jobs created).

Even elite large-company CEOs are stretched: the Harvard Business School Porter/Nohria study (60,000 tracked hours across 27 CEOs, 13 weeks each, 24/7) found CEOs work **62.5 hrs/week**, do business on **79% of weekend days** and **70% of vacation days**, spend **~36% of time in reactive mode** handling unfolding issues, **~11% on routine duties** (reviews, board, earnings-type obligations), only **~3% with customers**, and **~25% on people and relationships**. The study's core prescription: CEOs must be ruthless agenda-driven (working from an explicit personal agenda rather than reacting), and must delegate everything that doesn't require them — because literally everything in the company would consume their time if allowed.

Carl's framing: **the founder's job is not to do work; it's to allocate the scarcest resource in the company — founder attention — like capital.** Every hour has an implicit price, and most founders are systematically paying $200/hr attention for $15/hr output.

## 2. The dollar-per-hour ladder (Perry Marshall, 80/20 Sales and Marketing)

Marshall applies Pareto to time: in any founder's week, a tiny fraction of hours produce almost all the value. He buckets tasks into four tiers. The tiers are illustrative, not literal wages — the point is the ~10x gap between adjacent tiers.

| Tier | Example tasks | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| **$10/hr** | Errands, cold-calling unqualified lists, expense reports, fixing website widgets, spelling/formatting passes, sorting files, driving to meetings, routine inbox, basic customer service | Eliminate or delegate immediately. These *feel* productive and are the default gravity of every founder's day. |
| **$100/hr** | Talking to qualified prospects, solving a problem for a real customer, writing emails to the list, running marketing tests, doing social media *well*, customer follow-up, outsourcing the $10 tasks | Do only until you can hand to a competent hire; systematize with checklists. |
| **$1,000/hr** | Reviewing marketing test results, building the sales funnel, writing sales copy/offers, refining positioning/USP, negotiating with top customers or sponsors, designing systems and processes, delegating complex work properly | This is where the founder should live most of the week. |
| **$10,000/hr** | The "1% of the 1%": big strategic bets, key hires, major partnerships, public speaking / flagship content, pricing and business-model decisions, the creative decisions only the founder can make | Rare hours; protect them fanatically. Often disguised as "thinking time" and therefore the first thing sacrificed. |

Two consequences Marshall draws:

1. **The raise math**: a founder who purges $10 work and fills a full year with $100 work alone is running a ~$200k/year engine (40 hrs × 50 wks × $100). Each tier jump is roughly a 10x raise achieved purely by reallocation — no new revenue skills required.
2. **Focused hours are few.** Everyone gets only a handful of genuinely focused hours a day; the tier of work occupying those hours determines the trajectory. A $10k/hr decision made tired at 6pm after a day of $10 tasks is made badly.

Carl's use: run a client's last week through this table and price every block. Founders are consistently shocked at their blended hourly rate. For a creator-founder (Mayday-type business), the $10k tier is usually: what to make, who to hire, sponsor/partner strategy, and the on-camera work only they can do. Everything in the edit/upload/schedule/inbox pipeline is $10–$100 work.

## 3. Buy Back Your Time (Dan Martell) — the operating system

Martell's book is the most complete practitioner system for this problem. Core thesis (the **Buyback Principle**): **"Don't hire to grow your business. Hire to buy back your time."** Growth follows automatically when the founder's reclaimed hours go into high-leverage work; hiring "for growth" without buying back time just adds management load to an already-saturated founder.

### 3.1 Buyback Rate (BBR) — the delegation trigger

Formula: **annual income ÷ 2,000 working hours ÷ 4 = Buyback Rate.**

- Example from the book: $400k income → $200/hr effective rate → **BBR = $50/hr**. Any task you could pay someone ≤$50/hr to do, you delegate.
- The ÷4 builds in margin: even paying a quarter of your rate, you profit on the swap as long as the freed hour goes into higher-tier work.
- Practical anchors: a $100k founder has a BBR of **$12.50/hr** — VA territory. A $300k founder: **$37.50/hr** — covers most editors, bookkeepers, coordinators, and junior ops hires.
- Recompute annually; the BBR rises with income, unlocking new layers of delegation each year.

### 3.2 The audit: time AND energy

Track 1–2 typical weeks in 15-minute increments. Score every task on two dimensions:

1. **$ value** (map to the Marshall tiers or simply low/high relative to BBR)
2. **Energy** — did it drain you or light you up?

The energy axis is what most time-management systems miss: a founder can theoretically "afford" to keep a high-value task that drains them, but drained founders make bad $10k/hr decisions and eventually burn out. (Creator context: 63–90% of creators report burnout in recent surveys — Vibely 2025 and prior; energy management is not soft for this population, it's survival.)

### 3.3 DRIP Matrix — four quadrants from the two axes

| | **Drains energy** | **Gives energy** |
|---|---|---|
| **Low $** | **Delegation quadrant** — admin, invoicing, inbox, scheduling. Remove first. | **Investment quadrant** — health, relationships, learning, industry peers, hobbies. Not directly measurable but compounds; do not delete. |
| **High $** | **Replacement quadrant** — sales, marketing, team management that pays but drains. Replace via key hires over time. | **Production quadrant** — the founder's genius work. The whole point. Maximize hours here. |

The **Buyback Loop** is the repeat cycle: **Audit → Transfer → Fill.** Audit the calendar, transfer the lowest-value/most-draining tasks to someone who's good at (and often enjoys) them, and deliberately fill the freed time with Production-quadrant work — if you don't consciously fill it, low-value work refills it automatically.

### 3.4 The Replacement Ladder — hiring order

Five rungs, in order. Each rung transfers a category of the founder's time; skipping rungs is the classic error.

1. **Administrative assistant** — owns calendar, inbox, scheduling, travel, follow-ups. The first hire, almost always. Rule of thumb from Martell's world: if you can't fill an assistant's week, you haven't audited honestly.
2. **Delivery** — someone owns fulfilling the product/service (for a media company: editing, publishing pipeline, community/client support; for a training facility: coaching delivery, session scheduling, floor operations).
3. **Marketing** — owns campaigns, content distribution, funnel.
4. **Sales** — owns pipeline and closing (for creator businesses: sponsorship sales/partnerships).
5. **Leadership** — managers/executives who own outcomes and other people; the founder moves to vision, standards, and capital allocation.

### 3.5 Transfer mechanics — how to hand work off without it boomeranging

- **The Camcorder Method / 4Cs playbooks**: record yourself doing the task (screen-record or literally film it), then have the *hire* turn it into the playbook: **Camcorder** (capture), **Course** (step-by-step doc), **Cadence** (when/how often), **Checklist** (quality gates). The founder should not write SOPs from scratch — that's another $10/hr trap.
- **The 80% rule**: accept that a hire doing the task 80% as well as you is a win, because your marginal 20% costs you $1,000/hr time. Perfectionism is the most expensive habit in the building.
- **10-80-10** (also in Unicorn Labs' bottleneck material): founder does the **first 10%** (define the outcome, constraints, quality bar, authority level) and the **last 10%** (review, coach, calibrate — *without redoing the work*). The middle 80% belongs entirely to the owner, including doing it differently than you would.
- **1:3:1 rule for upward problems**: anyone bringing the founder a problem must bring **1** clearly defined problem, **3** viable options, and **1** recommendation. Kills the "monkey transfer" where every conversation deposits a task on the founder's back, and trains judgment in the team.
- **Perfect Week design**: after buyback, design the calendar proactively — batch maker blocks, meeting days, energy-matched scheduling (hard creative work when fresh) — rather than letting the freed time be colonized by requests.

### 3.6 The Pain Line

Martell's observation: most founders grow until the pain of the workload hits a ceiling (the "Pain Line"), then unconsciously respond one of three ways — **sabotage** growth (turn down opportunities), **stall** (plateau and cope), or **sell** (exit a business that had more room to run). The buyback system is the fourth option. Carl should name this pattern when a client starts saying "I don't want it to get bigger" — sometimes that's genuine preference, but often it's an un-diagnosed delegation failure talking.

## 4. Delegation levels and calibration

### 4.1 Michael Hyatt's Five Levels of Delegation (Free to Focus)

Every delegated item should carry an explicit level. The levels define how much decision authority travels with the task:

1. **Level 1 — Do exactly as I say.** No deviation; founder has already decided. (New hires, high-stakes/compliance work.)
2. **Level 2 — Research and report.** Gather info, present findings; founder decides and instructs.
3. **Level 3 — Research and recommend.** Options with pros/cons plus a recommendation; founder approves before action. (Pairs perfectly with the 1:3:1 rule.)
4. **Level 4 — Decide and inform.** Full ownership of decision and execution; keep the founder in the loop.
5. **Level 5 — Act independently.** Full autonomy, no report-back needed.

Hyatt's core warning: **most delegation blowups are level mismatches, not competence failures.** His canonical example: a leader delegated event planning intending Level 2; the team member assumed Level 4 and committed to decisions; both ended up frustrated and trust was damaged. People are not mind readers — the fix is a shared vocabulary, stated at handoff: "This is a Level 3 — bring me options and a recommendation."

### 4.2 Andy Grove — Task-Relevant Maturity (High Output Management)

Grove's calibration rule: choose management style and monitoring frequency by the person's **experience with this specific task**, not their general seniority or talent.

- **Low TRM** → structured, precise instructions (what, when, how); frequent check-ins. This is not micromanagement — it's the correct style for the situation.
- **Medium TRM** → two-way communication, support, coaching.
- **High TRM** → minimal involvement; agree on objectives and monitor loosely.
- As TRM rises, deliberately lower the monitoring cadence. And remember Grove's non-negotiable: **delegation without monitoring is abdication** — the delegator remains responsible for completion, and monitoring is the only practical way to carry that responsibility.
- Corollary: there is greater leverage in delegating tasks you know well (you can monitor them cheaply and coach effectively) than tasks you don't understand — the opposite of the instinct to offload the stuff you're bad at first.

Mapping: TRM tells you *which Hyatt level to start at* and *how fast to promote through levels*. A brilliant senior hire is Level 2–3 on a task new to them; a junior person can be Level 4–5 on a task they've run fifty times.

## 5. Decision rights — what only the founder decides

The mature form of the bottleneck problem isn't too much *work* on the founder's plate — it's too many *decisions* routing through them. As headcount grows and authority stays undefined, decisions drift upward until the founder is the default approval layer (Unicorn Labs).

### Keep (founder-only)

- **Vision and values** — what the company is and refuses to be
- **Strategy and positioning** — where to play, market direction, innovation bets
- **Key hires** (and fires) — especially leadership and culture-defining roles
- **Capital allocation** — major spend, new ventures, financing
- **Irreversible, one-way-door decisions** (Bezos, 1997 shareholder letter): Type 1 decisions you can't back out of get made "methodically, carefully, slowly, with great deliberation." Everything else is a **Type 2 two-way door** — reversible — and "can and should be made quickly by high-judgment individuals or small groups." Bezos's warning: growing orgs default to heavyweight Type-1 process for everything, producing "slowness, unthoughtful risk aversion, failure to experiment sufficiently, and consequently diminished invention."
- **The quality bar / taste** — in a creator business, the founder's taste often IS the product; see §7 on Founder Mode.

### Delegate (with written thresholds)

- Anything **repeatable, low-risk, reversible, or recurring weekly**
- Decisions **closest to the customer/product details**
- Create **safe-to-fail thresholds**: spend limits (e.g., "anything under $500, just decide"), refund limits, discount/pricing bands, hiring authority below a level, content decisions within an agreed format
- **Document decision rights**: who decides what, to what dollar amount, with what consultation. Without documentation, "ask the founder" is the default for everything. For each consequential decision type, name ONE person who gathers input, frames trade-offs, decides within a time window, and records the reasoning.

### The Founder Operating System (structural replacement for founder presence)

Unicorn Labs' four components, useful as a checklist even for a 5–15 person team:

1. **Decision-rights documentation** (the authority matrix above)
2. **Meeting cadence** — weekly syncs, monthly business review, quarterly planning; each meeting owned by someone who is *not* the founder, with defined outputs. Structured rhythm replaces ad-hoc founder access.
3. **Information architecture** — dashboards and shared docs so context lives outside the founder's head (this is exactly what an internal ops app is for)
4. **Coaching rhythms** — replace approval chains with decision-quality coaching (review *how* the call was made, not just what was decided)

Diagnostics: the **bus-factor test** — if the founder vanished for a week, how long does normal operation continue? Under a week = single point of failure. Other symptoms: team brings problems not proposals; decisions stall during founder vacations; senior hires' work gets redone by the founder; founder required in every meeting "for context." Typical danger zone is the 20–100 employee range, with the founder→decision-architect transition needed around 20–50 — but in tiny creator teams the same dynamics show up at 3–8 people because the founder is also the talent.

**Chief of Staff vs COO** (relevant when a client wants to "hire someone to run things"): a Chief of Staff multiplies the CEO's time and acts on their behalf (fits ~15–60 people); a COO owns operations and makes independent decisions (fits 50+). Wrong-stage COO hires become a blocking layer — and a founder who overrides the COO creates a two-person bottleneck worse than the original. Most small companies asking for a COO actually need rung 1–2 of the Replacement Ladder plus documented decision rights.

Realistic change timeline: **3–6 months** for measurable de-bottlenecking; structural pieces (decision rights, cadence) land in weeks 1–4, behavioral pieces (not overriding, tolerating different execution styles) take longer, and at least one founder backslide is normal — tell clients to expect it so it doesn't discredit the whole effort.

## 6. The calendar audit — Carl's standard playbook

Run this with any client complaining of overwhelm, stalled growth, or "no time to think":

1. **Capture** 10 working days in 15–30 min blocks (calendar export + honest annotation; or the client texts a log at lunch and end-of-day). No behavior change during capture.
2. **Price every block** with the Marshall tiers ($10/$100/$1k/$10k) and mark **energy** (+/−) per the DRIP axes.
3. **Compute**: % of hours per tier; blended hourly rate vs their BBR; % reactive vs agenda-driven (HBS benchmark: even elite CEOs run ~36% reactive — a founder above ~50% has no strategy, only a queue); % maker time in blocks ≥2 hours.
4. **Compare against stated priorities.** Ask "what are your top 3 priorities?" *before* revealing the data, then show the calendar. The gap is the conversation.
5. **Cut list**: every $10 block → eliminate, automate, or delegate within 30 days (this alone usually recovers 10–15 hrs/week given the ~36%-admin baseline).
6. **Assign authority levels** (Hyatt) to every recurring delegated item; write the 3–5 decision thresholds that remove the founder from weekly approvals.
7. **Rebuild the week** (Perfect Week): maker blocks first (see below), then meeting clusters, then buffer for the irreducible reactive load. Protect at least one half-day/week of $10k-tier thinking time.
8. **Re-audit in 90 days.** Calendars regress; the audit is a quarterly hygiene ritual, not a one-time fix.

### Maker's schedule vs manager's schedule (Paul Graham)

Founders who both create and manage (every creator-founder, and any founder building product) run two incompatible schedules:

- **Manager's schedule**: the day in 1-hour slots; meetings are cheap to add.
- **Maker's schedule**: half-day or full-day units; a single mid-afternoon meeting "can blow a whole afternoon, by breaking it into two pieces each too small to do anything hard in." Meetings are "like throwing an exception" — they change the working mode, and even the *anticipation* of one suppresses starting ambitious work.
- Fixes: cluster meetings at day edges or on designated manager-days (Y Combinator ran office hours at the end of the day for this reason); Graham himself partitioned into day-mode (business) and night-mode (programming). For a creator: film/write/edit days must be meeting-free by policy, not by luck.

## 7. Energy management and the zone of genius

Gay Hendricks (The Big Leap) — four zones, from the founder's perspective:

1. **Zone of Incompetence** — things others do better; delegate without ceremony.
2. **Zone of Competence** — you're fine at it, so is everyone else; delegate.
3. **Zone of Excellence** — you're *very* good at it and it pays — the dangerous zone, because comfort and competence keep founders here (e.g., the creator who is an excellent editor and therefore still edits). Excellence work is exactly Martell's Replacement quadrant.
4. **Zone of Genius** — the work only you can do at your level, that energizes rather than drains, where effort feels disproportionately productive. Maps to Martell's Production quadrant and Marshall's $10k tier.

Operating rules Carl applies:

- **Energy audit alongside time audit** — productivity science over-indexes on time and under-indexes on energy; drained hours are low-quality hours regardless of the task tier.
- **Match task type to energy curve**: creative/strategic work in the founder's peak window (usually mornings), calls and management in the trough.
- **Protect the Investment quadrant** — health, relationships, learning, peers. It's low-$ and thus first cut under pressure, but it's the input to sustained Production capacity. For creators specifically, this is the burnout firewall (63% of US creators reporting burnout symptoms in 2025 data; ~90% having experienced it and 71% having considered quitting in earlier surveys).
- **The excellence trap is the usual client blocker**: when a founder resists delegating something, ask whether it's genius (keep) or merely excellence (replace). The tell: genius work energizes; excellence work quietly drains even when it goes well.

## 8. Founder Mode — the 2024–2026 correction

Paul Graham's September 2024 essay "Founder Mode" (sparked by Brian Chesky's talk) pushed back on the standard scale-up advice — "hire good people and give them room to do their jobs":

- Graham's claim: that advice describes **manager mode** — running divisions as black boxes via direct reports — and it's how you manage a company you *didn't* build. Following it took Airbnb to "disastrous results": silos, bottlenecks, and a middle-management power-grab culture. Chesky recovered by studying Steve Jobs and re-engaging directly ("I basically got involved in every single detail"), and Airbnb subsequently posted among the best free-cash-flow margins in Silicon Valley.
- **Founder mode** features: skip-level engagement (working beyond the org chart — Jobs's annual retreat for Apple's 100 most important people regardless of title), direct knowledge of operations, the founder as guardian of the quality bar. As of 2025 Chesky still personally manages hire/fire/promote decisions for ~50 people.
- Graham's own caveats: founder mode is messier and under-theorized, and he predicted the exact misuses that followed — **founders using it as cover to avoid legitimate delegation**, and professional managers cosplaying it.

**How Carl reconciles this with everything above** (they are not in conflict):

- Founder mode is about **which things** the founder stays deep in — product, quality, standards, the customer experience — not about doing all the work or approving all the decisions. Chesky is in the details of *the product*; he is not doing Airbnb's expense reports.
- The synthesis is **"eyes on, hands off"**: stay in the details via review, skip-levels, and taste enforcement (the last 10% of the 10-80-10), while execution ownership and reversible decisions live with the team.
- For a creator business the resolution is natural: **the founder's taste and voice are the product** — that's founder-mode territory forever. The pipeline around it (editing to an established style, scheduling, ops, admin, bookkeeping) is manager-mode/delegation territory immediately.
- Red flag Carl watches for: a client quoting "founder mode" while drowning in $10/hr tasks. Founder mode without buyback is just burnout with a blog post to justify it.

## 10. Playbooks

### 30-day buyback sprint (first engagement with an overwhelmed founder)

- **Week 1**: time + energy capture (no changes). Compute BBR.
- **Week 2**: price and score every block (tier + DRIP quadrant). Build the cut list. Pick the top 5 recurring Delegation-quadrant items.
- **Week 3**: camcorder the top 5 (record while doing them one last time). Post/assign the role — usually a part-time assistant or VA at or below BBR. Draft 3 decision thresholds that end weekly founder approvals.
- **Week 4**: hand off with explicit Hyatt levels; hire drafts the 4Cs playbooks from the recordings; founder installs 1:3:1 as the team-wide rule for bringing problems. Book the 90-day re-audit.

### Delegation handoff checklist (per item)

1. Outcome defined (what done looks like, measurably)
2. Quality bar shown, not described (examples of good/bad)
3. Authority level named (Hyatt 1–5) and matched to TRM
4. Thresholds set (spend/scope limits where they decide alone)
5. Monitoring cadence agreed (frequent at low TRM, scheduled to decrease)
6. Playbook exists or is being written by the new owner (4Cs)
7. Founder commitment stated: no reclaiming the work on the first miss — coach the last 10%, don't redo the 80%

### Anti-boomerang rules (why delegation reverts, and the counter)

- First failure → founder takes it back. Counter: pre-commit to a coaching response; failure at low TRM is the tuition.
- "Handle it but check with me first." That's not delegation; it's tasking with veto. Counter: pick a real Hyatt level.
- Undefined "good." Counter: examples and checklists before handoff, not feedback after.
- Freed time silently refills with new low-value work. Counter: Fill step of the Buyback Loop — pre-schedule what the reclaimed hours are *for*.

### Applied notes (small media company / training facility)

- **Creator-founder**: on-camera presence, creative direction, thumbnail/title taste, and sponsor relationships are Production/genius work. Editing, clipping, upload/scheduling, community management, invoicing, and analytics reporting are rungs 1–2 of the Replacement Ladder. Use the ≥3-of-5 editor-hiring signals above; a retention-focused freelance editor at $40–150/video is usually the first delivery hire.
- **Training facility**: the founder's genius is program design, coaching methodology, and reputation-building; floor coaching beyond a signature amount, scheduling, memberships/billing, and facility ops are delivery/admin rungs. A facility that can't run a normal week without the founder on-site fails the bus-factor test — and unlike media, the facility has fixed hours, so founder-as-bottleneck caps revenue directly.
- **Internal tooling** (an ops app) is the "information architecture" leg of the Founder Operating System — dashboards, task systems, and playbooks that move context out of the founder's head. Caution: building internal tools is often Zone-of-Excellence work masquerading as leverage; audit the founder's hours spent building tools vs. the hours the tools actually save.

## 11. Common mistakes

1. **Hiring for growth instead of buyback** — adding salespeople/marketers while the founder still does their own inbox; the new hires just generate more decisions routed through a saturated founder.
2. **Skipping the assistant rung** — trying to hire a COO/GM (rung 5) before rung 1. Produces either an expensive assistant or a blocking layer.
3. **Delegating tasks but not authority** — everything still needs approval; you've hired hands, not owners. Diagnose by counting approvals per week.
4. **Level mismatch at handoff** — no shared vocabulary for how much autonomy travels with the work (the Hyatt failure mode).
5. **Uniform management style** — treating a senior hire as high-TRM on tasks new to them (abdication), or an experienced person as low-TRM (micromanagement). Style follows task experience, not title.
6. **The "faster to do it myself" fallacy** — comparing one instance against training cost instead of 52 instances/year against one training cost.
7. **Perfectionism pricing** — rejecting 80%-as-good output while spending $1,000/hr attention to capture the last 20%.
8. **Reclaiming work on first failure** — guarantees the team never develops capability and teaches everyone to route everything back to the founder.
9. **Delegating only the stuff you hate and don't understand** — you can't monitor or coach it (Grove: leverage is higher delegating what you know).
10. **Treating every decision as a one-way door** — heavyweight process on reversible calls; the Bezos failure mode of scale.
11. **Founder-mode cosplay** — citing Chesky/Graham to justify staying in every detail, including the $10/hr ones.
12. **No fill plan** — buying back 15 hours and letting Slack and email eat them. The audit without the Fill step just funds more reactive time.
13. **Writing SOPs personally** — documenting is itself delegable (camcorder + hire writes the playbook).
14. **Calendar as archaeology, not architecture** — never re-auditing; assuming January's fixed calendar still matches July's priorities.

## 12. Questions Carl should ask

**Diagnosis**
- "Walk me through last Tuesday, hour by hour. What did each block cost at your effective hourly rate?"
- "What's your annual income divided by 2,000, divided by 4? What are you currently doing that you could buy for less than that?"
- "If you disappeared for two weeks with no phone, what breaks on day 1? Day 3? Day 10?" (bus factor)
- "How many things did people ask your permission for this week? How many actually needed you?"
- "What are your top three priorities?" — then — "show me where they appear on your calendar."
- "What % of your week is reacting vs. working an agenda you set?"

**Energy / genius**
- "Which tasks last week gave you energy? Which drained you even though they went fine?"
- "What are you very good at that someone else should still be doing?" (excellence trap)
- "When did you last have a half-day of uninterrupted thinking or making time?"

**Delegation quality**
- "When you last handed something off, did you say how much authority came with it — or did they have to guess?"
- "What happened the last time a delegated task came back wrong? Did you coach it or take it back?"
- "Who on your team can spend money without asking you? Up to how much?"
- "Do problems come to you with options and a recommendation, or just as problems?"

**Structure**
- "Which recurring meetings would run identically without you in the room?"
- "Where does company context live besides your head?"
- "What's the next rung on your Replacement Ladder — and are you trying to skip it?"

**Founder mode calibration**
- "Which details are you in because the product needs your taste — and which are you in because letting go feels risky?"
- "If you stayed deep in only ONE part of the business, which part would it be? What does that imply about the rest?"
