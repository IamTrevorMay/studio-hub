# SOPs, Systems & Process Documentation

## TL;DR

- **Document the revenue path first.** Start with the 10–15 systems in the "Critical Client Flow" (how a client is attracted, sold, delivered, invoiced) — not the whole business. Everything else waits.
- **The owner should almost never write the SOPs.** Assign a "Systems Champion" who *extracts* knowledge from the people doing the work (screen recordings, shadowing), rather than the founder writing idealized versions from memory.
- **The format that gets used is layered:** one 2–7 minute Loom video + a 5–12 item checklist + screenshots for the fragile steps. Text-only manuals die; 30-minute videos die; the hybrid survives.
- **Checklists ≠ instructions.** Per Gawande: 5–9 "killer items" only, under 60–90 seconds to run, explicit pause point, tested in the field. A checklist is a verification layer on top of competence, not a substitute for training.
- **Every SOP needs one named owner and a review date.** "The company" cannot own a document. Quarterly review for software-dependent processes, annual for stable ones, immediate on tool change or incident. No owner = guaranteed rot within a quarter.
- **98% is perfection.** (Sam Carpenter) Ship the imperfect procedure now; the last 2% costs more than it returns. An SOP improves through use, not through polishing before release.
- **Document first, automate second, never automate a broken process.** Manual + documented → stable + measured → then automate. Roughly 40% of automation projects miss ROI expectations, mostly from automating chaos.
- **The payoff is real and measurable:** businesses that run without owner dependency sell for ~71% more (Value Builder, n>52,000); ~80% of processes in typical orgs are undocumented; 40–60% of onboarding time is spent absorbing tribal knowledge that was never written down.

## 1. Why systemize at all — the economic case

Numbers Carl can quote:

- **~80% of processes in most organizations are undocumented** (Tallyfy / process-industry estimates). The business "works" because specific humans remember things.
- **40–60% of onboarding time** is new hires acquiring undocumented tribal context that already exists in someone's head. Typical time-to-full-productivity for a skilled hire: 3–6 months; documentation compresses this materially.
- **Replacing a mid-level employee costs 6–9 months of salary** (SHRM); for senior specialists 200%+. In a 3–20 person team, one key departure with undocumented knowledge is a $30k+ event before you count the process breakage.
- **47% of organizations name key-person dependency as a significant operational risk** (Deloitte Human Capital Trends 2023, up from 34% in 2020).
- **Exit value:** in the Value Builder System dataset (52,000+ businesses), companies that could run without the owner received offers **~71% higher** than average. Systemization is literally an enterprise-value multiplier, not just an ops nicety.
- One well-known micro-case: Sam Carpenter's first written procedure at Centratel (the deposit procedure) saved ~2 hrs/week — over 21 years, roughly one full year of 40-hour workweeks from a single document.

The founder-level framing: an undocumented business is a **job with employees**. A documented one is an **asset**. For a creator business specifically, this is the difference between "the channel dies if the founder takes a month off" and "the machine publishes while the founder does only the irreplaceable parts (on-camera, taste, relationships)."

## 2. The canonical frameworks (know these cold)

### 2.1 The E-Myth Revisited — Michael Gerber (franchise prototype)

The foundational text. Core ideas:

- **The Fatal Assumption:** "If you understand the technical work of a business, you understand a business that does technical work." False. Great editors build editing jobs, not media companies; great coaches build coaching jobs, not training facilities.
- **Three personas** in every owner: Entrepreneur (vision), Manager (order), Technician (doing). Typical small-business owner is ~10% E / 20% M / 70% T — that ratio is why they're trapped.
- **Business lifecycle:** Infancy (owner = business) → Adolescence (hiring by *abdication*, not delegation; quality drops because "nobody cares like I do") → either retreat back to small, or build to Maturity (business designed to work *without* you).
- **The Franchise Prototype:** "Pretend your business is the prototype for 5,000 more just like it." You never franchise, but you build to that standard. "The true product of a business is the business itself" — McDonald's product isn't the hamburger, it's McDonald's.
- **Six operating standards of the prototype:** (1) consistent value beyond expectation, (2) operable by people with the *lowest necessary skill level*, (3) impeccable order, (4) all work documented in operations manuals, (5) uniformly predictable service, (6) uniform presentation/brand codes. Standard #2 is the one owners resist — "we only hire rockstars" is an anti-system.
- **Business Development cycle:** **Innovation** (find the best way) → **Quantification** (measure its impact in numbers) → **Orchestration** (remove discretion at the operating level so the best way happens every time). "If you haven't orchestrated it, you don't own it."
- **The org-chart exercise:** draw the chart for the *finished* company (positions, not people), write a Position Contract (accountabilities + standards) for each seat, put your own name in most boxes today, then systematically replace yourself box-by-box — starting at the bottom (most tactical work first). Each position's operations manual is written *before* the hire.
- **Systems Strategy:** hard systems (physical/tools), soft systems (processes, scripts, how people interact), information systems (data that tells you whether the other two work).

Gerber's most quotable line for clients: *"Great businesses are built by ordinary people doing extraordinary things — and for ordinary people to do extraordinary things, a system is absolutely essential."*

### 2.2 Work the System — Sam Carpenter

The blue-collar, operational counterpart to Gerber. Carpenter ran Centratel (telephone answering service) at 100-hour weeks for 15 years, then systemized down to ~2-hour weeks with ~20x monthly income.

- **Systems mindset:** a business is not one blob of chaos; it's a collection of discrete, separable systems, each of which can be isolated, examined, and fixed permanently. Firefighting is treating symptoms of unexamined systems.
- **Three-tier documentation stack** (this is the minimal viable "constitution" for a small company):
  1. **Strategic Objective** — one page: what the business is, for whom, and the goals. Written once, rarely edited.
  2. **General Operating Principles** — ~30 short rules for making decisions when no procedure exists ("we do X, we never do Y"). This is the layer most companies skip, and it's what stops the SOP library from being brittle.
  3. **Working Procedures** — the SOPs themselves, one per recurring process, simple enough for a new hire to follow.
- **The 98% rule:** 98% accuracy is perfection; the last 2% has brutal diminishing returns. Corollary for documentation: publish rough procedures immediately and improve in place.
- **Execution discipline:** staff follow the procedure *exactly as written* — and anyone can propose a change at any time. This combination (compliance + open amendment channel) is what keeps procedures both authoritative and current. If the doc is wrong, fix the doc; don't silently deviate.
- **Cadence:** 1–2 hours per day working on procedures at the start; the time savings compound and fund further systemization.

### 2.3 The Checklist Manifesto — Atul Gawande (checklist *design*)

The other two books say "write it down"; Gawande tells you how to make the artifact actually work under pressure.

- **Why failures happen:** volume and complexity of what we know exceeds any individual's ability to deliver it reliably. Checklists protect against *ineptitude* (failing to apply what we know), not ignorance.
- **Two types:**
  - **DO-CONFIRM** — team does the work from memory, then pauses to confirm all critical steps happened. Best for experienced operators (e.g., pre-publish check on a video).
  - **READ-DO** — read a step, do it, check it off, like a recipe. Best for rare/novel/high-stakes procedures (e.g., quarterly payroll tax filing, restoring a database).
  - Ambiguity between the two kills the checklist — state which type it is on the document.
- **Design specs:** 5–9 items (working-memory limit); runnable in **60–90 seconds**; only **"killer items"** — steps that are critical *and* commonly skipped, never a comprehensive process description; one page; precise familiar language; an explicit **pause point** ("before incision", "before hitting Publish", "before the client call").
- **Field-test rule:** first drafts always fail. Watch real users run it, note where they stall, revise, retest. A checklist is engineered, not written.
- **Communication function:** the best checklists force the team to talk (WHO surgical checklist includes team introductions). Results: WHO surgical checklist cut deaths and complications by roughly a third across 8 hospitals; the Keystone ICU line-infection checklist cut infections 66% in three months, saving ~1,500 lives and ~$175M.
- **Resistance:** skilled people feel checklists are beneath them ("greatness is improvisation"). The counter: pilots and surgeons — the highest-status operators alive — use them precisely *because* the stakes are high. Adoption requires the senior person visibly using the checklist first.

### 2.4 SYSTEMology — David Jenyns (who does the work, in what order)

The most practically useful modern framework for <20-person companies, because it solves the "founder never gets around to it" problem.

- **Critical Client Flow (CCF):** the first and only initial target is the **10–15 systems** that take a stranger to a paying, served client: attention → lead → sale → onboarding → delivery → invoice → repeat/referral. Do not document HR policies or the office wifi first.
- **Systems Champion:** the documentation program is owned by a designated person who is *not the owner*. "Business owners are often the worst people to create their own systems — their wiring works against them." The Champion needs organization, listening, curiosity, and follow-through — not documentation experience.
- **Extract, don't write:** the person who *does* the work demonstrates it (Loom / screen recording / GoPro in the field) while talking; the Champion turns the recording into the doc. The knowledgeable worker never has to write anything. This single move removes ~80% of the friction that kills documentation projects.
- **Validation by new hire:** the test of a system is whether a fresh person can execute from it without live help. New hires are your best QA — task them with following (and red-lining) SOPs during onboarding.
- **Sequencing:** (1) name the Champion, (2) protect a minimum **half-day per week**, (3) map the CCF, (4) extract via video + observation, (5) validate with real use.
- **Expected timeline:** initial results in 30–60 days; significant transformation in 6–12 months; compounding effects 12–18 months.

### 2.5 Vocabulary: SOP vs runbook vs playbook

Use the terms precisely; they imply different designs (Cutover / Upstat definitions):

| Artifact | What it is | Predictability | When it's read |
|---|---|---|---|
| **SOP** | One consistent way to do a *routine, recurring* task | High — inputs/outputs known | Scheduled work (publish the newsletter, run payroll, close a client) |
| **Runbook** | End-to-end procedure for a specific *operational/technical* scenario, often mixed human+automation | Medium | Reactive — an alert fired, something broke (sync-youtube stopped, site down) |
| **Playbook** | Strategy + scenarios + linked procedures for *unpredictable* situations | Low–medium | When judgment is needed (sponsor dispute, PR issue, key hire quits) |

Rule of thumb: routine → SOP; incident → runbook; ambiguity → playbook. A small company needs mostly SOPs, a handful of runbooks for its tech, and 2–3 playbooks.

## 3. SOP formats that actually get used

The graveyard is full of 40-page Word manuals. What survives contact with a real team:

### The layered format (default recommendation)

1. **Loom video, 2–7 minutes.** Screen recording of the actual work, narrated: name each click, explain judgment calls, flag risks, end on the "done" state. If the process is longer, split into modules — people rewatch a 4-minute clip; they avoid a 26-minute monster.
2. **Checklist, 5–12 bullets.** The execution layer — enough to do the task without rewatching once you've learned it.
3. **Screenshots with annotations** only for fragile/risky steps (irreversible actions, easy-to-miss toggles).

Why it works: video carries context and sequence (fast to *create*, great for *learning*); the checklist carries speed (great for *executing*); screenshots de-risk the scary parts. Text-only fails on transfer; video-only fails on day-to-day reference.

### Standard SOP header (metadata that keeps it alive)

Every SOP document should carry:

- **Name** — action verb + outcome ("Publish weekly newsletter," "Onboard new sponsor")
- **Owner** — one named human
- **Last updated / next review date**
- **Trigger** — when this SOP fires (schedule, event, request)
- **Tools + access needed**
- **Estimated time**
- **Type** — READ-DO or DO-CONFIRM (for the checklist portion)
- **Common mistakes** section
- **Definition of done** — what the world looks like when this is complete

### Format-to-task matching

| Task type | Best format |
|---|---|
| Simple recurring task (invoicing, publishing) | Checklist + short video |
| Complex judgment-heavy work (editing style, sales calls) | Video exemplars + principles doc + review loop (not a checklist) |
| Rare high-stakes procedure (tax filing, DB restore) | READ-DO checklist, heavily tested |
| Team-coordination moments (launch day, event day) | DO-CONFIRM checklist with pause points |
| Onboarding a role | Ordered playlist of the role's SOPs + 30/60/90 plan |

Key insight: **don't checklist creative judgment.** For taste-driven work (thumbnails, edit pacing, coaching cues at a training facility), document *principles + examples + a review/feedback loop*, and reserve checklists for the mechanical wrapper around the creative core (export settings, upload metadata, end screens).

### Naming and storage

- Folder hierarchy by function (Operations → Sales / Finance / Content / Support / Hiring), not by author or date.
- Action-verb-first names so search works ("Publish…", "Invoice…", "Reconcile…").
- **One tool, boring stack.** Notion/Google Docs + Loom beats a fancy dedicated platform nobody opens. The best SOP tool is the one already inside the team's daily workflow.

## 4. Tooling landscape (2025–2026 snapshot)

- **Notion / Google Docs** — free-to-$10/user; right answer for teams <20 unless there's a compliance need. Weakness: no review-cycle enforcement; you must build the cadence yourself (recurring tasks).
- **Loom** — the de facto video-SOP capture layer; transcripts auto-convert to draft checklists.
- **Scribe / Tango** (~$16/user/mo, free tiers) — auto-capture browser workflows into step-by-step screenshot guides. Excellent for software SOPs. Note: Tango pivoted in April 2025 toward AI browser *automation* (executing workflows, not just documenting) — buying it purely for documentation is buying yesterday's roadmap.
- **Trainual** (~$249/mo + $1,000 setup) — structured training with quizzes/completion tracking; overkill below ~20 people; teams often still need a separate SOP/knowledge base.
- **Process Street** ($100–150/mo tier) — checklist-*execution* engine with conditional logic; the distinction that matters: it runs processes as workflows with audit trails, vs. documenting them as pages.
- **Waybook, SweetProcess, Whale** — mid-market SOP platforms (~$99/mo for ~20 seats); reasonable if you want built-in review reminders and read-tracking without Trainual pricing.
- **AI generation wave (2024–2026):** tools now generate full SOPs from a raw screen recording (Scribe, Clueso, Kommodo, Guidemaker, etc.) — automatic step detection, screenshot extraction, editable text output. Practical implication: **the cost of the first draft has collapsed to near zero.** The bottleneck has moved entirely to (a) deciding what to document, (b) verification, and (c) maintenance. Advice accordingly: never accept "we don't have time to write SOPs" anymore — record the work once, let AI draft, have the Champion edit.

Selection heuristic Carl can give: *pick by the job* — knowledge lives in pages (Notion), demonstrations live in video (Loom), repeated executions live in checklist runs (Process Street or your task tool), training completion lives in an LMS-ish tool only when headcount justifies it.

## 5. The systemization playbook (step-by-step)

A concrete 90-day program for a 5–25 person company:

**Weeks 1–2: Inventory & triage**
1. List every recurring task in the business (founder + each team member braindump; 30–60 items typical).
2. Score each: frequency × pain-if-done-wrong × founder-dependency.
3. Mark the **Critical Client Flow** subset (10–15 systems from stranger → served, paying client). That's phase one. Park everything else.

**Weeks 2–3: Assign the machine**
4. Name a **Systems Champion** (organized, curious, not the founder). Protect a half-day/week minimum for them.
5. Pick the stack (default: Notion + Loom). Create the folder skeleton and the SOP template with the metadata header (§3).
6. Write the two top-layer docs once: Strategic Objective (1 page) and General Operating Principles (20–30 bullets). Two hours of founder time, enormous downstream leverage — it's what lets people decide correctly when no SOP exists.

**Weeks 3–10: Extract**
7. For each CCF process: the person who does it records themselves doing it (Loom), narrating clicks and judgment calls.
8. Champion converts recording → layered SOP (video + checklist + screenshots), applying Gawande rules to the checklist (killer items only, 5–9, typed as READ-DO or DO-CONFIRM, explicit pause point).
9. **Field test:** someone who did NOT make the SOP runs the task from it, no live help. Champion watches, logs every stall, revises. First drafts always fail — that's the process working.
10. Ship at 98%. Publish with owner + review date. Move to the next one. Target pace: 2–4 SOPs/week.

**Weeks 10–13: Operate & institutionalize**
11. Wire the maintenance loop (§6): recurring review tasks, "flag it" channel for corrections, last-reviewed dates visible.
12. Route all delegation through SOPs: from now on, when the founder hands off a task, the handoff *is* the recording session (delegate → document → verify), never a verbal walkthrough.
13. Measure: first-pass completion rate without help, founder hours reclaimed/week, questions-per-task trend, error/rework rate. After 3 months also track new-hire ramp time.

**Ongoing:** as roles fill in, do Gerber's org-chart exercise — chart of the *finished* company, position contracts per seat, founder replaces themselves bottom-up, each seat's manual assembled from the SOPs it owns.

## 6. Keeping SOPs alive (the part everyone fails)

Documentation rot is the default outcome. When ownership is fuzzy, an SOP starts drifting from reality **within a quarter**, and one visibly-wrong SOP poisons trust in the whole library ("the docs are always stale" becomes self-fulfilling).

**Ownership**
- Every SOP has exactly **one named owner** (a person, never "the ops team"). Optionally an approver for consequential ones. *No owner, no review; no approver, no scrutiny.*
- The owner is whoever runs or manages the process — not the Champion forever. Champion owns the *program*; process owners own their docs.

**Cadence (by volatility, not one-size)**

| SOP type | Review cadence |
|---|---|
| Software/SaaS-dependent | Quarterly (vendors ship UI redesigns without warning) |
| High-stakes (money, security, safety) | Quarterly + after every incident |
| Stable process-only | Annually |
| Compliance-adjacent | Annually minimum (12-month "current" convention) |
| Onboarding/role docs | On every role change + after each new-hire feedback cycle |
| Vendor/partner-dependent | At contract renewal |

**Trigger events (review within a week, regardless of cadence):** tool/UI change, incident or near-miss, the person in the role leaves, a complaint pattern, an audit finding.

**Mechanics that actually work**
- Recurring task per SOP in the existing task tool, assigned to the owner, with 2-week lead time. (No new tool — new tools are where cadences go to die.)
- Visible **"last reviewed"** date on every doc; a red/yellow/green freshness dashboard if the library is big.
- Lightweight versioning: patch (typos/screenshots, no approval) / minor (step changes, owner signs) / major (restructure or new tool, approval + notify users). Archive old versions; don't delete.
- **Follow-exactly-or-fix rule** (Carpenter): staff execute as written; if it's wrong they flag or fix it — silent deviation is the one prohibited move. This turns every user into a maintenance sensor.
- Cheap freshness signal: cost of staleness is measurable — a new hire following outdated software steps burns 20–60 minutes of troubleshooting per occurrence; multiply across a cohort and staleness has a price tag.
- New hires red-line SOPs as part of onboarding — the freshest eyes are the best auditors, and it teaches them the library exists.

## 7. Automate vs. document (the decision)

The maturity ladder — skipping rungs is the classic failure:

1. **Ad hoc** (in someone's head) →
2. **Documented** (SOP; a human follows it) →
3. **Standardized & measured** (done the same way every time; you know volume, time, error rate) →
4. **Assisted** (templates, keyboard macros, AI drafts inside the human loop) →
5. **Automated** (software executes; humans handle exceptions).

**Never automate a broken or undocumented process** — automation executes the broken process faster and hides it from view. Industry experience: ~40% of automation projects fail to deliver expected ROI and ~23% are abandoned within a year, with the main causes being governance gaps and unclear success criteria, not technology.

**Automate when:** the process is stable (unchanged ~3+ months), rule-based (no judgment mid-flow), volume is meaningful and growing, errors are creeping in, or an audit trail is required.
**Keep it human + documented when:** low volume, judgment-heavy, changing frequently, or the automation build cost exceeds a year of the manual labor it saves.

Quick ROI arithmetic Carl can do live: *(minutes per run × runs per month × loaded $/minute) vs. (build hours + maintenance hours/year)*. A 10-min task done 3×/week rarely justifies a custom build; the same task at 20×/day almost always does. And note the middle path that changed in 2024–26: **AI-assisted execution** (drafting, extraction, classification inside a human-reviewed loop) now captures most of the win at a fraction of the build cost — the SOP itself becomes the prompt/spec for the AI step.

The virtuous link: a good SOP is a *specification*. Teams with clean SOP libraries automate faster and safer because the spec already exists. (Relevant to Mayday: the Mayday Studio Automations system — trigger→action rules replacing manual reminder work — is exactly rung 5, and each automation should trace back to a documented process it replaced; the SOP remains the fallback runbook when the automation breaks.)

## 8. Common mistakes

1. **Founder writes everything from memory.** Produces idealized fiction that doesn't match real work; team ignores it. Fix: extract from the person doing the job, via recording.
2. **Documenting everything instead of the Critical Client Flow.** Six weeks on the PTO policy while sponsor onboarding lives in the founder's head.
3. **The 40-page manual.** Cognitive overload; people skim or skip. Long ≠ thorough. Split by outcome; 2–7 min video units; 5–9 item checklists.
4. **Checklist-as-training.** A checklist assumes competence and catches skipped killer items; it can't teach. Pairing failure: giving a new hire a DO-CONFIRM list for a task they've never seen.
5. **No named owner.** "Company-owned" docs rot within a quarter, and rot is contagious to the library's credibility.
6. **Write-and-forget (no cadence, no triggers).** The stale-wiki spiral: doc drifts → someone gets burned → nobody trusts docs → nobody updates docs.
7. **No field test before rollout.** First drafts always fail; untested SOPs ship their failures to the newest, most vulnerable users.
8. **Vague steps and missing rationale.** "Make sure everything is correct" is not a step. Steps without a *why* get skipped the moment they're inconvenient.
9. **Punishing deviation instead of harvesting it.** If people quietly work around the SOP, the SOP is wrong. Make "fix the doc" cheaper than "ignore the doc."
10. **Tool-first thinking.** Buying Trainual before having ten SOPs. Content and cadence first; platform later, if ever.
11. **Automating chaos.** Rung-skipping (§7). Also its mirror: refusing to automate a rung-3 process out of habit ("we've always done it manually").
12. **Systemizing the creative core.** Checklisting taste kills the product. Systemize the container (pipeline, publishing, QC, finance); protect judgment inside it with principles + exemplars + review, not steps.
13. **Hiring rockstars as a substitute for systems** (anti-Gerber). "Operable at the lowest necessary skill level" is the design goal; heroics are a system smell.

## 9. Questions Carl should ask a client

**Exposure / diagnosis**
1. "If you disappeared for 30 days starting tomorrow, what breaks first, and in what order?" (Maps owner-dependency; the answer list ≈ the documentation priority list.)
2. "Which tasks can exactly one person do? What happens the week that person quits?" (Key-person risk — the 47%-of-orgs problem.)
3. "How long until a new hire in role X is fully productive, and what fraction of that is them extracting things from people's heads?" (Benchmark: 3–6 months; 40–60% tribal-knowledge absorption.)
4. "When something is done wrong, is your instinct to blame the person or fix the system?" (Systems mindset check.)
5. "What did the last mistake cost, and would a 60-second checklist have caught it?"

**Program design**
6. "What are the 10–15 steps between a stranger and a paying, delighted client?" (Elicits the Critical Client Flow.)
7. "Who on your team is organized, curious, and finishes things — and is not you?" (Systems Champion candidate.)
8. "Can we get a half-day a week protected for this, and what will you drop to protect it?"
9. "For this SOP: is it READ-DO or DO-CONFIRM, and where's the pause point?"
10. "Is this task judgment or mechanics? Are we writing steps, or principles plus examples plus a review loop?"

**Health check on an existing library**
11. "Pick any three SOPs at random — who is the named owner, and when was each last reviewed?" (No answer = the library is decorative.)
12. "When someone finds a doc that's wrong, what do they actually do?" (Looking for a flag/fix channel; silence means silent deviation.)
13. "What percentage of new-hire questions are answered by a doc vs. by interrupting a human?"

**Automation gate**
14. "Show me the SOP for the process you want to automate." (No SOP → not ready.)
15. "How many times per month does this run, how long does it take, and how often has the process itself changed this quarter?"
16. "When the automation fails at 2am, what's the documented manual fallback and who owns it?"

**Founder psychology**
17. "Which tasks are you holding because only you *can*, versus because only you *always have*?"
18. "Are you delegating or abdicating — when you hand something off, does an SOP, a standard, and a check-in cadence go with it?"
19. "If your business had to work as the prototype for 5,000 more, what would you change first?"

## 10. Notes for the applied layer (Mayday context)

- A YouTube-first media business is unusually systemizable: the publish pipeline (ideation → script → shoot → edit → thumbnail/title → upload/metadata → distribution → sponsor deliverables) is a textbook Critical Client Flow, and most steps are screen-based — ideal for Loom/Scribe extraction. The creative core (concepting, on-camera, taste) should be protected with principles + exemplars, not steps (§3, mistake #12).
- Pre-publish is a canonical DO-CONFIRM checklist candidate (sponsor read included? end screen? title/thumbnail final? scheduled time?). Sponsor onboarding/invoicing are READ-DO candidates.
- A training facility (Neptune) is the E-Myth case almost literally: consistent client experience delivered by coaches at defined skill levels, uniform facility standards, documented session structures — the franchise-prototype standards map one-to-one, and they're also the substrate for future multi-location or licensing plays (where the 71% valuation premium logic bites hardest).
- The in-house ops app (Mayday Studio) sits at rung 4–5 of the maturity ladder: its Automations and Workflows features should each trace to a documented process, with the SOP kept as the human fallback runbook.
