# Productizing Services & Building Scalable Revenue

## TL;DR

- **The test for scalability is Warrillow's triad: teachable, valuable, repeatable.** If a senior hire can't deliver the offer start-to-finish from a documented process without founder involvement, it's not productized yet — it's a job with better branding.
- **Sequence matters: problem → buyer → scope → package → price.** Most founders package first and position later, which is the #1 cause of productized offers reverting to bespoke work within 90 days.
- **Productize your most repeatable service, not your most popular one.** Popularity often correlates with customization demand. Score candidates on repeatability, automation potential, market demand, and unit economics at scale (RAMP).
- **Recurring revenue is the single biggest driver of business valuation**, and not all recurrence is equal — contracted/auto-renewing revenue beats re-purchase habits beats one-off repeat buyers. Design for the highest rung you can credibly reach.
- **Churn math is brutal and non-negotiable:** at 8% monthly churn a 100-client business loses half its clients in nine months. Good SaaS-style benchmark is ~3–4% monthly; consumer/media runs ~6.5%. Annual plans cut churn ~40% vs monthly; failed-payment recovery alone can reclaim a large share of "involuntary" churn (~25–40% of total cancellations).
- **The ladder from services to leverage:** custom service → productized service → membership/subscription → licensing your system → software. Each rung trades revenue-per-client for margin-per-hour and enterprise value. Gym Launch (service→licensing) and Basecamp/Mailchimp (agency internal tool→SaaS) are the canonical climbs.
- **Charge upfront.** Upfront payment is simultaneously a cash-flow engine, a commitment filter, and a valuation signal. Warrillow: charge in advance or progress-bill; never fund client work with your own working capital.
- **Fixed price must be public and fixed scope must include an explicit "not included" list.** An offer without a written exclusions document becomes a custom retainer within two client cycles.

## 1. The core problem: the time-for-money ceiling

Every service business starts as selling hours. The ceiling is structural, not motivational:

- **Revenue is capped by capacity** (bodies × billable hours × utilization). Growth requires hiring ahead of revenue, which compresses margin exactly when you're growing.
- **Every sale is a negotiation** — proposals, scoping calls, custom pricing. Sales cost scales linearly with revenue.
- **The founder is the product.** Clients buy the founder's judgment; delegation degrades the deliverable; the founder can't leave.
- **Enterprise value is ~zero.** A buyer acquiring a bespoke services firm is buying a payroll obligation and a founder flight risk. Warrillow's whole thesis in *Built to Sell*: businesses built on custom work are unsellable regardless of profit.

The escape is not "work harder" or "hire more" — it's changing the unit of sale from *time* to *outcome*, then from *outcome* to *access*, then from *access* to *system*. That's the whole document in one sentence.

**Scalability spectrum (memorize this ladder):**

| Rung | Unit of sale | Marginal cost of next customer | Typical gross margin | Enterprise value multiple |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Custom services | Hours / bespoke projects | ~Linear (labor) | 30–50% | 0.5–1× SDE, often unsellable |
| 2. Productized service | Fixed-scope package | Sub-linear (process + juniors) | 50–70% | 2–4× EBITDA |
| 3. Subscription / membership | Ongoing access | Low (shared delivery) | 60–80% | 3–6× EBITDA, MRR-multiple pricing |
| 4. Licensing / certification | Your system, others deliver | Near-zero | 80–90% | High — IP business |
| 5. Software / digital product | Code / content | ~Zero | 80–95% | Revenue multiples (3–10× ARR) |

You don't jump rungs. Each rung is built from the documented learnings of the one below it. Gym Launch couldn't have existed without Hormozi personally doing turnarounds first; Basecamp couldn't have existed without 37signals' agency pain.

## 2. The Built to Sell framework (John Warrillow)

The most durable named framework for the service→product transition. Core criteria for a scalable offer:

1. **Teachable** — deliverable by employees through process or technology, not founder genius.
2. **Valuable** — differentiated enough to avoid commoditization and price pressure.
3. **Repeatable** — customers need to buy again and again (recurring by design, not by luck).

### Warrillow's standardization playbook
1. **Specialize.** Pick the one service you do best; kill the rest. Niching down is the prerequisite, not an optimization.
2. **Name the process.** Turn the service into a named, step-by-step offering ("Five-Step Logo Design Process"). Naming converts a service into an asset and flips the sales conversation from "what would you like us to do?" to "here's our proven solution."
3. **Fix the scope and the price.** Written deliverables, written exclusions, one price.
4. **Charge upfront** or progress-bill. Positive cash-flow cycle; excess cash also raises acquisition offers.
5. **Say no** to out-of-scope work — the hardest and most load-bearing step. Every exception you grant re-bespokes the business.
6. **Diversify the client base:** no single client >15% of revenue.
7. **Hire two salespeople, not one** — two competitive reps outperform a lone one and prove the sale isn't founder-dependent. Hire people who've sold *products*, not services (services salespeople over-customize to close).
8. **Two years of financials** under the standardized model before you're credibly sellable; speak like a product company ("customers," not "clients").

### Warrillow's hierarchy of recurring revenue (ranked, least → most valuable)
1. Consumables (toothpaste — repurchase habit, no lock-in)
2. Sunk-cost consumables (razor blades — installed base)
3. Renewable subscriptions (magazines)
4. Sunk-cost renewable subscriptions (Bloomberg Terminal — switching cost + subscription)
5. Auto-renewal subscriptions (storage — default-continue)
6. **Hard contracts** (wireless agreements — contractually guaranteed)

Design decision for Carl's clients: whatever you're building, ask "which rung is this on, and what would move it up one?" (e.g., a month-to-month gym membership is rung 5; a 12-month training-block agreement is rung 6; a merch drop is rung 1).

## 3. Productized services: the working model

### Definition
A productized service sells a **fixed-scope, fixed-price, pre-defined package** delivered by a standardized process — bought off the shelf like a product, delivered by humans like a service. It tells the buyer exactly what they get, when, and for how much, before any conversation happens.

### The Three-Decision Framework (do these in order)
1. **What problem** — one specific, painful, recurring buyer problem.
2. **For whom** — a defined customer profile you can actually reach through specific channels. Specificity test: "social media management" fails; "LinkedIn content for B2B SaaS companies with sales teams" passes. One-sentence test: "monthly SEO content for B2B SaaS companies" — if you can't say it in one sentence, the niche is too broad.
3. **At what scope** — bounded deliverables with an explicit not-included list.

Most founders run this backwards (package first, positioning later) and fail.

### RAMP: scoring which service to productize
Score each candidate service 1–10 on:
- **R**epeatability — how standardized is delivery already?
- **A**utomation potential — which steps can be systematized or templated?
- **M**arket demand — validated interest (people already paying someone for this)?
- **P**rofitability at scale — do unit economics improve or degrade with volume?

Services scoring 8+ across all four are primary candidates. Critical warning from failure-mode research: **productize your most repeatable service, not your most popular one** — popular services are often popular *because* they're customized.

### The three delivery models + pricing benchmarks (2025–26, US market)

| Model | Structure | Best for | Typical pricing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **One-off** | Single payment, defined output | High-value discrete deliverables (audits, brand kits, migrations) | $500–$5,000 |
| **Retainer** | Monthly fee, fixed work volume | Consistent monthly output (articles, ads, edits) | $1,500–$10,000/mo |
| **Unlimited (queue)** | Flat fee, unlimited requests, one-at-a-time queue | High-volume, fast-turnaround work | $3,000–$15,000/mo (agency-grade); solo/design subs $500–$5,000/mo |

**The "unlimited" trick (Design Pickle's insight):** unlimited requests at a flat price with turnaround time as the natural throttle. One dedicated designer works the queue sequentially; clients self-regulate volume vs. speed. "Unlimited" is a marketing word for "serialized" — capacity is protected by the queue, not the contract.

**Pricing methodology:** price on value delivered, not hours consumed. Start cost-plus (delivery cost + target margin), raise with demand. Never underprice to "test demand" — validation is calling five prospects and asking about the price, not launching cheap. Underpriced launches anchor the market and attract the worst-fit clients.

### Launch playbook (validated sequence)
1. **5–15 customer conversations before building anything.** Ask: what's the problem, what have you tried, what do you spend now, how often does this recur? Capture exact language. (Operators at 25–32% margins validated with 10–15 interviews first; those at 18–22% built first and hoped.)
2. **Package**: scope, exclusions, turnaround, revision policy, cancellation terms, pricing model, guarantee/trial structure.
3. **Test delivery internally** before taking paying clients — run the process on a fake or friendly client.
4. **Sell the discounted first cohort** back to your interview subjects in exchange for feedback + testimonial: "Based on our conversation, I put together [package] at [reduced rate] in exchange for honest feedback."
5. **Service page** = one-sentence description, real work samples, case studies, FAQ that pre-answers objections (turnaround, revisions, cancellation).
6. **Intake form replaces the discovery call.** Structured brief collected at purchase, conditional logic per service, becomes the project brief. Calls only for genuinely out-of-scope custom requests.
7. **Scope-creep decision rules, written in advance:** in-scope → do it without discussion; adjacent → priced add-on/upsell; out-of-scope → decline clearly, refer out. No live judgment calls.
8. **Document, then hire.** "If you can't write down the process, you're not ready to hire for it." Automate rule-based tasks first (status updates, task assignment, invoicing, onboarding emails); hire for judgment tasks second.

### Delivery ops: the four documents
1. **Onboarding** — the first-48-hours experience, welcome sequence, intake-completion triggers.
2. **Request handling** — assignment logic, quality benchmarks, internal review before anything reaches the client.
3. **Communication standards** — update cadence, channels, delay protocols, escalation.
4. **Quality control** — pre-submission checklist, named reviewer.

### The founder-dependency test
> If the offer requires the founder to scope, approve, or quality-check every deliverable, it's not a productized service.

The test: can a senior hire deliver start-to-finish from the documented process with zero founder involvement? This is the single most common failure point for technically-founded shops.

### Real productized-service outcomes (with numbers)
- **Design Pickle** (unlimited graphic design, flat monthly): $0 → $160K MRR / 45 staff in 2 years (2015–17); later $100K → $500K+ MRR.
- **Testimonial Hero** (video testimonials, one-off pricing): $3.5M/yr, 48 staff, cash upfront before delivery.
- **SeoBrothers** (white-label SEO components sold to 60 agencies): $3.6M/yr, $300K+ MRR, 45 staff.
- **VideoHusky** (subscription video editing): $1.2M/yr; founder hired a GM to detach himself.
- **180Sites** (web design subscription): $950K/yr, ~500 clients at $180/mo (24-mo commitment → $150/mo), 8-person team, 100+ clients from referral partnerships with adjacent agencies; later reported at ~$130K MRR / 650+ clients via community strategy.
- **The Match Artist** (dating-app photography — B2C, proof productization isn't only B2B): $896K/yr, 9 people.
- **MixBloom** (white-label social content for agencies): $636K/yr, $199–549/mo tiers; B2B white-label = bigger deals, better retention, built-in upgrade path.
- **Hilvy** (solo Webflow designer, UK): $74K/yr with zero employees — the floor case: productization works at n=1.
- **WP Curve** (unlimited WordPress fixes, monthly sub): acquired by GoDaddy — the exit case.
- Micro-pricing exists too: Roast My Landing Page $350/audit; GMB Gorilla $400/mo; 100 Pound Social £100–255/mo.

Pattern across winners: **narrow niche + one delivery model + white-label or referral channel + retention obsession.** Multiple founders cite "being selective about which clients we serve" as the retention lever.

## 4. Recurring revenue & subscription mechanics

### Why recurrence is the prize
Predictable revenue de-risks hiring and marketing spend, smooths cash flow, and is the primary driver of valuation multiples. But recurrence only compounds if churn is controlled — otherwise you've built a leaky bucket with a subscription billing system.

### Churn benchmarks (know these cold)
- **Overall subscription average:** ~3.3% monthly (Recurly, across their base). ~2.4% voluntary + ~0.9% involuntary.
- **B2B** (software, professional services): ~3.8% monthly average.
- **DTC/consumer** (media, retail, education): ~6.5% monthly average.
- **SaaS rule of thumb:** 1–5% monthly; 4% is "good," under 2% is excellent.
- **Physical subscriptions:** 5–7% monthly vs. 3–5% digital. Replenishment boxes 4–7%; discovery/curation boxes 10–15%.
- **Maturity effect:** companies <3 years old churn 4–24%; 10+ year companies churn 2–4%. Early churn chaos is normal; permanent churn chaos is fatal.
- **The compounding kill-shot:** at 8% monthly churn, a 100-client business loses half its clients in nine months. Growth spend just refills the bucket.

### The five highest-leverage churn levers (with effect sizes)
1. **Annual plans:** annual subscribers are ~40% less likely to cancel than monthly, with materially higher LTV. Discount the annual plan; it's cheaper than the churn.
2. **Involuntary churn recovery:** failed payments cause up to ~40% of all cancellations. Smart retry logic, dunning sequences, and card-updater services recover a majority of these automatically. This is free money; fix it before touching anything else.
3. **Three-tier pricing:** captures ~60% more revenue than single-tier (anchoring + self-selection + upgrade path).
4. **Price-increase discipline:** 71% of churned subscribers cite price increases as reason #1. Grandfather existing subscribers or pair increases with visible added value.
5. **Commitment structures:** contracts/commitment periods (180Sites' 24-month term that *rewards* completion with a lower rate) move you up Warrillow's recurrence hierarchy.

### Membership economics (the hybrid pattern)
Brands pairing a membership with transactional commerce report members = 20–30% of customers but **60–70% of revenue** — the "I'm already paying, I should use it" psychology drives frequency and AOV. This is directly relevant to any business with both a subscription and one-off sales (creator with merch; gym with camps/assessments): the membership is the flywheel hub, everything else is spokes.

### Creator/community membership benchmarks (2025–26)
- 88% of community builders now monetize via paid memberships (up from 54% a year prior) — memberships have become the *primary* revenue foundation for community-led creator businesses, not one option among many.
- Most common price band: **$26–50/month.**
- Revenue distribution is a power law: ~21% pre-revenue, ~21% under $10K/yr, 16% at $10–50K, 15% at $50–250K, 8% at $250K–1M, 4% over $1M.
- Stacked monetization ranked by adoption: memberships 88%, courses 53%, coaching/services 51%, digital products 37%, affiliates 22%, sponsorships 18%. Average professional creator runs ~3.2 revenue streams.
- Retention drivers reported by operators: member transformation as the growth strategy (69%), emotional safety as differentiator (73%). Notably 39% deliberately de-prioritize growth in favor of higher-ticket offers and 12% cap membership size — small-and-expensive is a legitimate strategy, not a failure state.
- Creator revenue mix industry-wide (2025): sponsorships 31.5%, ad-share 26.8%, subscriptions 18.4%, merch 11.3%, courses/licensing 7.2%, crowdfunding 4.8%. Sponsorship-heavy mixes are the most fragile (single-buyer risk, ad-market cyclicality); subscription share is the stability metric to grow.

### Training-facility recurring revenue (Neptune-relevant reference points)
- Typical gyms: 70–80% of revenue from membership dues; 20–30% ancillary.
- **Two-Brain small-gym model:** ~1,000 sq ft, high-value services only (personal + semi-private training), target **ARM (average revenue per member) $500+/month**, top performers $600+. PT ARPU ~$440/mo vs ~$280/mo for unlimited classes. Small client count × high ARM × long retention beats big-box volume for a specialized facility.
- Revenue density: traditional gyms $50–100/sq ft/yr; boutique/specialized $100–350/sq ft/yr. Utilization (40% → 85%) is the primary lever once the lease is signed, because rent is fixed.
- Margins: 10–15% net typical; boutique studios can break even in month 1 with capital payback ~14 months on ~$330K build-out (model figures, not guarantees).
- For a baseball lab: semi-private training blocks, assessed-and-programmed memberships, and team/org contracts map to Warrillow rungs 5–6; drop-in cage rentals are rung 1. Sell blocks and memberships; treat drop-ins as lead gen.

## 5. Software as a side-product of internal tools

The pattern: an agency/operator builds a tool to solve its own pain → clients see it and ask for it → the tool out-earns the service → the service gets shut down.

### Canonical cases
- **Basecamp (37signals):** web design firm builds internal project-management tool (2003) to fix its own coordination chaos; clients used it on projects and asked for their own accounts; charging began 2004; **within ~18 months Basecamp out-earned the entire web design business**; the firm exited services entirely. Bonus: Ruby on Rails itself was extracted from Basecamp's internals — one internal tool yielded two world-changing products.
- **Mailchimp:** Atlanta web agency (Rocket Science Group) builds a simple email tool from spare code in 2001 because clients found existing tools too complex/expensive. Spent all of 2005 talking themselves into trading $25–50K web gigs for $25/mo accounts, shut the agency in 2006–07. Accidental-freemium in 2009 (engineering constraint forced a single free-tier product) became the growth engine. $525M revenue 2017 → $800M 2021 → **$12B acquisition by Intuit**, fully bootstrapped.

### The decision framework: should you productize your internal tool?
Signals it's worth pursuing:
1. **External pull exists** — clients/partners have *asked* to use it (Basecamp's trigger). Building it speculatively is a different, riskier business.
2. **The pain is general**, not an artifact of your weird workflow. Test: can you describe the user without describing yourself?
3. **You can support strangers.** Internal tools have zero onboarding, zero docs, zero permissions model, zero billing, and an SLA of "yell across the room." Productizing typically means rebuilding 60–80% of it. Budget for that, not for "we'll just add a login page."
4. **It doesn't cannibalize your edge.** If the tool *is* your operating advantage, licensing it arms competitors (fine if you're exiting services, dangerous if you're not).
5. **You'll accept the identity shift.** Both canonical cases required killing the profitable service business. Half-measures (agency + SaaS side hustle indefinitely) tend to starve both; Mailchimp's founders explicitly forced the choice.

**Middle paths short of full SaaS:** white-label the tool to a handful of peers (validates demand, revenue without a product org); open-source the framework for authority (Rails play); or keep it internal as a margin/quality moat and sell the *service* it powers. Not every internal tool should become a company — most shouldn't. The bar is external pull plus willingness to staff a real product.

*(Mayday-relevant: an internal ops app like Mayday Studio is exactly this category. The honest default is "moat, not product" until other creator teams are actively asking for it — then the 60–80% rebuild rule applies.)*

## 6. Licensing expertise

Licensing = granting others the right to use your methodology, materials, brand, or system to serve *their* customers. It's the rung where your marginal delivery cost approaches zero because licensees supply the labor.

### The Gym Launch case study (the definitive service→licensing arc)
1. **Phase 1 — done-for-you (proof):** Hormozi flew to struggling gyms, ran two-week turnarounds personally — his marketing, his sales closing — for a revenue share. Brutal, unscalable, and *exactly the right first move*: it produced a proven, documented system with receipts.
2. **Phase 2 — licensing (scale):** 2016, sold his six gyms, packaged the sales letters, ad creatives, scripts, and SOPs into a turnkey licensed system. Gym owners paid a licensing fee, got the system, executed it themselves.
3. **Results:** first year ~$2.3M/month revenue, $17M profit; by 2020, 4,500+ facilities across 13 countries, $120M+ cumulative sales; 2021, 66% stake sold for $46.2M. Capital-light throughout.
4. **Why it scaled:** the system was born from personally solving the problem repeatedly (credibility + real SOPs); the buyer (gym owner) captured the upside directly, making ROI self-evident; delivery labor belonged to the licensee.

**The transferable lesson:** the sequence is *do it yourself → do it for others → teach others to do it → license the system*. Skipping the early rungs produces info-products with no receipts; staying too long on them caps you at your own hours.

### License vs. certification
- **License:** right to use your methodology + materials, usually with training included. Lower control, lower price, wider distribution.
- **Certification:** license + training + **demonstrated mastery** (testing, continuing education, renewal). Higher price, quality control over who represents your method, and a recurring revenue layer (renewals, recert fees). Certification programs also create a practitioner network that markets the methodology for you.

### Deal structures
- Typical shape: **upfront fee + ongoing royalty** (flat annual, per-user/per-seat, or % of licensee revenue). Curriculum/course licensing often uses flat fees or per-user royalties; exclusivity, territory, and breadth of rights push rates up; non-exclusive narrow rights push them down.
- The value drivers of your rate: uniqueness of the IP, demonstrated results (case-study receipts), hardness to replicate, and how much of the licensee's revenue the system credibly drives.
- Protect the asset: trademark the method's name, keep the deepest layer (community, updates, data, certification) subscription-gated so licensees stay attached rather than graduating away.

*(Neptune-relevant: a proven baseball development curriculum — assessment protocol, programming system, coach training — is licensable to other facilities/academies once it has receipts from the flagship lab. The flagship is Phase 1; licensing is a Phase 2/3 decision, never a launch strategy.)*

## 7. Evaluating which revenue streams scale: the scorecard

When a client has (or is considering) multiple revenue streams, score each 1–5 on six dimensions:

| Dimension | Question | 5 looks like | 1 looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Marginal cost** | What does customer n+1 cost to serve? | ~$0 (software, license) | Full labor cost (custom service) |
| **Founder hours** | Does the founder's calendar cap it? | Zero founder involvement | Founder in every delivery |
| **Recurrence quality** | Warrillow rung? | Contract / auto-renew | One-off, no repurchase |
| **Churn/retention** | Monthly churn vs. category benchmark | Beats benchmark (<3% B2B / <6% DTC) | Above benchmark, unmeasured |
| **Acquisition channel** | Is there a repeatable, non-founder channel? | SEO/referral/partner engine running | Founder's network only |
| **Pricing power** | Differentiated or commodity? | Named method, receipts, waitlist | Bid against five competitors |

**Interpretation:**
- Streams scoring 24+ deserve disproportionate investment — feed them.
- Streams at 15–23 usually have one or two fixable dimensions — fix the cheapest one first (it's almost always involuntary churn or founder-in-delivery).
- Streams under 15 are lifestyle revenue: fine to keep if low-effort, but never "scale" them — you'd be scaling a treadmill.
- **Portfolio rule:** grow the *subscription share* of total revenue over time (creator mixes average ~18% subscriptions vs ~31% sponsorships; every point moved from sponsorship to subscription is a point moved from fragile to durable). And enforce Warrillow's 15% cap — no single client/sponsor over 15% of revenue.

**The compounding question:** for each stream, ask "if we doubled it, what breaks?" If the answer is "nothing — servers/queue/licensees absorb it," it scales. If the answer is "we hire six people and the founder reviews everything," it doesn't yet.

## 8. Common mistakes

1. **Productizing the popular service instead of the repeatable one.** Popularity often *comes from* customization. Run RAMP, not applause.
2. **Half-productization: hiding the price.** Beautiful packaging, defined scope, fixed timeline… "contact us for pricing." A fixed, visible price is the core of the model, not a detail.
3. **No written exclusions list.** Without a "this is not included" document, the offer becomes a custom retainer within two client cycles.
4. **Scope creep via bad-fit sales.** 52% of projects experience scope creep (avg 27% cost overrun). It usually starts in the sales process: saying yes to a client whose situation doesn't match the offer, then making exceptions to close. Clean offers get customized back into bespoke work within ~90 days this way.
5. **The "just one small change" death spiral.** One accommodation per client → six months later, eight clients on eight different processes → 15–30% margin erosion and 40–60% longer delivery times. Standardization dies quietly, one yes at a time.
6. **Underpricing to validate.** Test price by asking five prospects, not by launching cheap. Cheap launches anchor the market and select for the worst clients.
7. **Founder-dependency dressed up as quality control.** If every deliverable needs the founder's approval, you've productized the marketing, not the business.
8. **Ignoring involuntary churn.** Up to ~40% of subscription cancellations are failed payments. Retry logic + dunning + card updaters is the highest-ROI retention project almost every subscription business hasn't done.
9. **Building before validating.** Operators who did 10–15 customer interviews first run 25–32% margins; build-first-hope-later operators run 18–22%.
10. **Running legacy custom work alongside the productized offer indefinitely.** The custom work always wins the internal resource fights (bigger invoices, louder clients) and the product starves. Set a sunset date. (Basecamp and Mailchimp both had to kill the agency.)
11. **Hiring before documenting.** If the process isn't written, the hire learns your improvisation, not your system.
12. **Confusing "unlimited" with uncapped capacity.** The queue is the throttle. Selling unlimited without a serialization mechanism is selling margin erosion.
13. **Raising prices on existing subscribers carelessly.** 71% of churned subscribers cite price increases first. Grandfather or add visible value.
14. **Treating licensing/courses as a shortcut.** Licensing without done-for-you receipts is an info-product with no proof. The sequence (do → do-for-others → teach → license) is not optional.

## 9. Questions Carl should ask (diagnostics)

**On the current model:**
1. "If you personally disappeared for 30 days, which revenue streams keep running? Which stop?" (founder-dependency map)
2. "What's your one-sentence answer to 'what do you do and for whom'?" (positioning specificity test)
3. "Which service do you deliver most *identically* across clients — not most often, most identically?" (RAMP-R candidate)
4. "Show me your last five proposals. How much of each was copy-paste?" (the copy-paste percentage *is* the productization opportunity)
5. "What percentage of revenue is your largest client or sponsor?" (>15% = concentration risk, act on it)

**On recurring revenue:**
6. "What's your monthly churn — measured, not felt? Voluntary vs. failed payments?" (if they can't split it, the involuntary fix is unclaimed money)
7. "What Warrillow rung is each stream on, and what's the cheapest move up one rung?" (e.g., month-to-month → annual with discount; repeat buyer → subscription)
8. "Do you offer an annual plan? What's the discount?" (annual cuts churn ~40%)
9. "What share of total revenue is subscription vs. one-off vs. sponsor?" (track the subscription share as a KPI)

**On the next rung:**
10. "Has anyone *asked* to buy your internal tool/system/process? Who, and what did they say?" (external pull test — the Basecamp trigger)
11. "If a competent stranger got your SOPs tomorrow, could they deliver your result? If not, what's missing — and is that gap your moat or just undocumented?" (licensability test)
12. "If this stream doubled next quarter, what breaks?" (marginal cost reality check)
13. "What would you have to *stop doing* to give the scalable stream a real chance?" (the sunset-date question; every canonical case killed the old business)

**On pricing and packaging:**
14. "Is your price on the website? Why not?" (half-productization check)
15. "Where's your written 'not included' list?" (scope defense)
16. "How many pricing tiers do you have?" (one tier leaves ~60% of capturable revenue on the table vs. three)
17. "When did you last raise prices, and what happened to churn?" (pricing power evidence)

## 10. Quick-reference: applying the ladder to a creator + facility business

*(Generic pattern, noted because it recurs with creator-led media companies building physical ventures.)*

| Asset | Rung 1 (now) | Rung 2–3 (productize/recur) | Rung 4–5 (license/software) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Audience/content | Sponsorships, AdSense (fragile: ~31%/27% of creator mixes) | Membership at $26–50/mo band; annual plans; member-only community | License content formats; sell the playbook |
| Expertise | 1:1 coaching, consulting | Cohort programs, assessed training blocks, semi-private ($500+ ARM target) | Certify coaches, license curriculum to other facilities (Gym Launch arc) |
| Internal tools | Private ops advantage (moat) | White-label to peer teams *if pulled* | SaaS only with external pull + product-org commitment (Basecamp/Mailchimp bar) |
| Physical facility | Drop-in rentals (rung-1 revenue) | Memberships + committed training blocks (70–80% of revenue from dues; utilization 40→85% is the lever) | Multi-site licensing of the proven model |

The strategic through-line: **use the unscalable thing (founder talent, flagship facility, custom wins) to generate receipts, then package the receipts.** Never skip the receipts.
