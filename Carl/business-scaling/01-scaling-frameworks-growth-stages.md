# Scaling Frameworks & Predictable Growth Stages

## TL;DR

- **Growth stalls are predictable, not random.** The population data is brutal: ~96% of US businesses never pass $1M revenue, ~4% sit between $1M–$10M, ~0.4% between $10M–$50M, and only ~0.06% exceed $50M. The stall points cluster at ~$1M, ~$3M, and ~$10M, and each requires a *different* company than the one that got there.
- **Every stall is the same stall in disguise: a system built for the previous size is still running.** At $1M it's the sales system (founder closes everything). At $3M it's the management system (no real second layer). At $10M it's the founder's identity (company organized around one brain).
- **Pick ONE operating system and run it for at least 4 quarters.** For a company under ~20 people, EOS is the default answer — it's simpler and prescriptive. Scaling Up is the upgrade when strategy, cash, or founder-dependency (not just execution discipline) is the constraint. A fully-implemented EOS beats a half-implemented Scaling Up every time.
- **The founder must move from doer → manager → architect, and each move is an identity crisis, not a skills gap.** Founders resist because roles have become part of who they are. The delegation ceiling reliably hits at 7–10 employees, when informal decision-making collapses.
- **Meeting rhythm is the cheapest, highest-leverage intervention.** Daily 5–15 min huddle, weekly 60–90 min tactical (Level 10 / weekly meeting), monthly management half-day, quarterly offsite. "To move faster, pulse faster." If a client does nothing else, install this.
- **One accountable name per number.** "If more than one person is accountable, then no one is accountable" (Harnish). Most small-company chaos traces to shared or absent ownership of functions and metrics.
- **Cash is the blind spot of small-company operating systems.** EOS barely touches it; Scaling Up treats it as one of four core decisions. Fast growth *consumes* cash — model the cash conversion cycle before pushing growth, especially for anything with physical operations (a training facility very much included).

## 1. The Base Rates: Why Scaling Is Rare

Carl should anchor every scaling conversation in these numbers, because founders systematically underestimate how unusual passing each threshold is.

**US business population by revenue (Harnish / Scaling Up data, widely cited):**

| Revenue band | Share of ~28–35M US businesses |
|---|---|
| < $1M | ~96% |
| $1M – $10M | ~4% |
| $10M – $50M | ~0.4% |
| > $50M | ~0.06% |

**SaaS-flavored attrition ladder (Notion Capital "rule of 1s and 3s" — directionally useful for any recurring-revenue business):**
- ~20% never reach $1M (no product-market fit)
- ~40% of the rest never pass $3M (founder-led sales ceiling)
- ~60% fail to build a real go-to-market and reach $10M
- ~80% fail to make GTM repeatable and pass $30M
- Of those passing $30M, only ~10% reach $100M within 10–15 years

**The $3M–$10M "valley of death":** roughly 60% of companies that reach $3M get stuck in this band and either die, plateau permanently, or sell cheap. Only ~20% of businesses reaching $3M ever make it to $30M.

Key insight Harnish hammers: **the skills to go $1M→$10M are completely different from the skills to go $10M→$50M.** A stall is almost never a market problem or an effort problem — it's the company still running last-stage systems. Each band also has a characteristic dominant pain: $1–10M is a *cash* problem, $10M+ is a *competition* problem, $50M+ is an *operational precision* problem.

## 2. Greiner Growth Curve (1972, revised 1998)

Larry Greiner's "Evolution and Revolution as Organizations Grow" (HBR) is the granddaddy of stage models. Core mechanic: each growth phase is a calm **evolution** powered by one management style, which eventually creates its own **revolution** (crisis). The solution to each crisis becomes the engine of the next phase — and the seed of the next crisis.

| Phase | Growth through… | Management style | Ends in crisis of… | Resolution |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Creativity** | Founders build product, informal comms, everyone does everything | **Leadership** — informality can't coordinate a growing team | Bring in professional management / formal structure |
| 2 | **Direction** | Hierarchy, budgets, functional departments, top-down | **Autonomy** — capable mid-level people are strangled by centralized decisions | Delegate real authority downward |
| 3 | **Delegation** | Decentralized units, managers own P&L-like scope, top mgmt monitors | **Control** — units drift apart, leadership feels it's lost the plot | Coordination systems, not re-centralization |
| 4 | **Coordination** | Product groups, formal planning, ROI-allocated capital, company-wide incentives | **Red tape** — process becomes more important than results | Culture of collaboration over procedure |
| 5 | **Collaboration** | Matrix/team structures, professional judgment over formal controls | **Internal growth** — organic growth exhausted | Growth via partnerships, M&A, networks |
| 6 (1998 addition) | **Extra-organizational solutions** | Alliances, outsourcing, mergers | — | — |

**How Carl uses it:**
- Small companies live almost entirely in Phases 1–3. The most common client situation: a Phase-1 company hitting the **leadership crisis** (typically 7–15 people) while the founder still thinks the fix is working harder.
- Diagnostic warning signs of an approaching crisis (Mindtools): people feel managers/procedures are getting in the way; people feel unfairly rewarded for effort; unhappiness and higher-than-usual turnover.
- The classic mistake is solving a crisis with the *previous* phase's tool — e.g., answering the autonomy crisis with more direction (more rules, more founder approvals), which just deepens it.
- Phases don't run on a clock; their length depends on market growth rate. But the longer a company sits in one phase, the harder the transition gets — habits calcify.
- Caveat Greiner himself gave: not every business hits every crisis in order. Use it as a lens, not a horoscope.

## 3. Predictable Success (Les McKeown)

McKeown's seven-stage arc adds what Greiner lacks: a **decline** side, and the idea that there's a peak state you can hold indefinitely if you balance entrepreneurship with process.

1. **Early Struggle** — fighting for cash and market validation. Two killers: running out of money, no real market.
2. **Fun** — sales take off, the myth-making era, "big dogs" (early loyal doers) dominate. Growth by heroics.
3. **Whitewater** — complexity arrives; the shift from sales-obsession to profitability and consistency. Heroics start producing errors. This is where systems must be installed, and where most small companies thrash.
4. **Predictable Success** — the peak: the org can *set and consistently hit* its goals. Entrepreneurial energy and process discipline in balance. Unlike Greiner, McKeown says you can stay here indefinitely — it's not a phase you inevitably exit, it's a state you maintain.
5. **Treadmill** — over-corrected into process; lots of energy, little forward motion; creative people start leaving.
6. **The Big Rut** — process and administration matter more than results; the org loses self-awareness and can no longer diagnose its own decline.
7. **Death Rattle** — final resuscitation attempts (acquisition, bankruptcy restructuring); the organization dies in its current form.

**Carl's use:** the Fun→Whitewater transition is the single most relevant McKeown moment for a small creator-led company — it's the point where "we just make great stuff and hustle" stops working and the founder experiences systems/process as the enemy of the magic. The counter is that process done right *protects* the creative core rather than replacing it. Also useful as a warning label when installing an operating system: overshoot and you land on the Treadmill.

## 4. Scaling Up / Rockefeller Habits (Verne Harnish)

The most complete of the small/mid-company operating systems. Built around **Four Decisions** every company must get right, each with a one-page tool. Origin: *Mastering the Rockefeller Habits* (1999) distilled John D. Rockefeller's three fundamentals — **Priorities, Data, Rhythm** — then *Scaling Up* (2014) expanded it.

### The Four Decisions

**1. People** — attract and keep A-players; organize as a "team of teams."
- **FACe** (Function Accountability Chart): every major function has exactly ONE accountable name, and every line of the P&L/balance sheet has an owner. Harnish's distinction: *accountability* ("the ability to count" — one person) vs *responsibility* (everyone involved).
- **PACe** (Process Accountability Chart): the 4–9 processes that cross functions, each with an owner.
- Topgrading-style hiring for A-players; One-Page Personal Plan for individuals.

**2. Strategy** — differentiation you can state simply, driving sustainable top-line growth.
- **7 Strata of Strategy:** words you own in the market (mindshare), core customer (definable in ≤25 words), 3 brand promises with KPIs, a guarantee (brand-promise catalyst), one-phrase strategy, 3–5 differentiating activities, X-factor (10–100x advantage on an industry bottleneck), BHAG + profit-per-X.
- **One-Page Strategic Plan (OPSP):** the whole strategy — values, purpose, BHAG, 3-year targets, annual initiatives, quarterly rocks, theme, individual KPIs — on one page everyone can see.

**3. Execution** — the three Rockefeller fundamentals, targeting profitability at ~3x industry average.
- **Priorities:** one company-wide #1 priority per quarter (the Critical Number) plus 3–5 ranked rocks, wrapped in a quarterly theme with a celebration attached.
- **Data:** weekly customer and employee feedback loops feeding the exec meeting.
- **Rhythm:** see meeting cadence below.

**4. Cash** — "Cash is the oxygen that fuels growth." Growth eats cash; the goal is to fund growth internally.
- **Cash Conversion Cycle:** days from paying a dollar out to getting it back. Shorten each segment: sales cycle, make/production cycle, delivery cycle, billing/collections cycle.
- **7 financial levers** on price, volume, COGS, opex, AR, inventory, AP — model how a 1% or 1-day improvement in each changes cash.

### The 10 Rockefeller Habits (checklist form)

1. **Executive team is healthy and aligned** — weekly strategic-thinking meeting, monthly exec education, safe constructive debate. (Do this one FIRST.)
2. **Everyone aligned on the #1 quarterly priority** — Critical Number, 3–5 ranked rocks, announced theme, visible progress. (Case in point: The City Bin Co.'s "Life Begins at 40" theme added €40K/month during a recession.)
3. **Communication rhythm established** — daily 5–15 min huddle; weekly 60–90 min; monthly half/full day; quarterly + annual 1–3 day offsites. "To move faster, pulse faster."
4. **Every facet has one accountable person** — FACe + PACe done; financial line items assigned.
5. **Ongoing employee input** — weekly Start/Stop/Keep conversations; obstacles and opportunities harvested and closed by mid-management.
6. **Ongoing customer input** — weekly "4Q" conversations; every senior leader talks to at least one customer weekly.
7. **Core values and purpose are alive** — referenced in praise and reprimand, wired into hiring/onboarding/appraisal.
8. **Strategy can be stated simply** — BHAG tracked visibly, core customer in ≤25 words, 3 brand promises with weekly KPIs, elevator pitch.
9. **Every employee can answer "did I have a great week?"** — 1–2 personal KPIs, a personal Critical Number, 3–5 personal quarterly priorities.
10. **Plans and performance are visible everywhere** — a situation room (physical or virtual), scoreboards, values/purpose/priorities posted. (Gung Ho! Pizza posts identical dashboards at every location so line staff can discuss company purpose.)

**Implementation reality:** roll out 1–2 habits per quarter after Habit #1. Expect 24–36 months for full adoption and another 2–3 years to mastery. Claimed reach: 70,000+ companies.

## 5. EOS / Traction (Gino Wickman)

The Entrepreneurial Operating System, from *Traction* (2011). Simpler and more prescriptive than Scaling Up; used by 250,000+ businesses. Philosophy: strengthen **Six Key Components**, in order, and most "drama" disappears.

### The Six Components

1. **Vision** — everyone agrees on where the company is going and how. Tool: **V/TO (Vision/Traction Organizer)** — a two-page plan answering 8 questions: core values, core focus, 10-year target, marketing strategy, 3-year picture, 1-year plan, quarterly rocks, issues list.
2. **People** — "right people" (fit the core values) in the "right seats" (fit the role). Tools: **People Analyzer** (values fit) and **GWC** — does the person *Get it, Want it, have Capacity* for the seat? The **Accountability Chart** (not an org chart) defines seats by the 5 roles each owns.
3. **Data** — run the business on numbers, not opinions or feelings. Tool: **Scorecard** — 5–15 weekly activity-based numbers, each owned by exactly one human. "Shared responsibility is a polite way of saying no responsibility."
4. **Issues** — surface problems openly and kill them permanently with **IDS**: Identify (root cause, not symptom), Discuss (once, openly), Solve (decision + owner + date).
5. **Process** — document the "core 20%" of each major process (the 20% that produces 80% of the outcome) and get it "Followed By All." Not SOP-mania — high-altitude checklists.
6. **Traction** — discipline and accountability: **Rocks** (3–7 quarterly priorities per person/team, crystal-clear finish line) and the **Level 10 Meeting** — same day, same time, 90 minutes weekly: scorecard review, rock check, headlines, to-do list review, then the bulk of time on IDS.

### The Visionary / Integrator split

EOS's signature org concept: the **Visionary** (usually the founder — ideas, big relationships, culture, R&D) is paired with an **Integrator** (runs day-to-day, harmonizes the leadership team, executes the business plan). Most founder-led companies fail to scale because the founder is trying to be both.

### Why EOS implementations fail (and the fixes)

Reported figure: **fewer than 30% of companies starting EOS reach full traction within 18 months.** The recurring failure modes:

1. **Stuck at the executive level** — the system never cascades. Fix: Level 10 meetings at every leadership tier, not just the top table.
2. **"80% done" rocks accepted as done** — perpetual near-completion. Fix: 80% is a checkpoint; remaining work due within a week.
3. **Wrong Integrator** — a weak Integrator's gaps get misattributed to everyone else for quarters. Fix: rigorous hiring/validation of the seat, scorecard from day one.
4. **Visionary won't actually let go** — identity loss disguised as quality control. Fix: define the Visionary's *future* role in writing before the handoff.
5. **Exec resistance to the Integrator-centric structure** — passive obstruction. Fix: earn buy-in or change the team; a resistant leadership team nullifies the system.
6. **Vague rocks and shared scorecard numbers** — if you can't tell when it's done, it's not a rock; if two people own a number, nobody does.
7. **IDS theater** — the same issue reappearing week after week means you're discussing, not solving.

## 6. Choosing an Operating System (the decision Carl actually gets asked)

### Quick positioning

| | **EOS** | **Scaling Up** | **4DX** | **OKRs** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| What it is | Complete, simple BOS | Complete, heavier BOS | Execution framework only | Goal framework only |
| Sweet spot | ~10–250 employees; $1M–$20M; chaos/firefighting | ~30–250+ employees; genuine growth ambition; strategy/cash problems | A team needing focus on 1 wildly important goal | Orgs already aligned needing goal cascade |
| Strategy depth | Light (core focus, 10-yr target) | Deep (7 Strata, differentiation, X-factor) | None | None |
| Cash discipline | Essentially absent | One of four core decisions | None | None |
| Meeting rhythm | Weekly Level 10 | Daily huddle + weekly + monthly + quarterly + annual | Weekly WIG session | Varies |
| Implementation | Franchise model — EOS Implementers follow a fixed process, ~$3–5K/session (up to $10K), ~10 sessions over 2 years | Certified coaches, more flexible; workshops ~$400–600/exec up to $20K–$250K/yr for private coaching | Book/self-serve or FranklinCovey | Self-serve/consultants |
| Failure mode | Execution-tidy but strategically adrift; founder still the answer to everything | "Expensive wallpaper" if the team lacks capacity for the heavier toolkit | Solves focus, leaves vision/people/process untouched | Cascading spreadsheets nobody believes |

### Decision rules

**Choose EOS when:**
- Leadership can't hold a consistent weekly meeting or name the quarter's priorities
- Processes are undocumented, ownership unclear, everyone firefighting
- The team needs basic alignment before strategic sophistication would even land
- Team is small (≤20–30 people) — simplicity wins

**Choose Scaling Up when:**
- Everyone can recite priorities yet the business still feels stuck (that's a strategy, people, or cash problem, not an execution problem)
- Growth is *tightening* cash rather than generating it
- The founder is still the answer to every hard decision — Scaling Up explicitly attacks founder dependency; EOS can coexist with it
- Approaching or past ~50 employees with management depth to run a heavier toolkit

**The meta-rule (most important):** commitment beats selection. *A fully implemented EOS outperforms a half-implemented Scaling Up every time, and vice versa.* Pick one, run it faithfully for at least four quarters, then iterate. And the timing tell: "You do not choose a Business Operating System when everything is calm. You choose one when the business has started to outrun the way you manage it."

**Note for a company like Mayday Media (small creator-led team + a physical facility being built):** EOS-grade simplicity fits the current team size, but the *cash* and *strategy* modules of Scaling Up should be bolted on early — a facility buildout is exactly the kind of capital-hungry, long cash-conversion-cycle move EOS gives you no tools for. A pragmatic hybrid: EOS meeting/accountability mechanics + Scaling Up's cash conversion cycle and 7 Strata thinking per business line.

### 90-day upgrade path (EOS → Scaling Up), when a client outgrows EOS

- **Days 1–30: Diagnose the ceiling.** Keep Level 10s and working KPIs. Test: does the founder still approve decisions that should sit elsewhere? Is strategy trapped in the founder's head?
- **Days 31–60: Translate the OS.** Rocks → outcome-based OKRs/priorities; V/TO conversations → OPSP; add daily huddles where speed matters.
- **Days 61–90: Add strategy + cash discipline.** Make explicit customer-focus and differentiation choices; redesign pricing, collections, margins, and hiring to fund growth internally.

## 7. Stall Points by Revenue: What Breaks at Each Stage

### $0 → $1M — "Prove someone will pay"
- Founder does everything; the only questions that matter are market validation and cash survival (McKeown's Early Struggle).
- ~86% of US small businesses are solopreneurs; the first stall is often refusing to hire at all.
- Milestone that matters: a sellable offer plus early advocates who *generalize* to a bigger market (not just friends-of-the-founder buyers).

### ~$1M — the first wall: founder-as-sales-system
- Below $1M the founder can personally touch every major deal. At $1M they can't — and deals stall in exactly the conversations the founder no longer has time for.
- The pattern: pipeline, sales process, and operations were all built for $500K, and all three break at $1M simultaneously.

### $1M → $3M — "systems problem, not a talent problem" (DeltaPoint's 8 breakdowns)
1. Owner remains the revenue bottleneck (relationships, prospecting, closing all route through one person)
2. No structured business development — referrals and reactive networking instead of a predictable cadence of new conversations
3. Conflating business development (creates opportunities) with sales (converts them) — expecting sales results without investing in opportunity creation
4. Hiring salespeople into broken systems — new hires depend on leads, messaging, and process that don't exist, then "fail"
5. Marketing activity disconnected from revenue — effort without integration
6. No visibility/metrics — leadership can't answer basic questions about conversation volume, qualification rate, conversion by stage
7. Operational complexity eats the owner's growth time — more clients, more delivery, less selling
8. Delayed-investment mindset — waiting for growth before building systems, when "growth is the result of investment, not the prerequisite"

**Fix pattern:** a consistent prospecting engine (outbound cadence + multi-touch follow-up), defined role separation (BD / sales / marketing), and measured pipeline stages with weekly activity tracking.

### $3M → $10M — the valley of death
- ~60% of companies get stuck here; headcount typically hits 25–75 by $5–10M, which forces the first real management layer.
- Notion Capital's Build phase: $3–10M is where a repeatable GTM *emerges* (specialists hired, playbook drafted); $10–30M is where it *hardens* (forecast variance under 10–15%).
- "Founder privilege erodes at $10M" — customers, hires, and partners stop giving you scrappy-startup grace.
- The three psychological traps (Pham): the **success paradox** (what got you here won't get you there), the **identity crisis** (ego attachment, survivor bias, fear of losing control → "founder drag": micromanagement, reactivity, firefighting), and the **delegation disaster** — founders hire **echoes** (people who think like them) instead of **executors** (systematic thinkers who challenge assumptions).
- Prescription at this stage: accept the missing systems without defensiveness; design workflows for 2x/3x/10x volume; install true executors and *don't* re-inject founder logic into their methods; reorganize around customer value streams instead of departmental silos; reframe leadership as designing systems that execute rather than executing.

### $10M+ — the machine phase (context, mostly beyond Carl's clients today)
- Leaders of leaders, multiple management layers, owned distribution, inorganic growth options, enterprise-grade systems, pipeline visibility quarters ahead.
- Growth persistence data (Bessemer): ~70% of growth rate persists year-over-year on average, but with very little actual correlation — a good year can follow a bad one and vice versa. Don't extrapolate one year in either direction.

## 8. Founder Role Evolution

The single thread running through every framework: **the company cannot outgrow the founder's willingness to change jobs.** Either the founder makes the identity shift or hardwires the ceiling into the business.

### The three-job arc

| Stage | Founder's actual job | Measured by | What must be delegated |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Doer** (0 → ~$1M, 1–10 people) | Product, sales, ops, culture — everything | Effort and output | Almost nothing yet — but start documenting |
| **Manager / player-coach** (~$1M–$5M, 10–30 people) | Building and running a leadership layer; still deep in product/vision | Team output | Operational execution; most sales conversations |
| **Architect / CEO** ($5M+, 30+ people) | Strategy, capital, key relationships, hiring/retaining leaders, designing the system | Direction, not effort | Operational *decisions*, not just tasks |

- **The delegation ceiling hits at 7–10 employees** — the point where informal, founder-centric decision-making collapses (matches Greiner's leadership crisis almost exactly).
- The shift is "from doer to designer, from solo act to conductor" — the founder is measured by effort, the CEO by direction.
- The core blocker is identity: founders experience roles as *who they are*, not what they do, which makes relinquishing them feel like self-erasure. Handle it as a grief/identity conversation, not a time-management one.
- Practical sequencing for creator businesses specifically: **delegate operations first, creative last.** Most creator businesses don't break because the founder is too involved in the product — they break because the founder gets pulled *away* from the product into inbox management, brand-deal chasing, and coordination. The first hires should buy back creative time, not replace creative judgment. (Directly relevant to a YouTube-first founder: the "product" is the founder's taste and on-camera presence; systematize everything around it.)
- The "founder mode" corrective (Graham/Chesky discourse, 2024): delegation dogma can overshoot — founders who go fully hands-off through layers of professional managers can lose the plot. The synthesis: delegate *execution* aggressively, stay engaged with *the details that define quality* via skip-level visibility. This is compatible with, not opposed to, the frameworks above — FACe/Accountability Charts define who *decides*; they don't forbid the founder from looking.
- Creator-economy version of the architect move: the businesses that scale cleanly treat themselves as **franchises** — owned IP, repeatable formats, sub-brands with their own audience equity, scalable production processes — rather than as one person's calendar.

## 9. Playbooks

### Playbook A: Install a minimum viable operating system (any small company, ~90 days)

1. **Week 1–2:** Leadership alignment session. Core values (discover, don't invent), core focus, 3-year picture, 1-year plan. One page. (V/TO or OPSP-lite.)
2. **Week 2:** Build the Accountability Chart / FACe. Every function one name; every scorecard number one name. Expect this to surface 2–3 awkward conversations — that's the point.
3. **Week 3:** Stand up the weekly 90-minute meeting (Level 10 format: scorecard, rocks, headlines, to-dos, then majority of time on IDS). Same day, same time, no exceptions.
4. **Week 3–4:** Build a 5–15 number weekly scorecard of *activity* metrics (leading), not just results (lagging).
5. **Week 4:** Set 3–5 company rocks for the quarter, each with a binary finish line and one owner. Pick ONE Critical Number.
6. **Month 2–3:** Add the daily huddle if coordination speed is a pain point. Document the core 20% of the 3 most important processes. Start weekly customer + employee feedback questions.
7. **End of quarter:** Quarterly session — grade rocks honestly (done/not done, no 80% credit), reset, and only now consider adding sophistication (cash levers, 7 Strata, themes).

### Playbook B: Break the $1M–$3M sales plateau

1. Map the current revenue engine honestly: what % of closed revenue had the founder in the room? (Usually >80%.)
2. Separate the three functions on paper first: BD (creates conversations), Sales (converts), Marketing (feeds both). Even if one person wears two hats, the *numbers* stay separate.
3. Build the prospecting cadence before hiring: target list, outbound rhythm, multi-touch follow-up, weekly activity quotas.
4. Instrument the pipeline: stages, conversion rates per stage, weekly review in the L10/weekly meeting.
5. Only THEN hire the first salesperson — into a working system, with the founder's process documented as the playbook, and 90-day scorecard expectations.
6. Founder's new sales role: late-stage appearances on big deals only, plus pipeline coaching.

### Playbook C: De-bottleneck the founder (any stage)

1. **Decision audit (2 weeks):** log every decision routed to the founder. Bucket: only-founder-can (strategy, key hires, brand), founder-by-habit, founder-by-vacuum.
2. Kill the vacuum bucket with the Accountability Chart; kill the habit bucket with explicit decision rights + spending/approval thresholds.
3. Hire or promote an Integrator/ops lead for day-to-day harmonization. Validate with a scorecard from day one — a weak Integrator's gaps get blamed on everyone else for quarters before detection.
4. Write the founder's *future* role BEFORE the handoff (Visionary job description) — the #1 predictor of the handoff sticking.
5. Watch for boomerang behavior: founder re-entering delegated decisions under stress. Name it in the weekly meeting; it's a pattern, not an event.

## 10. Common Mistakes

1. **Framework tourism.** Sampling EOS for two quarters, then OKRs, then Scaling Up. Every switch resets trust. Four-quarter minimum commitment before judging.
2. **Solving a stage-N crisis with stage-N-1 tools.** More founder control to fix an autonomy crisis; more hustle to fix a systems gap; more process to fix a strategy vacuum.
3. **Hiring into broken systems.** Salespeople without pipeline, ops managers without decision rights, an Integrator the founder overrides daily. The hire "fails" and the founder concludes delegation doesn't work.
4. **Hiring echoes instead of executors.** Recruiting people who think like the founder feels safe and reproduces the ceiling.
5. **Shared accountability.** Two names on a function or metric = zero names. The FACe/Accountability Chart conversation is uncomfortable precisely because it's load-bearing.
6. **Accepting 80% rock completion as done.** Perpetual near-completion trains the team that finish lines are decorative.
7. **OS stuck at the top table.** The leadership team runs Level 10s while the rest of the company never hears about rocks or scorecards. Cascade or decay.
8. **Execution tidiness masking strategy weakness.** Great meetings, clear rocks, everyone busy — wrong direction. EOS's known blind spot; check differentiation and cash annually no matter the OS.
9. **Ignoring cash while growing.** Growth consumes working capital; a company can grow itself to death. Model the cash conversion cycle *before* the growth push — doubly true for physical-facility businesses with buildout capex and slow ramp.
10. **Waiting for growth to fund systems.** Causality runs the other way: systems investment precedes growth.
11. **Over-processing past the peak.** McKeown's Treadmill: when process compliance becomes the culture's scorecard, the creative people leave first. Especially fatal in creative/media companies.
12. **Treating founder transition as a skills problem.** It's an identity problem. Coaching, explicit future-role design, and gradual scope handoff work; exhortations to "delegate more" don't.

## 11. Questions Carl Should Ask

**Diagnosing stage & stall:**
- What revenue band are you in, and how long have you been there? (>18 months flat = stall, not pause.)
- What percentage of closed revenue in the last 12 months had you personally in the deal?
- If you disappeared for 30 days, what breaks first? In what order?
- Which of your current systems were built when the company was half this size?

**Operating system readiness:**
- Can every person on the leadership team name the company's #1 priority this quarter? (Ask them separately.)
- Do you have a weekly leadership meeting with the same agenda, same time, every week? What % of it is status vs. actually solving issues?
- For each major function — who is the ONE accountable person? (Watch for two names or a pause.)
- What are the 5–10 numbers you look at weekly? Are they activities (leading) or results (lagging)?
- Show me an issue that's appeared in three consecutive meetings. Why is it still alive?

**Founder role:**
- What decisions came to you this week that shouldn't have? Why did they?
- Which parts of your current job would you defend even if the business didn't need you to do them? (Identity detector.)
- Are your recent key hires people who think like you or people who challenge how you think?
- What's your role 3 years from now, in writing?

**Cash & growth capacity:**
- If revenue doubled next quarter, does your bank balance go up or down over the following 6 months?
- What's your cash conversion cycle — days from dollar-out to dollar-back? (For a facility: what's the ramp curve to breakeven utilization, and what funds the gap?)
- Which of the 7 levers (price, volume, COGS, opex, AR, inventory, AP) have you touched in the last year?

**Multi-business complexity (relevant to media company + facility):**
- Are these one company or two on your P&L, your org chart, and your calendar? (They should match.)
- Does each business line have its own core customer (≤25 words), own scorecard, and own accountable operator — or does everything route through you?
