# Sports Training Facility Economics & Operations

## TL;DR

- **This is a real-estate-utilization business wearing a baseball hat.** The core metric is revenue per square foot per year ($30–80/sq ft is the realistic band for indoor training space; the number one killer is empty cage-hours 9am–4pm on weekdays.
- **Recurring revenue or death.** Hourly cage rentals alone almost never carry the lease. The winning stack is memberships + lesson programs + team/league contracts + camps/clinics, with rentals as filler. Teams/seasonal programs retain at 87% over a year; prepaid credit packs lose a third of members in 90 days — sell commitment, not credits.
- **Benchmark unit economics:** a well-run 8-cage indoor facility does ~$250K–$600K/yr revenue at 10–30% net margin; D-BAT franchises (the category leader, ~110–150 locations) require $536K–$1.03M all-in to open and are estimated around $800K–$1M gross per unit with a 6–8 year payback.
- **CapEx sanity check for a warehouse conversion:** turf $1.15–$9/sq ft, cages ~$1K–$10K each installed, HitTrax <$10K/unit, TrackMan ~$19K–$30K, Rapsodo ~$3K. A credible 8,000–15,000 sq ft buildout in an existing shell runs roughly $150K–$500K, not counting lease deposits and working capital. Ground-up construction is $150–$250/sq ft — avoid it.
- **Demand is strong and price-insensitive at the top.** U.S. families spend $40B+/yr on youth sports; average baseball family spending is up 68% since 2019; only 0.6% of facility membership churn cites price. The constraint is share of the family's schedule, not their wallet.
- **Coach comp is the margin lever and the legal landmine.** Standard lesson splits are 50–70% to the coach; misclassifying coaches as 1099 contractors while controlling their schedule and curriculum is the most common legal exposure in the industry (W-2 costs ~10–12% more and is usually worth it).
- **Seasonality is inverted vs. baseball itself:** indoor facilities earn 60–70% of revenue November–March. Anything that flattens summer (camps, adult leagues, other sports, remote programming) is disproportionately valuable.
- **For Neptune Performance specifically:** the differentiated, tech-forward "development lab" model (Driveline-style assessment + programming) commands 3–10x the price per athlete of a commodity cage barn, and pairs naturally with a media company that can generate demand for free.

## 1. The business you're actually in

Three mental models, in order of importance:

1. **Real estate utilization.** You lease (or own) a large box and resell it in time-slices. Every economic decision reduces to: what does one cage-hour (or sq-ft-hour) sell for, and how many of the ~5,000 annual open hours are sold? Facilities on manual scheduling typically run 60–70% utilization even in peak season; the best run 85–90% in prime windows. The delta between those two numbers is most of the profit.
2. **A recurring-revenue services business.** Memberships and committed programs (teams, semester-long lesson blocks, seasonal training groups) are what make cash flow survivable through the summer trough. Median facility membership revenue grows from ~4% of total revenue at month 3 to ~15% by end of year one — it takes deliberate effort to build, and most operators never do.
3. **A talent marketplace.** Lessons are the highest-revenue-per-hour product, but the facility doesn't produce them — coaches do. Your real product is the pipeline of students you hand to coaches, and your margin is the spread you can defend (typically 20–50%).

Failure mode summary (Upper Hand, Sports Facilities Advisory, CageList): facilities fail from (a) no strategic plan / no defined market, (b) poor financial control — mispriced offerings, untracked expenses, (c) empty daytime hours, (d) weak marketing and no retention system, (e) the "travel team pivot" — bleeding facilities pivot to running many travel teams for volume, which dilutes coaching quality and turns a development brand into a churn machine, (f) deferred maintenance driving families away, (g) misreading the local market (building what the owner wanted as a player, not what parents buy).

## 2. Market context (why now)

- U.S. families spend **$40B+/yr on youth sports** — roughly 2x NFL revenue. Global youth sports market ~$57B (2025) growing ~10%/yr.
- Average family spend on a child's **primary sport: $1,016/yr (2024)**, up 46% since 2019 (2x inflation). All sports: ~$1,500/child/yr. **Baseball was the most expensive of the big three sports in 2024** and is up 68% since 2019. Baseball parents spend most on registration, travel/lodging, lessons/instruction, and camps.
- Travel baseball families spend an estimated **$3,000–$5,000/yr on travel and lodging alone**.
- Income skew is severe: $100K+ households spend ~$1,471/yr more than <$50K households on the primary sport. A premium training facility is fundamentally selling to the top two income quintiles — site selection should follow household income and travel-team density, not general population.
- NIL and college scholarship psychology have raised parents' willingness to pay for "development" framed as an investment. This favors assessment/data-driven positioning over "come hit in a cage."
- 2025 was a breakout M&A year for youth sports (Sportico) — private equity is rolling up facilities, leagues, and tournament operators. Implication: well-run independent facilities have an exit path; badly run ones will compete against capitalized consolidators.
- Participation caveat: 54.6% of kids played organized sports in 2022–23, still below 2019 (56.1%). The market is growing in **dollars per participant**, not participants. Premium > volume.

## 3. Revenue architecture

### 3.1 The five streams (and how to weight them)

| Stream | Typical price points | Character | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cage/space rentals | $30–$60/hr per cage; $200–$500 for 90–120 min group events | Spot revenue, walk-in, zero commitment | Filler — never the foundation |
| Memberships | $80–$150/mo median (region-dependent); tiers up to ~2.7x base | Recurring, smooths seasonality | Foundation |
| Lessons (private/semi-private) | $70–$140/hr; $50–$75 per 30 min; packs discounted 10–25% | Highest $/hr, coach-dependent | Profit engine |
| Teams, leagues, camps, clinics | $200–$400/team/season (adult league); camps $150–$400/wk/kid; travel team fees $1,500–$4,000/yr | Bulk, committed, prepaid | Cash-flow anchor + retention |
| Retail / pro shop / vending | Varies; D-BAT budgets $33K–$40K opening inventory | Low margin, high convenience value | Ancillary (skip in v1 unless franchised) |

Benchmarks:
- Well-managed 8-cage indoor facility: **$250K–$600K annual revenue**, net margins **15–30%** at the high end of operational quality; the broader indoor-sports average is 10–20% net.
- Revenue per sq ft: a 10,000 sq ft facility at $500K = $50/sq ft. Treat **<$30/sq ft as a red flag, $50 as solid, $80+ as excellent** for training-focused (non-event) facilities.
- 3–4 concurrent adult rec leagues yield **$7,200–$19,200 per season** in predictable revenue and fill off-peak slots.
- Private lessons: certified coaches bill $60–$100+/hr; a facility keeping ~50% nets ~$35–$50 per lesson-hour against a cage-rental alternative of $30–$60 for the whole cage — lessons roughly **double revenue per cage-hour** with better retention.

### 3.2 Membership design (Baseline 2026 report — 44,279 memberships, $19.8M in transactions, 44 states)

The single best benchmark dataset in the niche. Key numbers:

**Pricing**
- Median monthly price by region: West $135, Northeast $100, South $100, Midwest $80. State medians span ~$41–$249.
- Median new-member price rose $120/mo (2024) → $150/mo (2025–26) — driven entirely by new plans/new facilities; the median facility **never reprices existing plans** (0% YoY). Lesson: grandfather old members, raise rack rate on new plans annually.
- Median monthly-equivalent by model: recurring dues $84, unlimited access $100, credit/prepaid packs $200, teams/seasonal programs $229.
- Tier ladder: top tier ≈ **2.69x** the bottom tier, with ~**17% steps** between tiers. Member distribution is a barbell: 52% bottom tier, 22% middle, 27% top. Build a real top tier — a quarter of members will take it.

**Retention (1-year)**
- Teams/seasonal programs: **87%**
- Annual billing: **82%**
- Unlimited access: 45%; recurring dues: 42%; monthly billing overall: 35%; **credit/prepaid packs: 32%** (and only 67% survive 90 days)
- Cancel-lock/contract plans: 46% vs 31% without.
- Annual members deliver **1.9x more paid months over 2 years** (20.1 vs 10.4). When an annual option is offered at a median 8% discount, 25.2% of members take it. Always offer annual.

**Churn mechanics**
- Median tenure of a lost member: **87 days**. 53% of preventable churn happens in the first 90 days; 79% within 6 months. Onboarding (first-session experience, coach assignment, 30/60/90 touchpoints) is the highest-ROI retention work.
- Churn reasons: program/season ended 36%, no reason recorded 20%, sport-season conflict 11%, **price 0.6%**. You are almost never losing people on price — you're losing them to the calendar. Counter-programming for the off-season (strength, other sports, arm-care blocks) directly attacks the top churn causes.
- Seasonality of joins (index, 100 = avg month): **August 372** (peak), Oct–Nov 144–149 (secondary), Mar–Apr 34 (trough). Cancellations peak in July (197). Membership *revenue* only swings ±18% despite 10x join swings — recurring billing is the shock absorber.

**Growth expectations for a new membership program**
- Median: ~12 active members at month 1, ~40 at month 12, ~$2,934 MRR at year one. 75th percentile: 100+ members at 12 months. Set expectations accordingly: memberships compound slowly; don't judge the program at month 4.

### 3.3 Lesson pricing reference points (2025–26)

- National range: $50–$75 per 30 min; **$80–$140 per hour**.
- The Battery: $70/45min, $90/60min private; semi-private (2 players) $80/45min, $100/60min.
- Future Prospects: age 7–12 $115/lesson (10-pack $1,000); 13+ $125/lesson (10-pack $1,100). Note pack discounts of only ~10–13% — you don't need 25% off to sell packs.
- Ex-pro or named-brand instructors command premiums well above these ranges. A former MLB player's name supports $150–$250/hr in most metros.
- Semi-private (2–4 athletes) is the margin sweet spot: ~70% of private price per athlete, 1.4–2.5x revenue per coach-hour, and kids often prefer it.

## 4. CapEx & buildout (warehouse-conversion playbook)

### 4.1 Space acquisition

- Lease an existing industrial/warehouse shell with **≥16 ft clear height** (20+ ft for full infield work / fly balls); never build ground-up in v1 (ground-up runs $150–$250/sq ft → $1.5M–$2.5M for 10,000 sq ft).
- Industrial lease rates vary ~4x by metro: ~$8/sq ft/yr (Atlanta-type markets) to ~$30/sq ft/yr (NYC). Typical operator budget: **$5,000–$15,000/mo**. Rent is the largest fixed cost — every $1/sq ft of rent needs ~$1.5–2/sq ft of revenue to stay healthy.
- Zoning/permitting/compliance: $0.15–$0.50/sq ft; confirm "indoor recreation" use is permitted before signing. Check parking minimums — evening peaks concentrate 30–60 cars at once.

### 4.2 Interior buildout line items

| Item | Cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Turf | $1.15–$1.69/sq ft budget; $7–$9/sq ft premium padded; ~$17K+ installed for 10K sq ft | Padded turf matters for ground balls & agility work |
| Batting cage (frame + net) | Netting $600–$2,400 (#36 nylon lasts 3–5 yrs); frames $500–$1K (PVC) to $2K–$10K (steel); ~$9K–$10K+ per cage fully installed with retractable systems | 55'x12' or 70'x12' standard; retractable curtains buy flexibility |
| Pitching machines | ~$3K entry (Hack Attack, Spinball); $15K+ programmable (iPitch); auto-feeders ~double the base price | 1 machine per 2 cages is fine at start |
| HVAC / lighting / flooring specialty work | $100K–$250K if the shell needs it; climate control $5K–$15K incremental | The silent budget killer — inspect the shell's mechanicals hard |
| Cage automation/token systems | ~$10K/cage | Only for unstaffed/commodity models |
| Renovation cage package (existing structure) | $20K–$50K for cage systems alone | |

### 4.3 Technology stack (the differentiator)

| Tool | Cost | What it earns |
|---|---|---|
| Rapsodo (hitting or pitching) | ~$3,000/unit + cloud subscription | Entry ball-flight data; fine for lessons |
| HitTrax | <$10,000/unit + annual license | The proven facility workhorse: gamified hitting leagues, data reports, birthday-party mode. Pays back via premium cage pricing (+$10–$25/hr), HitTrax leagues, and upgraded membership tiers |
| TrackMan (indoor B1/practice) | ~$18,995 indoor, $24,995 outdoor, ~$30K MSRP full | Pro-grade credibility; justified only for a true development-lab positioning |
| Blast/Diamond Kinetics bat sensors | ~$150–$300 each | Cheap, portable; bundle into assessment packages |
| Force plates, motion capture, Edgertronic | $5K–$50K+ | Driveline-tier lab; phase 2+ |
| Booking/facility software | $100–$500/mo | Non-negotiable: online booking cuts no-shows from 15–20% to ≤5% with deposits + automated reminders |

Recoup pattern that works: put tech access in the **top membership tier** (remember, 27% of members buy the top tier) and price assessments as standalone products ($150–$400 per assessment).

### 4.4 Total startup envelopes

- **6-cage lean cage business** (rentals + lessons in an existing shell): **$150K–$250K**.
- **8–15K sq ft training academy** (turf areas, 6–10 cages, HitTrax x2, strength corner, office/lobby): **$300K–$600K** all-in including deposits and 6 months working capital.
- **D-BAT-spec franchise**: **$536K–$1.03M** (see §6).
- Rule of thumb: hold back **6 months of fixed costs** as working capital; break-even commonly takes 12–36 months, and August (the join peak) may be 10 months from your opening date.

## 5. Operations

### 5.1 Utilization & scheduling (the profit variable)

- Prime time = **weekdays 4–9pm + weekends**. Target ≥70% paid utilization in prime slots before worrying about anything else; the healthy overall band is 60–85%.
- 70–80% of revenue arrives in peak hours/seasons. Facilities that moved from manual to modern scheduling report 20–30% revenue lifts purely from gap-elimination and dynamic pricing.
- Off-peak plays, in rough order of value: (1) homeschool/PE programs and daytime pro/college guys (winter), (2) adult leagues and corporate events (evenings shoulder), (3) senior/toddler programs, (4) subleasing daytime blocks to other trainers (physical therapy, speed schools), (5) birthday parties (weekend daytime — HitTrax party mode is the industry's best-kept margin secret).
- **Dynamic pricing**: premium prime-time rates (+20–40%) and discounted dead hours (−30–50%) — customers accept it readily; software automates it.
- No-show control: require card-on-file or deposits for bookings, automated reminders; this is worth 10–15 points of realized utilization by itself.

### 5.2 Seasonality (plan the year around this)

- Indoor facilities in cold climates: **60–70% of revenue Nov–Mar**. Traffic craters when outdoor ball starts.
- Counter-seasonal levers: summer camps (the single biggest one — school's out and parents need childcare-with-development), summer memberships pre-sold in spring at a discount, adult leagues, other-sport rentals (softball obvious; also golf sims, lacrosse, cricket in some metros), remote/hybrid programming that keeps billing alive while athletes play outside.
- Sell **annual commitments during the Nov–Mar demand peak** so the obligation carries members through summer. August is the #1 join month — run your biggest marketing push late July–August, second push October.

### 5.3 Coach compensation & classification

Compensation models (from gym/training industry practice):
- **Revenue split**: facility keeps 20–50%, coach keeps 50–80%. Most common: coach 70/facility 30 when coach brings the clients; closer to 50/50 when the facility supplies the client pipeline, booking, and marketing. Own the client relationship (booking, billing, CRM in facility software) or you're just a landlord.
- **Flat court fee**: coach pays $10–$25/lesson or a monthly rent for cage access, keeps everything. Simple; zero facility upside; coach owns the relationship — dangerous for enterprise value.
- **Hourly/salary W-2** ($18–$35/hr for cage staff & group coaches; $40K–$65K + incentives for a lead instructor/GM): needed for anyone running your programs, curriculum, and camps.
- Hybrid that works: W-2 base for facility shifts and group programs + split on private lessons booked through the facility + bonus on membership retention.

Classification (the landmine): the IRS and state agencies increasingly treat coaches as **employees** when the facility sets their schedule, dictates curriculum, reviews performance, or is their primary income source. Misclassification exposure = back payroll taxes, workers' comp penalties, unemployment insurance, lawsuits. W-2 costs roughly **10–12% more** than 1099 — pay it for anyone who is functionally staff. Legit 1099s: independent instructors with their own clientele, own insurance, multiple facilities, who rent your space.

Also: every coach needs background checks (SafeSport-style), and the facility needs abuse-prevention policies — one incident is existential in youth sports.

### 5.4 Staffing pattern for a ~10K sq ft academy

- Owner/GM (often player-facing early), 1 front-desk/ops per open shift, 3–8 part-time or split-comp instructors, 1 marketing person (part-time or agency) — total payroll typically 25–35% of revenue at maturity.
- Insurance: general liability + participant accident + abuse/molestation coverage; budget $5K–$15K/yr depending on size and programming.

## 6. Case studies & competitive landscape

### 6.1 D-BAT (the franchise benchmark)

- Founded 1998 (Dallas); the dominant baseball/softball academy franchise: ~109–153 locations across 37+ states (sources vary by date), heavy South/Texas concentration, stated goal of 250 locations in 5 years. 38 new leases signed in Dec 2025 alone.
- **Economics**: franchise fee $45K; total investment **$536K–$1.03M** (leasehold improvements $100K–$375K; facility buildout $213K–$330K; pro shop inventory $33K–$40K; signage $17K–$21K; computers $15K–$20K). Royalty **8%** + **2.5%** ad fund. No Item 19 (they don't publish unit revenue), but third-party estimates put average unit revenue around **$796K–$1.04M** with estimated owner earnings **~$125K–$156K** → margin ~12–15% and payback **6.1–8.1 years**.
- SBA data: 131 loans, average $780K, **default rate 3.1%** (4 of 131) — remarkably low for recreation; the model works when followed.
- Model mix: cage rentals + memberships + lessons + camps/clinics + a real pro shop (D-BAT is also an equipment brand — retail is core to them, unusual for the category). Typical footprint ~15K sq ft (e.g., 14,669 sq ft Gainesville FL).
- Takeaway for an independent: D-BAT proves ~$1M gross is achievable on a 15K sq ft box, but a third of the economics get eaten by royalties+ad fund+corporate spec. An independent with equivalent operational discipline keeps that 10.5%.

### 6.2 Driveline Baseball (the development-lab benchmark)

- The category-defining "data-driven development" business: gyms in Kent WA (40K sq ft), Scottsdale AZ, Tampa FL (20K sq ft); 1,000+ pro clients.
- Product ladder: in-gym assessment + training (**12-month in-gym program >$9,000**), remote training subscriptions (coach-reviewed programming after athletes leave the gym — the retention engine), DrivelinePLUS content membership for coaches/players, certifications/courses for coaches, plus hardware (PlyoCare balls etc.) and open R&D content that functions as marketing.
- Strategic lessons: (1) assessment-first funnel — every athlete starts with a paid assessment that generates the training plan and the upsell; (2) the gym is the flagship, but **remote programming scales revenue past the walls**; (3) publishing research/content built the brand at near-zero CAC — directly transferable to a media company running a facility; (4) premium pricing prices out the middle — Driveline concedes the $80/mo family market and owns the $500+/mo committed-athlete market.

### 6.3 Other reference points

- **Extra Innings**: older franchise, 13K–18K+ sq ft facilities, cages + instruction + pro shop; less growth energy than D-BAT.
- **Non-franchise local academies** (The Battery, Future Prospects, Hunter Pence Academy, etc.): compete on named instructors and lesson quality; pricing in §3.3.
- **Washington Heights NYC datapoint**: 4,500 sq ft fully-turfed indoor facility built for **$382K** — a real-world urban buildout comp (~$85/sq ft conversion cost).
- **CageList / sharing-economy angle**: marketplaces now let facilities (and private cage owners) rent idle cage time — a channel for off-peak fill, and a signal that commodity cage rental is being disintermediated. Don't build a business whose core product is the thing being commoditized.
- **PE roll-ups (2025–26)**: Unrivaled/TSFC-style consolidators are acquiring facility groups. Multiples favor recurring-revenue-heavy P&Ls; a facility that's 60% membership/program revenue is worth structurally more per EBITDA dollar than a rental barn.

## 7. Local competition analysis playbook

1. **Inventory the supply**: every cage facility, academy, D-BAT/Extra Innings, college/HS facility with public hours, and private instructors within a 20–25 minute drive (that's the real trade radius for weekly training; families won't drive farther more than ~1x/week).
2. **Mystery-shop each**: price sheet, prime-time availability 2 weeks out (a fully-booked competitor = unmet demand), cleanliness, tech, coach bios, membership options. Book a lesson at the top two.
3. **Count the demand**: number of travel organizations, Little League/PONY registrations, HS programs within radius; median household income by zip (target $90K+ concentrations); youth population 7–18.
4. **Capacity math**: (competitor cages × prime hours × observed utilization) vs. (estimated serious players × 1–2 sessions/wk). A market is open when demand exceeds prime-time supply by ≥30%, or when supply is all commodity (no data/assessment offering).
5. **Positioning decision**: never open a "slightly nicer cage barn" next to an incumbent. Enter with a wedge: technology/assessment lab, a named coach, a specific age/segment (youth-only, pitching-only, softball), or a team-contract anchor (sign 2–3 travel orgs to winter training contracts *before* signing the lease).
6. **Anchor tenant rule**: pre-sell 20–30% of prime winter capacity via team contracts and founding memberships before buildout starts. If you can't pre-sell it, the market is telling you something.

## 8. Financial model skeleton (for a ~10K sq ft, 8-cage academy)

Assumptions to pressure-test with local data:

- **Revenue at maturity (yr 3)**: memberships 120 members × $110 avg = $158K; lessons 60 lesson-hrs/wk × $95 × 46 wks × 40% facility share = $105K; teams/camps/clinics = $120K; rentals/parties/leagues = $80K → **~$460K** (=$46/sq ft).
- **Costs**: rent $10K/mo = $120K; payroll (W-2 + splits already netted above for lessons) $120K; utilities/insurance/software $45K; marketing $25K; maintenance/misc $30K → ~$340K.
- **EBITDA ~ $120K (26%)** — top-quartile but achievable; median outcome is closer to 12–15%.
- Break-even ~$28K/mo revenue; expect to cross it month 10–20. Fund accordingly.
- Sensitivity: ±10 points of prime-time utilization ≈ ±$45–60K revenue at ~85% incremental margin. Utilization is the whole model.

## 9. Common mistakes

1. **Underestimating the shell** — signing a cheap lease then discovering $150K+ of HVAC/electrical/sprinkler work. Get a contractor walkthrough before LOI.
2. **Rental-first business model** — hourly cage rentals as the primary product; it can't cover fixed costs and it's the segment being commoditized by marketplaces and home cages.
3. **Selling credit packs instead of commitments** — prepaid session packs retain at 32%/yr vs 87% for seasonal programs. Packs feel easy to sell and quietly destroy LTV.
4. **The travel-team pivot done desperately** — layering on 6–10 travel teams to buy volume dilutes coaching, burns the brand, and converts the business into low-margin logistics. (One or two flagship teams as a funnel is different and fine.)
5. **Coach misclassification** — controlling contractors like employees. Also: letting star coaches own the client relationship, then losing 40 clients the day they leave. Contracts + facility-owned booking/CRM.
6. **No daytime strategy** — accepting that 9am–4pm is dead. That's ~40% of open hours at zero revenue.
7. **Ignoring onboarding** — 53% of churn happens in the first 90 days; most facilities spend 100% of marketing on acquisition and $0 on the first-month experience.
8. **Never raising prices** — the median facility reprices existing plans 0%/yr while new-member median rose 25% in two years. Raise rack rates annually on new plans; price is nearly irrelevant to churn (0.6%).
9. **Buying TrackMan before having a programming product** — $30K of radar with no assessment→training→reassessment loop is a demo toy. Tech follows methodology, not the reverse.
10. **Opening in April** — missing the Nov–Mar revenue engine and the August join peak means eating 6+ months of fixed costs before the market shows up. Target a Sept–Oct opening.
11. **No pre-sales** — signing the lease on hope instead of founding memberships + team contracts.
12. **Owner does everything** — owner-as-only-coach caps revenue at one person's hours and makes the business unsellable.

## 10. Questions Carl should ask (diagnostics)

**Market & positioning**
- What's the drive-time radius, and how many travel organizations / HS programs / registered youth players are inside it? What's median household income in the top 5 feeder zips?
- Who are the 3 nearest competitors, what do they charge, and how booked is their prime time two weeks out?
- What's the wedge — why does a family switch to you? ("Nicer" is not a wedge.)

**Model & pre-sales**
- What % of projected year-1 revenue is committed before lease signing (founding members, team contracts, camp pre-registrations)?
- What's the revenue mix target at maturity — and is recurring/committed revenue ≥50%?
- What does one prime cage-hour sell for in each product (rental vs lesson vs program), and which are you prioritizing?

**Space & CapEx**
- Clear height? Condition of HVAC/electrical/sprinklers? What did the contractor walkthrough estimate before you signed anything?
- Total CapEx vs 6 months of fixed costs held in reserve — is the working-capital buffer real?
- Lease terms: TI allowance? Personal guarantee? Options to expand/exit?

**Operations**
- Utilization by daypart — what's the plan for weekday 9am–4pm?
- Membership design: is there an annual option, a top tier at ~2.5–3x base, and a seasonal-program product? Or just monthly + punch cards?
- What happens in a member's first 30 days? Who owns onboarding?
- How are coaches paid, who owns the client relationship, and are the 1099s actually independent?
- What's the summer plan — camps, leagues, remote programming, or hope?

**Metrics discipline**
- Do they know, monthly: prime-time utilization %, MRR, 90-day new-member retention, revenue per sq ft run-rate, lesson-hours per week, CAC per member?

**For Neptune specifically**
- Is the positioning commodity cages or development lab? (The lab commands Driveline-adjacent pricing and matches the media flywheel — content creates demand, facility creates content.)
- Can Mayday's audience pre-sell founding memberships/assessments before buildout, converting media reach into the anchor-tenant pre-sale that de-risks the lease?
- Is there an assessment-first funnel (paid eval → program → reassessment) rather than lesson-by-lesson retail?
- What's the remote/hybrid programming product that keeps revenue alive when athletes are playing outdoors — and that scales beyond the building?
