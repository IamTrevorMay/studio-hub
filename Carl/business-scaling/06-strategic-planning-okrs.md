# Strategic Planning: OKRs, Rocks & Operating Cadences

## TL;DR

- **Pick ONE system and run it for at least 3 quarters before judging it.** ~60% of OKR deployments succeed; most failures are abandonment after one messy cycle, not framework defects. Cycles 1–2 are capability-building, not results.
- **For a company under ~20 people, EOS-style Rocks + a weekly scorecard beats textbook OKRs.** OKRs assume you already have operational hygiene; EOS *creates* it (meeting rhythm, issue-solving, metric review). Layer OKR-style measurability onto Rocks later.
- **The whole game is the weekly meeting, not the quarterly document.** Teams that check in weekly complete ~43% more of their goals than teams that don't. A plan reviewed weekly is a plan; a plan reviewed quarterly is a wish.
- **Cap everything ruthlessly:** 1–3 objectives per team, 2–4 key results each (5 absolute max), 3–7 Rocks per person per quarter (fewer is better — 3 is the practical ceiling for a founder who also does the work), 5–15 numbers on the weekly scorecard.
- **Goals cover 50–70% of work effort, not 100%.** Business-as-usual (publish the videos, run the lessons, pay the bills) is the "whirlwind" — it lives on the scorecard, not in the OKRs. OKRs are only for *change*.
- **Track lead measures, not just lag measures.** Revenue, subs, and memberships are lag; outreach emails sent, videos shipped, trial lessons booked are lead. The scoreboard must contain things the team can move *this week*.
- **Never wire goal scores to compensation or performance reviews.** It kills ambition instantly — everyone sandbags. Expected score on stretch goals is 0.6–0.7; committed goals are binary (1.0 or a post-mortem).
- **Annual = direction, quarterly = commitment, weekly = accountability.** One page of vision, one day of quarterly planning, 90 minutes a week. Anything more elaborate at small scale is bureaucracy cosplay.

## 1. Why planning systems exist (and why small companies skip them at their peril)

The founder of a <20-person company is the strategy. That works until (a) a second business line appears, (b) the first non-founder leaders appear, or (c) the founder's attention becomes the bottleneck. All three are exactly the situation of a media company adding a training facility. Symptoms that a planning system is overdue:

- Priorities change weekly based on whatever's on fire or whatever the founder read last.
- Team members can't state the company's #1 priority for this quarter without guessing.
- The same problems get "discussed" repeatedly but never killed.
- Big initiatives (a facility buildout, a new channel, a product launch) drift because nobody reviews them on a rhythm.
- Everything feels busy; nothing feels *done*.

The fix is not more planning. It's a **cadence**: a small set of recurring rituals, each attached to a source of truth (a scorecard, a goal list), at three speeds — annual, quarterly, weekly. Sierra Ventures frames operating cadence as exactly two components: **sources of truth** (quantified definitions of success) and **rituals** (a consistent practice of reviewing them). Everything below is variations on that.

## 2. OKRs, done properly

### 2.1 The mechanics (Grove → Doerr, *Measure What Matters*)

- **Objective** = qualitative, inspiring, time-bound direction. "Become the default hitting-development destination in the county."
- **Key Results (2–4 per O)** = quantitative proof the objective happened. Must be measurable, verifiable by an outsider, and outcome-shaped: "from X to Y by when." Not a task list.
- Andy Grove's original insight at Intel: answer two questions — *Where do I want to go?* (objective) and *How will I pace myself to see if I'm getting there?* (key results).
- **Initiatives/tasks** hang underneath KRs but are not KRs. If completing the task doesn't move a number, the number was wrong or the task was.

### 2.2 Committed vs. aspirational (Google's split)

Google's playbook (via whatmatters.com) distinguishes:

- **Committed OKRs**: "we agree these will be achieved, and we will adjust schedules and resources to deliver them." Expected score: **1.0, binary**. A miss requires a genuine post-mortem on planning/execution — no partial credit. Tag them "C" at cycle start.
- **Aspirational OKRs**: "how we'd like the world to look, even though we have no clear idea how to get there." Expected average score: **~0.7 with high variance**. They deliberately exceed one quarter's capacity and roll forward until done.

Classic trap: labeling a committed goal aspirational (gives everyone permission to miss payroll-critical work) or vice versa (crushes morale on a genuine moonshot). Declare the type when you set it, not when you grade it.

### 2.3 Scoring and closing the cycle

Grading scale 0–1.0 (whatmatters.com):

- **0.7–1.0 green** — delivered (for aspirational, this is a win)
- **0.4–0.6 yellow** — real progress, fell short
- **0.0–0.3 red** — failed to make significant progress

Google targets an **average of ~0.7 across aspirational OKRs**. Consistent 1.0s = sandbagging; consistent <0.3 = fantasy goals. Add **self-assessment on top of raw math**: a PR team that landed 2 of 3 placements (0.67) but one was a front-page WSJ hit that brought in major clients can legitimately grade 0.9 — judgment beats arithmetic when the number misrepresents impact.

End-of-cycle ritual (don't skip — it's the most-skipped, highest-value step): ~5 minutes per KR to (1) finalize score, (2) capture the learning, (3) decide keep/modify/kill for next cycle. Reflection questions: Did we accomplish the objectives? What blocked us? How would we rewrite them? What did we learn that changes next quarter's approach?

### 2.4 The rules that keep OKRs from becoming bureaucracy

From Ben Lamorte's 40-mistakes catalog (okrs.com) and AKF/whatmatters:

1. **1–3 objectives per entity, 2–4 KRs each.** Five KRs is the absolute maximum.
2. **OKRs are not everything** — target 50–70% of work effort. Exclude business-as-usual.
3. **OKRs sit downstream of strategy.** If you can't state the strategy in a paragraph, fix that first; OKRs express a strategy, they don't substitute for one.
4. **Don't cascade mechanically.** Company sets top-level; teams *propose* their own OKRs in response, leadership aligns through conversation. Copy-pasting a parent KR as a child objective deletes the thinking.
5. **Not all KRs should be lagging financials.** Include leading indicators the team can act on weekly ("trial lessons booked," "sponsor pitches sent"), not just "revenue."
6. **Never wire to comp/performance reviews** — it converts stretch into sandbagging and learning into fear. State this explicitly at rollout.
7. **One tracker, one source of truth.** Spreadsheet is fine at small scale. Conflicting trackers kill trust.
8. **Confidence check-ins, not just status.** Each KR owner reports a confidence level (start at 50%, update weekly). A *change* in confidence triggers the conversation — this surfaces problems mid-quarter instead of week 13.
9. **Commit to 2–3 full cycles up front.** Cycle 1 builds understanding, cycle 2 improves quality, cycle 3 builds discipline. Organizations typically need 2–3 cycles before consistent benefit; abandoning after one imperfect quarter is the #1 self-inflicted failure.
10. **CFRs are the delivery mechanism** (Conversations, Feedback, Recognition — Doerr's companion system): weekly 1:1-style conversations about progress, real-time feedback, and public recognition of *progress and learning*, not just hits. Without CFRs, OKRs are a spreadsheet nobody opens.

### 2.5 Canonical case studies (with numbers)

- **Intel, Operation Crush (1980):** existential response to Motorola's 68000 chip. Company-wide OKR: win the 16-bit market; a key objective was **2,000 design wins** — beaten by 25% within a year; Intel held ~85% of the 16-bit market by 1986. Lesson: OKRs shine brightest as a *whole-company pivot mechanism* — everyone reorients in weeks.
- **Google:** adopted OKRs at ~40 employees (1999, Doerr's pitch); still in use at >100k. Lesson: the system scales, but Google also invented the committed/aspirational split because pure-stretch broke down.
- **Superhuman:** single north-star KR — % of users answering "very disappointed" if they lost the product — pushed to 40%+; became the standard PMF metric and unlocked a $33M raise. Lesson for a small company: **one great KR can be the whole company OKR**.
- **Allbirds:** aspirational objective "lowest carbon footprint in the industry" was initially unmeasurable; forced them to build carbon accounting, which later drove product innovation (carbon-negative materials). Lesson: an aspirational O can legitimately precede its own measurability — but you must commit to building the measurement within a cycle (Lamorte's rule: create a baseline metric before next cycle rather than dropping the objective).

## 3. EOS / Traction (Gino Wickman) — the small-company default

EOS is a *complete* operating system, not just goal-setting: six components (Vision, People, Data, Issues, Process, Traction). Explicitly designed for **10–250 employee** entrepreneurial companies; used by 200,000+. It is prescriptive on purpose — you buy the whole meal. Small firms (The Charity CFO started EOS at 4 people) routinely **self-implement** straight from the book; a certified implementer costs real money and is unnecessary under ~20 people if the founder has discipline.

### 3.1 V/TO (Vision/Traction Organizer) — the two-page strategy doc

Eight sections. This replaces the 30-page strategic plan nobody reads:

1. **Core Values** (3–7, actually used for hiring/firing)
2. **Core Focus** (purpose + niche — the "why us")
3. **10-Year Target** (one big quantified ambition; BHAG-equivalent)
4. **Marketing Strategy** (target market, 3 uniques, proven process, guarantee)
5. **3-Year Picture** (revenue, profit, measurables, ~5-15 bullet "what it looks like")
6. **1-Year Plan** (revenue/profit targets + 3–7 goals for the year)
7. **Quarterly Rocks** (below)
8. **Issues List** (everything unsolved, parked in one visible place)

For a two-business setup (media company + facility), the pragmatic move is **one shared V/TO for the holding entity, separate 1-Year Plans and Rocks per business** — shared values and 10-year target, distinct execution lanes. Don't force one Rock list across businesses with different economics.

### 3.2 Rocks — the 90-Day World

- Rocks = the **3–7 most important priorities for the next 90 days**, per company and per person. Named for the rocks/pebbles/sand jar metaphor: put the big rocks in first or the sand fills the jar.
- Each Rock has one owner, a due date (end of quarter), and a binary done/not-done definition. That binary-ness is Rocks' strength *and* weakness vs. OKR KRs (see §5).
- 90 days is deliberate: long enough for a step-change, short enough that humans hold focus. Wickman: organizations fray at ~90 days without recalibration.
- Realistic completion target: **80%+ of Rocks done** is healthy. 100% every quarter = sandbagging (same signal as OKR 1.0s).

### 3.3 The weekly Scorecard

- **5–15 numbers, reviewed weekly**, each with an owner and a goal threshold. "An absolute pulse on the business."
- Rule: report **on-track / off-track only — zero discussion** in the review. Off-track numbers "drop down" to the Issues List and get solved in IDS.
- Numbers should be **activity-weighted (leading)** wherever possible: leads generated, pieces published, lessons delivered, proposals sent, cash balance, AR aging. If the scorecard is all lag metrics, it's a rear-view mirror.
- Litmus test for each number: if this were the only data you saw for a week, would you know whether the business is okay?

### 3.4 The Level 10 Meeting — the weekly engine

Weekly, 90 minutes, leadership team (ideally ≤7 people, hard ceiling ~10), **same day, same time, same agenda, start on time, end on time**. Named for the goal: everyone rates the meeting 1–10 at the end; target ≥8. The fixed agenda:

| Segment | Time | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Segue (good news, personal + business) | 5 min | Humanizes; transitions from "in" to "on" the business |
| Scorecard review | 5 min | On/off-track only; off-track → Issues List |
| Rock review | 5 min | On/off-track only; off-track → Issues List |
| Customer/employee headlines | 5 min | Anything the team should know, good or bad |
| To-do list review | 5 min | Last week's commitments; target **≥90% completion weekly** |
| **IDS** (Identify, Discuss, Solve) | **60 min** | The actual work of the meeting |
| Conclude | 5 min | Recap to-dos, cascade messages, rate 1–10 |

**IDS discipline:** pick the **top 3** issues (not the oldest, not all of them), identify the *root cause* (most stated issues are symptoms), discuss once — no politicking, no re-litigating — and solve it forever. Solutions become next week's to-dos with owners. A solved issue never reappears; if it does, you solved a symptom.

Why this design works at small scale: the first 30 minutes is pure reporting with a no-discussion rule (kills meandering), and the hour of IDS means the meeting *produces decisions*, which is why people rate it 8+ and keep showing up. Most "weekly syncs" die because they're 100% status and 0% solving.

### 3.5 EOS quarterly + annual pulse

- **Quarterly session (1 day, off-site if possible):** review prior quarter (Rock completion %), review V/TO, refresh the Issues List, set next quarter's Rocks, IDS the big stuff.
- **Annual session (2 days classic EOS; 1 day is fine under 15 people):** day 1 = team health + review year + update Core Values/Core Focus/3-Year Picture; day 2 = set 1-Year Plan, Q1 Rocks, IDS.

## 4. 4DX (McChesney/Covey/Huling) — the execution-only overlay

*The 4 Disciplines of Execution* is narrower than EOS or OKRs: it's purely about executing 1–2 goals **despite the whirlwind** (their name for the day-job that consumes everyone). Its concepts are worth stealing even if you run EOS or OKRs:

1. **Focus on the Wildly Important (WIG):** no team focuses on more than **2 WIGs at once**; format is always "X to Y by when." Senior leaders can veto a team's WIG but not dictate it. "The battles you choose must win the war."
2. **Act on Lead Measures:** the discipline's core IP. Lag = the result (revenue, weight, subscriber count). Lead = predictive AND influenceable behaviors (sales follow-up calls, hours of deliberate practice, videos published per week). The team bets on which lead behaviors move the lag number, then tracks the *behaviors*.
3. **Compelling Scoreboard:** simple, visible to the whole team, shows lead + lag, **max ~6 measures**, and a player can tell in 5 seconds whether they're winning. "People play differently when they're keeping score."
4. **Cadence of Accountability:** weekly WIG session (20–30 min), three-part agenda: **Account** (report on last week's commitments) → **Review** the scoreboard → **Plan** (each person names the 1–2 things they'll do this week to move the lead measures). Commitments are made *to teammates*, not to the boss.

4DX's lead-measure discipline is the single most useful graft for a content business or training facility: "grow YouTube revenue" is unactionable on Monday morning; "publish 2 longforms + 5 shorts and send 10 sponsor outreach emails per week" is a scoreboard.

## 5. Choosing and combining systems at small scale

### 5.1 The honest comparison

| Dimension | OKRs | EOS/Rocks | 4DX |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scope | Goal-setting layer only | Whole operating system | Execution of 1–2 goals |
| Prescriptiveness | Low (dangerous for novices) | High (a feature under 50 ppl) | Medium |
| Goal shape | Outcome metrics (X→Y) | Binary deliverables | X→Y + lead behaviors |
| Meeting system | BYO (usually the failure point) | Level 10, fully specified | Weekly WIG session |
| Stretch culture | Built-in (0.7 target) | Not built-in (100%-ish expected) | Not built-in |
| Best when | Ops already run smoothly; need alignment + ambition | Company feels chaotic; needs structure | One goal matters overwhelmingly, whirlwind is winning |

Consensus across comparison sources (whatmatters, mooncamp, Strety, quantive): **if the company feels chaotic, EOS first; if the machine runs and you need better goals, OKRs.** They are not rivals — EOS governs *how the business runs*, OKRs govern *how priorities become measurable*.

### 5.2 The recommended hybrid for a founder-led ~5–20 person company

This is the configuration Carl should default to prescribing (it's also mooncamp's recommended integration: EOS chassis, OKR-shaped goals):

1. **V/TO-style two-pager** as the strategy doc (values, focus, 3-year picture, 1-year plan). Refresh annually, glance quarterly.
2. **Quarterly Rocks, written like OKRs**: each Rock gets an outcome definition ("Facility soft-open with 15 founding members paying, by Mar 31") instead of a bare deliverable ("Open facility"). 3 company Rocks max; 1–3 per person.
3. **Tag each Rock committed or aspirational** (Google's split). Rent-critical Rocks are committed. Growth swings are aspirational, graded on the 0.7 curve.
4. **Weekly scorecard of 5–15 numbers**, majority lead measures, one owner each.
5. **Weekly L10-format meeting** (60–90 min; a 5-person team can run the same agenda in 60). This meeting IS the system. Everything else is paperwork feeding it.
6. **Quarterly one-day planning session**; annual 1–2 day session.
7. **Nothing else.** No monthly all-hands theater, no cascading team OKRs, no software beyond a spreadsheet/Notion page until >25 people. (David Sacks-style guidance for slightly larger cos: cap the company dashboard ~30 rows, 5–7 metrics per leader, 3 objectives × 3 KRs max — small teams should be well under these ceilings.)

### 5.3 Alternative goal formats worth knowing

- **NCTs — Narratives, Commitments, Tasks** (Ravi Mehta, ex-Tinder CPO; via First Round Review): replaces the O with a *narrative paragraph* explaining strategic reasoning, then **3–5 measurable commitments** (expected to be hit, ~unlike 0.7-graded KRs), then tasks. Fixes the two most common OKR complaints — missing context and stretch-goal fatalism. Good fit for teams that found OKRs hollow.
- **W Framework** (Nels Gilbreth/Eventbrite, via First Round): planning as four passes — leadership shares **context** down → teams build **plans** up → leadership **integrates** and decides → teams **buy in** and finalize. The point: plans built without shared context and plans dictated without team authorship both fail. Even a 6-person company should do a micro-W: founder writes one page of context *before* asking anyone to propose Rocks.
- **Kill criteria** (Annie Duke, via First Round): at planning time, pre-commit to the conditions under which an initiative gets killed ("if founding memberships <8 by Feb 15, we delay buildout phase 2"). Removes ego from mid-quarter reversals. Extremely valuable for capital projects like a facility.
- **Scenario planning:** best/middle/worst cases with pre-agreed responses; 12–18 month priority horizon. Cheap insurance for a business with sponsor-revenue concentration risk.

## 6. The operating calendar — full cadence spec

### 6.1 Annual (1–2 days, November/December for a Jan fiscal year)

Pre-work (send 1–2 weeks ahead): Start/Stop/Continue from every attendee; prior-year metrics summary; external opportunities/threats. Rhythm Systems' flow, adapted:

1. **Review & reflect** — prior year vs. plan; what did we learn.
2. **Strategic alignment** — validate/refresh core values, core focus, 3-year picture.
3. **Vision** — "destination postcard": what does Dec 31 next year look like, concretely.
4. **Annual plan** — revenue/profit targets, an annual theme (one phrase), **3–5 key initiatives** with owners and success criteria.
5. **Q1 plan** — first quarter's Rocks/OKRs before leaving the room.

Notes: an outside or at least non-CEO facilitator markedly improves candor; the CEO facilitating their own annual session is a known anti-pattern. First Round's warning for early-stage: annual *plans* are dangerous when things change fast — set annual *direction*, keep commitment horizons at 90 days (some scale-ups like On Deck settled on 6-month planning windows as the compromise; under 20 people, stick to quarters).

### 6.2 Quarterly (1 day, or two half-days; last 2 weeks of the quarter)

Agenda: (1) grade last quarter — Rock completion % / OKR scores + 5-min-per-KR reflect-and-reset; (2) review V/TO and annual plan — still true?; (3) empty the heads — refresh the Issues List; (4) IDS the top strategic issues; (5) set next quarter's Rocks/OKRs — propose, debate, cut to 3; (6) each owner writes the first two weeks of tasks before leaving. Whole-team, off-site if possible, phones away. One day per quarter = 4 days/year of planning; that is the *entire* acceptable planning overhead at this scale.

### 6.3 Weekly (60–90 min, same slot forever)

Run the L10 agenda from §3.4. Additional operator rules (Sacks-school, cremanski, GCE):

- Every red/off-track item leaves the meeting with **a single owner and a date** — no orphaned reds.
- No slide decks; review the live scorecard + a shared running-notes doc.
- KR/Rock owners update their numbers *before* the meeting; meeting time is for exceptions and solving, never for data entry.
- Periodically audit the ritual itself: does each meeting/artifact reduce risk, accelerate learning, or allocate resources better? If not, cut it. Cadence is what "turns the operating system from documentation into behavior" — but only the minimum effective dose.

### 6.4 Monthly (optional at <15 people)

If used at all: 30–60 min financial review (P&L vs. plan, cash runway, AR) — this is the one place lagging financials belong on a rhythm of their own. Fold into the first weekly of the month rather than adding a meeting.

## 7. Playbook: standing this up from zero in 6 weeks

**Week 1 — Strategy on a page.** Founder drafts the V/TO two-pager solo, then pressure-tests with the team. If strategy can't be articulated, stop here and fix that; goals without strategy is mistake #3 on every failure list.

**Week 2 — Scorecard.** Pick 5–10 weekly numbers (majority leading), assign owners, backfill 4 weeks of history so the first review has trend context. Set goal thresholds.

**Week 3 — First weekly meeting.** Run the full L10 agenda even though it'll be awkward. Rate it. Do not skip the rating.

**Week 4 — First Rocks/OKRs.** Half-day session: founder shares one page of context (micro-W), everyone proposes, cut to 3 company Rocks + 1–3 personal each. Write each as X→Y-by-when. Tag committed vs. aspirational.

**Weeks 5–6 — Groove.** Two more weeklies. Add confidence levels to Rock check-ins. Kill or fix any scorecard number nobody acts on.

**End of quarter 1 —** Grade honestly, run reflect-and-reset, and *change the process parameters* (number of Rocks, meeting length, scorecard rows) based on what chafed. Running cycle 2 identically to cycle 1 is its own failure mode.

Adoption rules: start with the leadership/whole team only (at 6 people that's everyone); pilot before cascading if there are sub-teams ("nail it before you scale it"); train by *drafting real goals together*, not by assigning the book.

## 8. Common mistakes (field guide)

1. **Too many goals.** The #1 killer everywhere. Everything-is-important = nothing is. Enforce the caps mechanically.
2. **KRs that are task lists.** "Launch the website" is a task; "20% of new bookings via website by June" is a KR. Ask "what's the intended outcome?" until you hit a number.
3. **Set-and-forget.** Goals written in planning week, reopened in grading week. The weekly review is non-negotiable; without it the whole apparatus is a quarterly guilt ritual.
4. **All lag, no lead.** A scoreboard of revenue/subs/followers tells you what already happened. If the team can't affect the board this week, they'll stop looking at it.
5. **Wiring goals to comp/reviews.** Instantly converts 0.7-culture into sandbagging. Also the mechanism by which OKRs become "an HR thing," which is fatal.
6. **Mechanical cascading.** Parent KR copy-pasted as child objective. Alignment happens through conversation about *why*, not through spreadsheet plumbing.
7. **Confusing goals with strategy.** OKRs/Rocks operationalize a strategy; adopted without one, they just make the drift measurable.
8. **Quitting after one cycle.** First cycle is always messy; 2–3 cycles to competence is the documented norm. Pre-commit to three.
9. **100% BAU goals.** Restating the day job as objectives ("publish weekly videos") wastes the system. BAU → scorecard; *change* → goals.
10. **The founder exempting themselves.** No CEO buy-in is failure mode #1 in Lamorte's list. The founder's Rocks are reviewed first, and the founder reports off-track like everyone else.
11. **Status meetings masquerading as accountability meetings.** If >30% of the weekly is people reading updates aloud, restructure: reporting is on/off-track only; time goes to solving.
12. **Sandbagging disguised as excellence.** Perfect scores every quarter is a warning sign, not a trophy — for OKRs (all 1.0s) and Rocks (100% quarter after quarter) alike.
13. **Tool-first adoption.** Buying OKR software before running two cycles on a spreadsheet. Under ~25 people the tool is a distraction; the ritual is the product.
14. **Punishing honest reds.** The first time someone gets roasted for reporting off-track early, all future reporting turns green until week 12. Celebrate early bad news explicitly.
15. **Multi-business blur.** For a two-business owner (media + facility): mixing both businesses' metrics into one scorecard and one Rock list guarantees the noisier business starves the newer one. Separate scorecard sections, separate Rock lanes, one shared meeting is the workable compromise until the facility has its own operator.

## 9. Questions Carl should ask a client

**Diagnosis**
- "What's the company's #1 priority this quarter? Now — would each of your teammates give the same answer?"
- "Show me the last plan you wrote. When did anyone last look at it?" (Gap between authorship and review = system health.)
- "What 5 numbers, seen weekly, would tell you the business is okay? Do you currently see them weekly?"
- "What issue have you discussed more than three times without killing it?"
- "What % of your week is whirlwind (keeping the machine running) vs. building something new?" (If >85% whirlwind, prescribe 4DX-style single-WIG focus before a full system.)

**System fit**
- "Does the business feel chaotic (→ EOS chassis) or smooth-but-unambitious (→ OKR layer)?"
- "Who besides you could own a Rock end-to-end today? If the answer is nobody, we have a people problem wearing a planning costume."
- "If a goal is missed, what happens right now — post-mortem, shrug, or blame?" (Predicts whether committed/aspirational split will be honored.)

**Goal quality (per proposed goal)**
- "Is this X to Y by when? What's X today?" (No baseline = not ready.)
- "Is this a change or the day job?"
- "Committed or aspirational — which, and would you bet the quarter's schedule on it?"
- "What's the lead measure — the thing you'd do *this week* to move it?"
- "What's the kill criterion — what evidence, by what date, means we stop?"

**Cadence audit**
- "Same day, same time, same agenda every week — yes or no?" (Any no = the cadence doesn't exist yet.)
- "In last week's meeting, how many minutes were status vs. solving?"
- "Did every off-track item leave with an owner and a date?"
- "When did you last cancel the weekly because you were 'too busy'?" (Cancelling the accountability meeting during crunch is like unplugging the smoke detector while cooking.)
- "Rate your weekly meeting 1–10. What would make it an 8?"

**Multi-venture specifics (Mayday-shaped clients)**
- "Do the two businesses share a scorecard? Show me where the facility's numbers hide behind the media numbers."
- "Which business owns your Tuesday?" (Attention allocation should be explicit in the plan, not emergent.)
- "For the facility buildout: what are the committed milestones vs. the aspirational membership targets — and are they tagged differently?"
