# Rights, Licensing & Legal Basics for Media

Practical media law for a small creator-led company. This is operator-grade knowledge, not legal advice — the goal is to know (a) the 90% of situations you can handle with good paper and good habits, and (b) the 10% where you stop and call a lawyer. Relevant everywhere in a business like Mayday Media (YouTube channels, sponsors, freelance editors, merch) and doubly so for a training facility like Neptune Performance (filming athletes, minors, league footage, NIL).

## TL;DR

- **Never use commercial music without a license.** A YouTube video needs BOTH a sync license (composition) and a master license (recording). Solve this once with a subscription library (Epidemic Sound / Artlist, ~$120–$300/yr) and a written team rule: "no track that isn't from the library or made in-house."
- **Fair use is a defense you argue in court, not a permission slip.** Post-*Warhol* (2023), commercial use that serves the *same purpose* as the original almost never wins. Commentary/criticism with genuine added analysis still does (*Hosseinzadeh v. Klein*).
- **Get a signed release from every non-employee who appears on camera** — guests, athletes, clients, kids (parent/guardian signs). One page, signed before recording, stored forever. This is the cheapest insurance in media.
- **Sponsorship contracts: five clauses decide everything** — deliverables, payment terms (fight for 50% upfront, Net 30), exclusivity (narrow category, short window), usage rights (paid media priced separately), and termination/kill fees.
- **FTC disclosure is non-negotiable and the standard is strict**: verbal AND on-screen/written, before the pitch, in plain language. "#ad" buried in a description does not comply. Civil penalties can exceed $50,000 per violation, and both the brand and the creator are liable.
- **Freelancer work is NOT automatically yours even if you paid for it.** Use a contract with both work-for-hire language AND a backup copyright assignment. Without paper, the editor owns the edit.
- **Register the trademark when the brand starts making money** — $350/class USPTO fee, Class 41 (entertainment/training services) + Class 25 (merch), 12–18 months to register. Do a clearance search *before* naming anything.
- **Athletes' NIL and game footage are two different rights.** The league/broadcaster owns the footage (copyright); the athlete owns their identity (publicity/NIL). Using either commercially without the right permission is a claim waiting to happen.

## 1. Music licensing for YouTube

### The two-license problem

Every recorded song embodies two separate copyrights:

1. **Composition** (melody, lyrics, arrangement) — owned by the songwriter/publisher. Using it synchronized with video requires a **synchronization ("sync") license**.
2. **Sound recording** (the specific recorded performance, the "master") — owned by the label/artist. Using the actual recording requires a **master use license**.

To put a commercial track in a YouTube video you need **both**. There is no compulsory license for sync — the publisher can simply say no, or quote any price. This is why "I'll just credit the artist" and "it's only 10 seconds" are both irrelevant: credit is not a license, and there is no de minimis safe harbor you can rely on.

Common myths to kill on sight:
- **"Royalty-free" ≠ free.** It means you pay once (or subscribe) instead of per-use royalties. It's still a license with terms.
- **A cover version doesn't help.** Re-recording avoids the master but you still need sync rights to the composition.
- **"No copyright intended" is legally meaningless** and functions as a written confession of knowledge.
- **A Content ID claim is not a license.** If the video is claimed and monetized by the rightsholder, you've lost the revenue AND you're still technically infringing — the claimant can escalate to a strike or a lawsuit at any time.

### Content ID mechanics (the enforcement layer)

- YouTube's Content ID fingerprints uploads against a rightsholder database — over **2.2 billion Content ID claims were processed in 2024**. Detection is effectively guaranteed for any commercial track.
- A **Content ID claim** lets the owner block the video, mute the audio, or (most commonly) **take your ad revenue** on that video. No strike, but the video earns for someone else — forever, in most cases.
- A **copyright strike** is the serious one: it comes from a formal DMCA takedown, removes the video, and **three strikes in 90 days terminates the channel** — all videos deleted, monetization gone, no new accounts allowed. For a business whose main asset is a channel, this is an existential risk, which is why music discipline is an operations policy, not a legal nicety.

### The practical solution: subscription libraries

For a small media company the answer is almost always a music subscription, made mandatory by policy:

| Platform | Rough price (2025–26) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Epidemic Sound | ~$10/mo annual (~$240/yr commercial), $17.99 month-to-month | 50k+ tracks, stems for every track, 300 downloads/week; they own/control both sync and master so clearance is total |
| Artlist | ~$9.99/mo annual (~$300/yr for broad commercial tier), $14.99 monthly | Broader license scope (broadcast, ads, film festivals, client work) |
| Musicbed / Soundstripe | varies | Musicbed skews cinematic/premium; Soundstripe is budget-friendly |

Key license terms to actually verify before subscribing:
- **Perpetuity for published work**: both Epidemic and Artlist let videos published during your subscription stay licensed and monetized forever after you cancel. But you **cannot use downloaded tracks in NEW projects after cancellation.**
- **Channel coverage**: standard plans typically cover **one channel per platform**. A multi-channel operation (main channel + clips channel + client channels) usually needs a commercial/teams tier — check this, it's the most common accidental breach.
- **Client work / ads**: if you make content for sponsors that they'll run as paid media, confirm the license tier covers advertising use.

Other lanes: YouTube's own Audio Library (free, safe, generic), commissioning original music (you then control it — get it under a proper work-for-hire/assignment contract, see §7), or direct sync licensing for a specific commercial track (expect anywhere from a few hundred dollars to five figures, weeks of lead time, and many non-responses; only worth it for flagship projects).

### Music policy checklist for a small team

1. One approved library subscription; everyone edits from the same account/tier.
2. Written rule in the editor onboarding doc: no track outside the library or in-house originals; no "temp music" that could survive to publish.
3. Log which track was used in each video (a column in the project tracker) so a future claim can be answered with the license in minutes.
4. Whitelist your channels inside the library's dashboard (Epidemic/Artlist both offer channel clearing to pre-empt Content ID claims on their own catalog).
5. Livestream caution: background music at events/facilities (e.g., gym playlists at a training facility during a filmed session) triggers claims too. Control the speakers when cameras roll.

## 2. Fair use realities

### The four factors (17 U.S.C. § 107)

1. **Purpose and character of the use** — commercial vs. nonprofit/educational; whether the use is "transformative" (adds new purpose, meaning, or message). This is the factor courts weight most.
2. **Nature of the copyrighted work** — creative works get more protection than factual ones.
3. **Amount and substantiality used** — both quantity and whether you took the "heart" of the work; measured against what's *necessary for your purpose*, not a fixed percentage.
4. **Effect on the market** — does your use substitute for the original or its licensing market?

Fair use is decided case-by-case by a judge, after you've been sued. Budgeting reality: even a winning fair use defense costs six figures in litigation. The operating question is never "would we win?" but "would anyone credibly claim, and can we avoid giving them the chance?"

### The creator-favorable anchor: *Hosseinzadeh v. Klein* (S.D.N.Y. 2017)

The H3H3 reaction-video case. The Kleins used **3:15 of a 5:24 video (~60%)**, interspersed in short segments with moment-by-moment criticism and mockery. Held: fair use on summary judgment.
- The court called it "quintessential criticism and comment" — statutory fair use purposes create a strong presumption on factor one.
- 60% was fine **because** the clips were broken up and each segment was the subject of commentary; the amount was "reasonable to accomplish the transformative purpose."
- No market substitution: nobody watches the reaction *instead of* the original for the original's entertainment value.

The implicit warning in the opinion: a "reaction video" that just plays the original with occasional facial expressions and no real commentary is closer to infringement. **The commentary must be the product; the clip must be the exhibit.**

### The 2023 earthquake: *Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*

The Supreme Court narrowed factor one. Warhol's silkscreen of Goldsmith's Prince photo — plainly "transformed" aesthetically — was **not** fair use for the specific licensing at issue, because both uses served the **same purpose** (a magazine image of Prince) and both were commercial. Holding, distilled: **adding new expression is not enough; the use must serve a genuinely different purpose than the original, and commerciality cuts against you.**

Post-*Warhol* pattern (2024–2025 appellate cases):
- *Philpot v. Independent Journal Review* (4th Cir. 2024): using a photo of Ted Nugent in a news article, essentially as-is — **not fair use**; same purpose (depicting the subject), commercial.
- *Griner v. King* (8th Cir. 2024): "Success Kid" meme in political fundraising — **not fair use**; purely commercial, same purpose.
- *Hachette v. Internet Archive* (2d Cir. 2024): format-shifting print books to digital — **not transformative**. "Merely shifting a work to a new format is not transformative."
- *Keck v. Mix Creative Learning* (5th Cir. 2024): art kits teaching recreation of artworks — **fair use**; educational purpose genuinely differed from the original's aesthetic purpose.

### Operator's rules of thumb (post-Warhol)

- **Safe-ish**: genuine criticism, commentary, analysis, parody OF the work itself; short clips each tied to specific commentary; news reporting on the clip as the story.
- **Danger zone**: compilations, "highlights," reuploads with light edits, using someone's photo/footage as b-roll or a thumbnail because it looks good, reaction content that's mostly silent watching, using a song "under" your content, parody of *something else* using the work (that's satire, which fair use protects far less).
- **The thumbnail trap**: using a photographer's image as your thumbnail is the same-purpose commercial use *Philpot* condemned. License it or shoot it.
- Ask three questions: (1) Am I commenting *on this work*, or just using it? (2) Did I take only what the commentary needs? (3) Could my video substitute for the original or a license the owner would normally sell? Any wrong answer → license it, replace it, or cut it.

## 3. Enforcement mechanics, DMCA, and statutory damages

### Claims vs. strikes vs. lawsuits (escalation ladder)

1. **Content ID claim** — automated, revenue redirected/blocked. Disputable in-platform; the claimant gets ~30 days to respond; escalation path runs dispute → appeal → the claimant must file a formal takedown or release.
2. **DMCA takedown / copyright strike** — a sworn legal notice. Video removed, strike issued. Options: wait 90 days for expiry, get a **retraction** from the claimant (often achievable by direct outreach/settlement), or file a **counter-notice**.
3. **Counter-notice** — you swear under penalty of perjury the takedown was mistaken and consent to federal court jurisdiction. The claimant then has **10–14 business days to file suit** or the video is restored. Never file one casually: it converts a platform dispute into an invitation to litigate, and it requires disclosing your identity/address.
4. **Federal lawsuit** — statutory damages of **$750–$30,000 per work infringed**, up to **$150,000 per work for willful infringement**, plus attorney's fees — but only if the plaintiff registered the copyright before infringement (or within 3 months of publication). Otherwise they're limited to actual damages/profits, which for most creator disputes are small. This asymmetry is why registration matters (below) and why most disputes settle.

### Protecting your own catalog

- **Register copyrights for your flagship works** ($45–$65 per registration at copyright.gov; group registration options exist for batches). Registration before infringement is the ticket to statutory damages and fees — it transforms your leverage against freebooters, course-sellers ripping your content, and re-uploaders.
- Enroll in YouTube's Copyright Match Tool / Content ID where eligible; use takedowns against verbatim re-uploads, but **never against commentary/criticism of you** — a bad-faith takedown against obvious fair use creates §512(f) liability and a PR fire (the *Hosseinzadeh* plaintiff also lost a DMCA misrepresentation-adjacent claim narrative and paid reputationally).
- Repeat-infringer math: three strikes in 90 days kills the channel. If the business runs on one channel, treat anything that could draw a strike as a board-level risk.

## 4. Guest and appearance releases

### Why: three separate rights walk into every shoot

A person on camera implicates (1) **copyright** in their contribution (a guest's interview answers can make them a joint author of the recording absent paper), (2) **right of publicity** (state-law control over commercial use of name/image/likeness), and (3) **privacy/defamation** exposure. A release cleans all three at once, in one page, for $0.

### Essential clauses (the one-page release)

1. **Parties and date** — legal names, the show/company entity.
2. **Grant of recording and distribution rights** — permission to record audio/video and distribute on "any media now known or later developed," worldwide, in perpetuity. (Platform-specific grants age badly.)
3. **Copyright assignment or broad license** — assign the guest's contribution to the company, or at minimum grant an irrevocable, royalty-free, transferable license. Assignment is cleaner: it blocks later co-authorship claims.
4. **Editorial control waiver** — you may edit, excerpt, and repurpose at your discretion; no obligation to publish; **no takedown right** for the guest. (Grant courtesy takedowns as a matter of relationship, never contract.)
5. **Repurposing/clips clause** — explicit right to cut Shorts, clips, audiograms, promos, thumbnails using their likeness.
6. **Name/likeness/publicity grant** — use of name, image, voice, biography for the content and its promotion.
7. **Compensation statement** — "no compensation except as agreed in writing" (or the payment terms). Consideration makes the contract solid.
8. **Representations** — guest has the right to say what they say; nothing knowingly defamatory/infringing/confidential.
9. **AI/likeness clause (modern addition)** — permit routine AI editing/transcription tools; **prohibit synthetic voice/face generation without separate written consent**. Increasingly expected by sophisticated guests; offering it builds trust.
10. **Minors** — parent/guardian signs; identify the minor and the guardian relationship. Non-negotiable.

### Operating playbook

- Sign **before** recording — a release signed after a contentious episode is a negotiation, not a formality. Bake it into the booking flow (e-sign link in the calendar invite).
- Store releases permanently, indexed by episode/project. A release you can't find doesn't exist.
- **Crowd/location situations**: filmed public events get posted-notice signage ("by entering you consent to being recorded...") plus individual releases for anyone featured prominently. For a training facility: build the media release into the client intake packet — every athlete (guardian for minors) signs a media consent covering promotional use, social clips, and coaching-analysis video **as separate opt-ins**. An athlete who consents to coaching video has not consented to being marketing content.
- Employees: put appearance consent in the employment agreement/handbook once, not per-shoot.
- Voice actors, hosts under contract: appearance and likeness rights live in their services agreement with survival terms — what happens to the back catalog if a host leaves is a top-five creator-company dispute; decide it in writing on day one.

## 5. Sponsorship contracts and FTC disclosure

### The contract: clauses and benchmarks

Every brand deal should have a written agreement (an email chain confirming a clean term sheet beats a handshake; a real short-form agreement beats both). The load-bearing terms, with market norms:

1. **Deliverables** — exact count, format, length, placement ("one 60–90s mid-roll integration in one long-form video"), publish window, and platform. Vague deliverables become free extra work.
2. **Payment** — amount, trigger, and timing. Norms: **Net 30 from publication** is standard; Net 60–90 with approval delays can push real payment past 75 days — reprice slow payers. **50% on signing / 50% on publish** is the standard ask, especially with first-time brand relationships. Specify what starts the clock (signing vs. delivery vs. publish). Finance/business niches command **$50–$200 CPM** for mid-roll integrations; usage rights and exclusivity justify going above rate-card.
3. **Exclusivity** — the silent rate-killer. Negotiate all three dimensions narrow: **category** (named direct competitors, not "all beverage brands" / "all fintech"), **duration** (e.g., 30 days post-publish; longer = more money), **territory** if relevant. Broad category language can block an entire vertical of future revenue for a month per deal.
4. **Usage rights / whitelisting** — organic placement and paid media are **different assets, priced separately**. If the brand wants to run your face in paid social, whitelist through your handles, or use the clip in ads: specify time window, platforms, geography, editing rights, and charge for it (common heuristics: +25–100% of the integration fee for 3–12 months of paid usage, scaling with scope).
5. **Approvals/revisions** — one script revision + one final-cut revision is standard; two rounds on larger compliance-heavy deals. "Unlimited revisions" = unpaid production. Put a business-days response deadline on the brand's review.
6. **Termination / kill fee** — tiered: X% if killed after signing, more after script approval, 100% (or near) after recording. Cancellation after work starts must trigger a fee. Cap any "makegood" obligations.
7. **Content ownership** — creator keeps copyright in the video; brand gets a license per the usage clause. Resist assignment of the whole video.
8. **Morality/termination-for-conduct clauses** — narrow them to convictions/adjudicated conduct, mutual where possible.
9. **FTC compliance clause** — both parties commit to compliant disclosure; brand supplies substantiated claims language **before recording** (claims that arrive after the shoot force reshoots).
10. **Substantiation & indemnity** — the brand warrants its product claims are true/substantiated and indemnifies you for claims arising from *their* claims copy. You indemnify for your own off-script statements. This clause is what stands between you and being a co-defendant in a false-advertising action.

### FTC disclosure rules (2023 Endorsement Guides, current regime)

- **Trigger**: any "material connection" — payment, free/discounted product, affiliate commission, employment, family/personal relationship, contest entry. The definition is deliberately broad; free product alone triggers it. Even tagging a brand can be an endorsement.
- **Standard**: "clear and conspicuous" = unavoidable, understandable, in the same medium as the claim. For **video: disclose BOTH verbally (say it out loud) and visually (on-screen text and/or top of description)**, before or with the endorsement — not after the CTA, not below the "Show more" fold.
- **Livestreams**: disclose **repeatedly**, verbal + on-screen, because viewers join mid-stream.
- **Inadequate**: "#sp", "#spon", "#collab", "thanks [brand]!", hashtag soup, disclosure only behind "more," and — explicitly — **platform tools alone** (YouTube's paid-promotion checkbox and Instagram's "Paid partnership" tag are supplements, not compliance).
- **Adequate**: "Sponsored by [Brand]", "[Brand] paid me to make this video", "I get commission from links below" — plain language an ordinary viewer of *your* audience understands (match the audience's language; kid-directed content gets heightened scrutiny).
- **Honest opinion + actual use**: endorsements must reflect genuine views and real experience with the product; you cannot say what you don't believe or haven't tried.
- **Liability runs three ways**: advertiser, endorser, AND intermediaries (agencies/networks) are each on the hook. Brands are expected to monitor their creators.
- **Teeth**: civil penalties can exceed **$50,000 per violation** (inflation-adjusted annually). Enforcement history: warning letters to 12 health influencers + two trade associations (Nov 2023); Teami — **$930k+ in refunds** over influencer health claims (2020); CSGOLotto (owners promoting their own site undisclosed, 2017); Fashion Nova — **$4.2M** review-suppression settlement (2022). Warning letters are cheap for the FTC and reputationally expensive for you; the real risk profile is "brand's category gets an FTC sweep and every creator in the campaign gets named."

**House policy worth adopting**: a standard disclosure block (verbal line in the script template + pinned description line), applied to every sponsored, affiliate, and gifted-product mention, no exceptions, no debates per video. Uniformity is cheaper than judgment calls.

## 6. Trademark basics for brands

### What trademark does (and doesn't)

Trademark protects **source identifiers** — names, logos, slogans — in connection with specific goods/services. It does not protect your content (that's copyright), your ideas, or stop non-commercial public use of a phrase. For a media company it protects: channel/show names, the company brand, merch branding, and (for a facility) the training-program brand.

### Common law vs. federal registration

- You get **common-law rights automatically** by using the mark in commerce — but only in your geographic/actual market, and enforcement is hard to prove.
- **Federal registration (USPTO)** gives nationwide priority, the ® symbol, presumption of validity, access to federal court and statutory remedies, a basis for platform enforcement (YouTube/Amazon/Etsy takedowns of knockoff merch), and blocks confusingly similar later filings.

### Numbers and process (2025)

- Filing fee: **$350 per class** (base fee, as of Jan 2025). Attorney help: **$500–$2,000+** — worth it; DIY applications fail on technicalities at high rates.
- Typical classes for a creator business: **Class 41** (entertainment/education services — the channel, shows, and *training services for a facility*), **Class 25** (apparel/merch). MrBeast's registration is the template: IC 041 for online videos, IC 025 for clothing.
- Timeline: **12–18 months** to registration.
- Maintenance: declaration of continued use between **years 5–6**, renewal at **years 9–10**, then **every 10 years**. No annual fee.
- Strength spectrum: fanciful (invented words) > arbitrary (real word, unrelated context) > suggestive > descriptive (weak, may be refused) > generic (unprotectable). "Neptune Performance" for a baseball lab is arbitrary/suggestive — decent. Descriptive names ("Elite Baseball Training") are trademark quicksand.

### Playbook

1. **Clearance search BEFORE committing to any name** — USPTO TESS, Google, domain/social handles, and state registrations. Rebranding a channel after a cease-and-desist costs far more than the search. This is the single highest-ROI legal task at naming time.
2. Register once the brand has revenue or a merch line — "serious about monetizing" is the standard trigger.
3. File in the classes you actually use or will use within ~3–4 years (intent-to-use filings hold a spot).
4. Enforce proportionately: knockoff merch → platform takedown with your registration number; a similarly named channel causing actual confusion → attorney letter; a fan account → leave it alone.
5. Multiple brands under one company (Mayday Media, Mayday Studio, Neptune Performance): register each mark that customers actually see; own them all in the operating entity (or a holding entity if the structure warrants), not personally, and paper any personal-to-company transfers.

## 7. Work-for-hire vs. licensing with freelancers

### The default rule that surprises everyone

**The creator owns the copyright by default. Payment does not transfer it.** Pay a freelance editor $2,000 for an edit with no contract → the editor owns the copyright in that edit; you have, at best, an implied license of uncertain scope. This is the most common silent liability in small media companies.

### The two-part statutory test for contractor work-for-hire

For an **independent contractor** (not a W-2 employee — employee work within job scope is automatically the employer's), a work is "made for hire" ONLY if **both**:
1. There is a **written agreement, signed before/at creation**, stating the work is a work made for hire, **and**
2. The work falls in one of **nine statutory categories**: contribution to a collective work; **part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work**; translation; supplementary work; compilation; instructional text; test; test answer material; atlas.

Good news for video: edits, motion graphics, and footage are typically "part of an audiovisual work" — squarely in category two. But **logos, standalone thumbnails/photography, podcast episodes (audio-only is contested), theme music as a standalone track, and software are NOT clearly in the nine categories** — for those, work-for-hire language alone can silently fail, leaving ownership with the freelancer.

### The belt-and-suspenders fix (use in every contractor agreement)

> "The work is a 'work made for hire' for Company. To the extent any deliverable does not qualify as a work made for hire, Contractor hereby irrevocably assigns to Company all right, title, and interest, including all copyrights, in the deliverables, and waives any moral rights to the extent permitted by law."

Plus: payment contingent on delivery + assignment; contractor warrants originality and that third-party assets (fonts, stock, plugins, music) are properly licensed for your use; portfolio-use permission defined explicitly (grant it — it costs nothing and freelancers care).

Two subtleties worth knowing:
- **Termination right**: a copyright *assignment* can be terminated by the author **35 years later** (17 U.S.C. §203); a true work-for-hire cannot. Irrelevant for weekly videos; relevant for a franchise IP, show format, or logo you expect to build a company on — one more reason to include the work-for-hire characterization where it validly applies.
- **Licensing as the alternative**: sometimes you don't need ownership — an exclusive, perpetual, sublicensable license covers most operating needs and freelancers may price it cheaper than assignment. But ownership is cleaner for anything brand-defining or resellable.

### Freelancer IP checklist

1. Signed agreement **before work starts** — WFH + assignment + originality warranty + licensed-assets warranty.
2. Confirm the freelancer's own tool stack is licensed for commercial/client use (their personal music-library subscription usually does NOT cover your channel — see §1; this is a classic Content ID surprise).
3. Deliverables include project files/assets, not just the export.
4. For overseas contractors, same paper — assignment language travels better than the US-specific WFH doctrine.
5. Audit old relationships: any long-running editor/designer with no signed IP terms is unfinished business; paper it retroactively with a short confirmatory assignment.

## 8. League footage, game broadcasts, and athlete NIL

### Footage: copyright belongs to the league/broadcaster

- MLB (like all major leagues) owns/controls game broadcast footage and enforces aggressively — creator accounts have been suspended for unlicensed highlight use, and MLB has historically been among the strictest leagues on YouTube.
- **Legit lanes**: (a) **MLB Film Room** — official archive of every pitch since 2017 plus historical clips to 1929, letting users build and *embed/share* reels through MLB's own tools (sharing via their embeds ≠ a license to download and re-edit into monetized videos); (b) MLB's **immediate news use** terms — short game highlights are licensed free for bona fide *news reporting* under specific conditions (not entertainment compilations); (c) direct licensing/partnership — some creators have formalized deals with MLB (effectively becoming licensed contractors).
- **Fair use for sports commentary exists but is narrow post-Warhol**: brief clips, each the direct subject of analysis (think swing-breakdown with telestration and coaching commentary), non-substitutive, favor fair use; "Top 10 plays" compilations and highlight re-cuts are same-purpose commercial use and lose. For a baseball-content operation: original filming, licensed feeds, athlete-provided footage, and genuinely analytical clip use — in that order of safety.
- **You own what you shoot** — footage filmed at your own facility of your own athletes is your copyright (with athlete releases per §4). This is the strategic answer: a training facility is a *footage factory* that owes nothing to any league.

### NIL and right of publicity: the identity layer

- **NIL/right of publicity** is state law (plus Lanham Act §43(a) false endorsement federally): a person controls *commercial* use of their name, image, likeness, voice, and persona.
- **NIL and copyright are independent and NIL loses where they collide**: per the 2025 N.C. Business Court decision in the *NC State 1983 championship team v. NCAA* line of cases — "NIL rights protect against the misuse of identity, but they don't override copyright ownership of the medium." Athletes who competed in a broadcast event impliedly consented to appearing in that footage; the **rights holder, not the athlete, controls the footage**. A valid NIL claim needs identity misuse *independent of* the copyrighted medium (e.g., putting the athlete's face on your ad or product).
- **Practical two-permission rule**: to use game footage commercially you need the *footage owner's* license; to use an athlete's identity to sell something you need the *athlete's* consent. A gym ad cutting broadcast footage of a famous alum needs BOTH — and usually has neither.

### Training-facility specifics (Neptune Performance-shaped notes)

1. **Media releases in the intake packet** for every client; guardian signature for minors; separate opt-ins for (a) coaching/analysis video, (b) promotional/social use, (c) name+performance results in marketing. Testimonials with real names are endorsements — FTC rules from §5 apply if the athlete gets free training or compensation (disclose it).
2. **Pro/college athletes who train there**: their team, league, agency, or school NIL rules may restrict how the facility uses their image commercially. "Player X trains here" in paid marketing without a written NIL agreement is a right-of-publicity claim and possibly a false-endorsement claim. Get a simple NIL/testimonial agreement (scope, term, comp — even in-kind training credit) before featuring anyone with a name.
3. **High school/amateur athletes**: state NIL rules for HS athletes vary wildly and change fast (some states prohibit HS NIL deals entirely) — check the athlete's state before any paid promotion featuring them; when in doubt, feature them non-commercially (coaching content) only.
4. **Never imply league/team affiliation**: team logos, uniforms in marketing, "MLB-level training" phrasing — trademark and false-association exposure. Athletes wearing their own gear in incidental footage is normal; putting the logo in your ad creative is not.

## Common mistakes

1. **Trusting "royalty-free," "no copyright music," or a YouTube reupload's claim of clearance** without reading the actual license. If you can't name the licensor and scope, you don't have a license.
2. **Treating a Content ID claim as the cost of doing business** — it silently donates the video's lifetime revenue and leaves strike/lawsuit exposure open.
3. **Believing the 10-second / 30-second myth** or "it's fair use because I credited them."
4. **Reaction/commentary content that doesn't actually comment** — playback with vibes is infringement with witnesses.
5. **No guest releases, or releases signed after publication** — every unsigned appearance is a latent takedown demand, publicity claim, or co-authorship claim.
6. **Handshake brand deals** — no kill fee, no usage limits, then the brand runs the clip as a paid ad for a year and pays in 90 days.
7. **Whitelisting/paid usage given away free** inside a standard integration fee.
8. **Disclosure theater**: "#ad" in a sea of hashtags, disclosure only in the description fold, relying on the platform's paid-promotion checkbox alone.
9. **Paying freelancers without IP paper** and discovering, at acquisition due diligence or a dispute, that the back catalog's edits belong to six different editors. (Also its cousin: the editor's personal music subscription didn't cover your channel.)
10. **Naming a brand without a clearance search**, or building merch revenue on an unregistered mark until a squatter files first.
11. **Using league footage or a famous athlete's image in marketing** because "everyone does it" — leagues run automated enforcement, and publicity claims come with real damages.
12. **Ignoring a DMCA notice or reflex-filing a counter-notice** — one path forfeits the video and takes a strike; the other invites a federal lawsuit. Both deserve 24 hours of thought and often a lawyer.
13. **No copyright registrations on flagship IP**, leaving statutory damages and fee-shifting off the table exactly when a serious infringer shows up.
14. **Filming minors without guardian consent** — at a youth-facing training facility this is the fastest possible way to a serious problem.

## Questions Carl should ask

**Music & content inputs**
- "Where does every piece of music in your videos come from — can you name the license for the last five uploads?"
- "Do your editors use their own asset subscriptions, and do those licenses cover *your* channel?"
- "What's your policy when Content ID claims a video — dispute, eat it, or investigate?"

**Fair use exposure**
- "Show me your last video that used someone else's footage/photo — what was your purpose relative to theirs, and how much did you take?"
- "Are any recurring formats built on third-party material (reactions, highlights, compilations)? What's the plan if the rightsholder wakes up?"

**People on camera**
- "Do you have a signed release for every non-employee who has appeared on the channel? Where are they stored?"
- "For the facility: what does the client intake packet say about filming, minors, and promotional use — are coaching video and marketing use separate opt-ins?"

**Sponsorships & FTC**
- "Pull up your last brand contract: what are the exclusivity category and window, the usage rights term, the payment trigger, and the kill fee?"
- "What's your standard disclosure — script line and description line — and is it applied to gifted product and affiliate links too, or just paid deals?"
- "Who reviews the brand's claims copy before you record?"

**Ownership & brand**
- "Does every freelancer, past and present, have signed work-for-hire + assignment language? Which ones don't?"
- "Who legally owns the channel, the marks, and the back catalog — the entity or a founder personally?"
- "Have you run a clearance search on [brand/show/facility name], and is it filed in Class 41 (and 25 if merch exists)?"
- "Which of your works are registered with the Copyright Office? Which flagship assets should be?"

**Sports-specific**
- "Any broadcast/league footage in your content or marketing? Under what theory?"
- "Which named athletes appear in your marketing, and do you have an NIL/testimonial agreement with each — including the high schoolers' state-rule check?"

**Escalation readiness**
- "Who is your on-call media/IP lawyer, and what's the internal rule for when a DMCA notice, C&D, or FTC letter arrives?" (Right answer: acknowledged same day, nobody responds substantively without counsel, nothing deleted that could be evidence.)
