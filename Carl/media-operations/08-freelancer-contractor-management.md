# Freelancer & Contractor Management for Creative Work

## TL;DR

- **Never hire without a paid test project.** $50–$150 for a small, self-contained, representative task is the standard and the single highest-signal vetting step. Free spec work filters out the good ones, not the bad ones.
- **The brief is the quality-control system.** If a video consistently needs 3+ revision rounds, the problem is almost always the brief or scattered feedback, not the editor. Two rounds of consolidated, timestamped, written feedback should be the contractual and cultural norm.
- **Match the pricing model to scope volatility:** per-project/per-video when the format is locked and repeatable; hourly when footage or scope is unpredictable; retainer once volume hits ~4+ deliverables/month (expect a 20–25% discount vs. per-unit rates in exchange for committed volume).
- **Budget for the real cost, not the sticker price.** Documented creator budgets show actual monthly editing spend running ~40–65% above the quoted rate once revisions, rush fees, licensing, thumbnails, and platform repurposing are added. Sticker price is typically only 60–70% of true cost.
- **Onboard before assigning work, and systematize by ~5 contractors, not 50.** Manual freelancer management demonstrably breaks around 20–50 active contractors, but the habits (W-9 before payment, signed IP assignment before work, one feedback channel, talent database with performance ratings) should be installed from contractor #1.
- **Classification is a live legal risk, especially for "integral" work.** A full-time-equivalent editor who works only for you, on your schedule, editing your core product is the textbook misclassification profile under both the DOL economic-realities factors and the IRS control tests. Back-pay + doubled damages + attorney fees is the downside.
- **Treat the bench as an asset with a maintenance schedule.** Rate every engagement (quality + would-rehire), keep 2–3 vetted names per critical skill, pay fast, and stay in touch between projects. Your best freelancers have options; being the client who briefs clearly and pays on time is the retention strategy.

## 1. Why a freelance bench, and what it actually is

A creative bench is a pre-vetted, performance-rated roster of freelancers you can activate in days instead of weeks. It is the middle path between "scramble on Upwork every time" and "hire full-time before the volume justifies it."

**Why it's the default model now:**
- 28% of U.S. knowledge workers freelance or work independently, generating ~$1.5T in earnings (Upwork, 2024 study); ~75% of art/design-profession workers are freelancers.
- 68% of agencies outsource content work to freelancers (Verblio, cited by Worksuite).
- A full-time employee costs roughly **2x salary** fully loaded (benefits, taxes, equipment, idle time). A US-based full-time editor is $5,400–$8,700/mo fully loaded vs. $1,500–$3,500/mo for a dedicated offshore editor or a few hundred to ~$600 per video freelance.
- The strategic logic for a small media company: keep the **taste functions** in-house (creative direction, thumbnails/titles strategy, final QC, sponsor relations) and flex the **throughput functions** (editing, motion graphics, clipping, design production) on the bench.

**The bench-depth rule:** for every skill that blocks publishing (long-form editor, shorts editor, thumbnail designer, motion designer), maintain **2–3 vetted, recently-active options**. One is a single point of failure; a bench of one is not a bench.

**The scaling threshold:** structured manual management (spreadsheets, email, one owner) works up to roughly **20–50 active contractors**; past that, administrative overhead compounds faster than a human can absorb, and you need a system — a talent database with compliance status, contracts linked to payments, and automated onboarding. (Worksuite benchmark: manual onboarding averaged **8 hours per contractor** before systemization; a searchable rated pool cuts re-engagement from multi-day sourcing to a 30-minute shortlist.) Small teams don't need the software, but they need the same data model early: skills, rate, availability, compliance docs, project history, performance rating.

## 2. Sourcing and vetting

### 2.1 Sourcing channels, ranked by signal quality

1. **Referrals from other creators/producers** — highest hit rate; a working editor's peer network is pre-filtered for reliability.
2. **Niche job boards** (YT Jobs / ytjobs.co for YouTube-specific roles, industry Discords, editor communities) — candidates already understand the format; over 2,000 creators have posted to YTjobs.
3. **Inbound from your own audience** — creators' audiences contain editors who already know the channel's style; often the fastest style-match.
4. **General marketplaces** (Upwork, Fiverr, Twine) — widest funnel, lowest average quality, highest vetting cost. Marketplaces "win on sticker price, lose on management overhead."
5. **Agencies / creative-as-a-service** — win on handoff speed, lose on brand-voice drift (rotating editors never internalize the channel's style).

### 2.2 The vetting funnel (playbook)

1. **Portfolio screen for *results in your format*, not general skill.** A brilliant wedding-video reel tells you nothing about retention editing. For a YouTube editor: ask for 2–3 videos they cut in a comparable format and, ideally, what the retention/performance outcome was.
2. **Short async screen (15–30 min or written):** communication clarity, tools, availability, timezone overlap, rate expectations, current client load. You are vetting *reliability signals* as much as craft.
3. **Paid test project — non-negotiable.** Small, self-contained, representative: one real video from your actual raw footage with your actual brief. $50–$150 is standard and is not an insult; top talent won't do free spec work, and asking for it starts the relationship badly.
4. **Grade the test on four axes:**
   - **Craft** — pacing, story sense, audio, brand adherence (weight ~40%)
   - **Brief-following** — did they hit the explicit requirements? (~25%)
   - **Communication** — questions asked, updates given, how they handled ambiguity (~20%)
   - **Deadline** — did it land on time? Missing a deadline on a tiny test is the single strongest predictor of delivery problems at scale (~15%, but treat a miss as near-disqualifying)
5. **Start with a 30-day / 3-deliverable probation** at agreed rates before any retainer commitment. Retainers are earned, not offered on day one.

**What a trial reveals that interviews can't:** deadline behavior, revision temperament (defensive vs. curious), whether their "portfolio style" survives contact with your footage, and how much hand-holding round one actually requires.

### 2.3 Rate-tier map for vetting expectations

What you pay predicts what supervision the work needs (2025–2026 US benchmarks):

| Tier | Hourly | What you get |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | $20–$45 | 1–2 yrs client work; fine for social clips, basic talking-head, recurring YouTube edits — needs a tight brief and QC |
| Mid | $45–$85 | Reliable retention editing, light motion graphics; most common US freelance band is $40–$80 (Cutjamm 2025 survey) |
| Senior | $85–$150+ | Story-level judgment, minimal supervision, niche expertise |
| Specialist (motion gfx, color) | $100–$250 | Bought by the deliverable, not managed day-to-day |

Offshore differentials are real and stable: Philippines $5–$20/hr, LatAm $10–$25/hr, Eastern Europe $25–$50/hr, South Asia $15–$30/hr. One documented comparison: a mid-senior editor hired in Argentina at $2,000/mo vs. a $5,500/mo US equivalent — 64% saving at comparable skill. The tradeoff is timezone, cultural/format fluency, and communication overhead — which the brief-and-feedback system (below) must absorb.

## 3. Briefs that work

The brief is where quality is won or lost. "The more detail upfront, the fewer revision rounds later" is the most consistently repeated finding across every practitioner source. One to two pages; concise-and-specific beats long-and-vague.

### 3.1 The working brief template (creative production version)

Adapted from the CMO Alliance framework + video-specific additions:

1. **Project ID** — name, point of contact (ONE person owns feedback), collaborators
2. **Dates** — brief date, first-cut due, revision windows, final delivery, go-live
3. **Objective** — what this piece must achieve (view goal, sponsor deliverable, launch support); one sentence
4. **Audience** — who it's for and what they already know
5. **Key message / story spine** — the one thing the viewer should take away; for video, the intended emotional arc
6. **Deliverables spec** — exact outputs: length target, aspect ratio(s), resolution, file format, caption files, cutdowns/verticals, thumbnail (yes/no)
7. **Creative direction** — tone, pacing reference ("cut like [link to reference video]"), 2–3 reference links; references beat adjectives every time
8. **Brand guidelines** — fonts, lower-thirds, color, logo animations, music library, what's *banned*
9. **Assets & access** — raw footage location, project file conventions, music/SFX licenses, b-roll sources
10. **Must-have vs. nice-to-have vs. out-of-scope** — the scope-creep firewall; name it explicitly
11. **Revision policy** — "2 rounds of consolidated written feedback included; further rounds billed at $X" — stated in the brief, not discovered in an argument
12. **Approval workflow** — who reviews, in what order, and the deadline for each reviewer's notes

### 3.2 Brief failure modes

- **Adjectives instead of references.** "Make it punchy" produces a coin-flip. A link to a specific video with "match this pacing at 0:00–1:30" produces the thing you wanted.
- **Multiple feedback owners with no consolidation.** The #1 cause of 5-round revision spirals. All stakeholder notes funnel through one person who reconciles contradictions *before* the editor sees anything.
- **Scope defined by deliverable name only.** "One YouTube video" without length, motion-graphics density, or sound-design expectations is not a scope — it's a future dispute.
- **Rigid-brief theater.** The brief is a living document until execution begins; a pre-work alignment call (15 min) catches misunderstandings a document never will. After work starts, changes are change orders.
- **No out-of-scope list.** If you never wrote down what's excluded, everything is arguably included.

**Systemize it:** reusable brief templates per format (long-form, short, sponsor integration, thumbnail) + SOPs for file naming, folder structure, and handoff. This is what makes an offshore or junior editor perform a tier above their rate.

## 4. Rate structures: per-project vs. hourly vs. retainer

### 4.1 Decision rule

- **Per-project / per-video** — use when scope is locked: a format-consistent series, fixed length, known footage volume. Best cost predictability; puts efficiency risk on the freelancer (which good ones price in).
- **Hourly (time & materials with a cap)** — use when footage or scope is variable: multi-cam events, documentaries, unpredictable interview lengths, "we'll figure it out in the edit" projects. Requires burn-rate monitoring, timesheet approval, and pre-cap alerts. Never open-ended hourly with no cap.
- **Per-finished-minute** — the video-industry hybrid: $50–$150/finished minute for professional long-form work ($1.25–$10 basic, $10–$50 professional, $50+ advanced/motion-heavy per ContentBeta's bands). Scales price with output length, so pair it with a length target or you're incentivizing bloat.
- **Monthly retainer** — use once volume is predictable at ~4+ deliverables/month. Standard economics: commit to volume, get **20–25% off per-video rates**; freelancer gets predictable income (retainer clients carry 3–5x the lifetime value of one-off clients, which is why good freelancers will discount for it). Define what a retainer month *contains* (N videos of X type, or N hours) and what happens to unused capacity — "use it or lose it" with a rollover cap of ~25% is a fair middle ground.
- **Hybrid** — the model most healthy small agencies converge on: projects to start a relationship, retainer to run it, one-off projects layered on top for out-of-scope work.

### 4.2 Benchmark tables (2025–2026)

**Per-video, YouTube-format:**

| Work type | Range |
|---|---|
| Shorts, entry-level/template | $5–$25 |
| Shorts, custom format | $25–$100 |
| Shorts, motion-graphics heavy | $100–$300 |
| Talking-head/vlog long-form, mid-tier | $30–$150 |
| Retention-edited professional long-form | $200–$600 |
| High-production ("MrBeast-style") | $500–$2,000+ |
| Market average, all long-form | ~$305/video |

**Monthly / dedicated:**

| Arrangement | Range |
|---|---|
| Part-time offshore (~20 hrs/wk) | $800–$1,800/mo |
| Full-time dedicated offshore | $1,500–$3,500/mo |
| Full-time US editor, fully loaded | $5,400–$8,700/mo |
| Documented creator retainer example | $2,800/mo for 4 videos |
| Salaried reference points | MrBeast editors avg ~$59.5K (90th pct ~$125K); Ali Abdaal listing £30–40K + 3 yrs exp |

**Other units:** day rates $300–$3,500 (most land $500–$1,500); typical freelance project totals: social clips $100–$500, YouTube/corporate/explainer $500–$2,500; traditional agency projects $4,000–$20,000+; creative-as-a-service subscriptions ~$3,000/mo flat.

### 4.3 Hidden cost multipliers — the real budget

Documented add-ons that push actual spend above quotes:

- Revision overages beyond included rounds: **$50–$150 per video** (or $50–$100 per extra round)
- Stock footage / music licensing: $20–$80 per video
- Rush turnaround (24–48h): **+25–50%**
- Platform repurposing (Shorts/Reels/TikTok cutdowns): $15–$40 per platform
- Thumbnail design if bundled: $20–$50 per video

Case study: a creator budgeting $2,100/mo averaged **$2,600/mo actual over 18 months**. Plan on sticker price being **60–70% of true monthly cost** and budget accordingly.

### 4.4 Understanding the freelancer's side (so your offers land)

Competent freelancers price from: target income + ~30% for self-employment taxes/costs + ~20% for non-billable time. A "$50/hr" freelancer is netting roughly what a ~$30/hr employee nets. Lowballing below this math doesn't get you a deal; it gets you the freelancers whose math doesn't work — i.e., the ones who churn, over-commit, or quietly deprioritize you.

## 5. Turnaround SLAs and capacity planning

Practitioner sources are thin on formalized creative SLAs, so this is the synthesized operating standard:

- **Set turnaround per deliverable class, in the contract:** e.g., first cut of a standard long-form video in 3–5 business days from asset handoff; revision rounds returned within 2 business days; shorts in 24–48h. CaaS benchmarks show 24–48h turnarounds are achievable for templated work; traditional agencies run 4–8 weeks — your bench should live near the fast end.
- **The SLA clock starts at complete handoff**, not at "we sent most of the footage." Define asset-complete explicitly (footage + brief + music selections + graphics assets), or every delay becomes a blame negotiation.
- **Your review SLA matters as much as theirs.** Commit to returning consolidated feedback within a fixed window (24–48h). A freelancer who delivers on time and then waits 6 days for notes has been de-scheduled — and your next deadline pays for it.
- **Rush is a priced product, not a favor:** +25–50% for 24–48h turnaround, agreed in advance.
- **Capacity rule of thumb:** a solid full-time-equivalent editor sustains roughly 3–5 standard long-form videos/month or daily shorts output, format depending. Book bench freelancers to ~80% of stated availability; the last 20% is your buffer for revisions and slippage.
- **Publish a pipeline forecast to your bench.** Freelancers plan weeks out; telling your roster "3 videos landing week of the 15th" converts your bench from theoretical to actually-available. This is also the retention move that costs nothing.

## 6. Feedback loops and quality control

### 6.1 The feedback system (Frame.io-style discipline, tool-agnostic)

1. **One channel, always.** All review happens in one place (Frame.io, Vimeo Review, GoVisually — anything with timestamped comments). Email threads, DMs, and verbal notes are where revisions go to multiply. If a decision happens on a call, someone writes it into the review tool before it counts.
2. **Timestamped, one point per comment.** "At 0:12–0:25 the pacing drags — tighten to match [reference]" is executable. "The intro feels off" is a séance. Many short specific comments beat few long ones; annotation/drawing tools for graphic-placement notes.
3. **Explain the why.** "Cut this joke — it delays the payoff the title promised" teaches the editor your taste; bare instructions don't. Feedback that explains reasoning is how round-3 problems become round-1 non-problems next video.
4. **Consolidate before sending.** All stakeholders review the same cut; one owner merges and de-conflicts notes into a single batch. Drip-fed feedback is the #1 generator of wasted revision work.
5. **Tag priority.** Must-fix vs. nice-to-have on every note. Editors triage; you get the critical fixes fast.
6. **Two rounds is the design target.** With a good brief and consolidated feedback, most videos need 1–2 rounds. Consistently exceeding 3 means the brief lacked direction or the feedback process is vague/scattered/contradictory — fix the system, don't blame the editor.
7. **Escalate to synchronous when written breaks down.** If a note has bounced twice, get on a call, decide, then document the decision in the tool.

### 6.2 QC checklist (final gate before "approved")

Run every deliverable against a fixed checklist — it catches the errors feedback rounds miss and reduces total rounds:

- Audio levels consistent (dialogue ~-14 LUFS target for YouTube; no clipping; music ducked under speech)
- Color consistency across cuts/cameras
- Text/graphics: spelling, names, titles, sponsor terms exactly as contracted
- Brand elements: correct logo, fonts, lower-thirds, intro/outro per guidelines
- Licensing: every music track and stock clip cleared for the platform
- Deliverable spec: length, aspect ratio, resolution, captions, export settings
- Sponsor/legal: disclosure present, mandated talking points included, banned claims absent

### 6.3 Closing the loop over time

- After each project, log a 2-field rating: **quality (1–5)** and **would-rehire (y/n)**. This is the minimum viable performance system and the entire basis of a functioning bench.
- Share performance context back to the freelancer ("that video hit 200K, retention held through your cold open") — editors who see outcomes edit for outcomes.
- Quarterly, review the bench: who's rising to preferred-tier, who's drifting, which skills have <2 vetted names.

## 7. Onboarding and contractor portals

### 7.1 Onboarding checklist — complete *before* first assignment

Legal/financial (blocking — no work starts without these):
- [ ] Signed contractor agreement: scope, rate structure, revision policy, turnaround SLAs, termination terms
- [ ] **IP assignment** — for commissioned creative work, do not rely on "work made for hire" language alone (US copyright law only recognizes it for specific commissioned categories with a signed writing); include explicit present-tense assignment of all rights in the deliverables. This clause is the difference between owning your channel's back catalog and licensing it from twelve ex-editors.
- [ ] NDA / confidentiality (unreleased content, sponsor terms, analytics)
- [ ] W-9 (domestic) or W-8BEN / W-8BEN-E (international individual / entity) — collect *before first payment*, not at 1099 season
- [ ] Classification documented (see §9) and payment terms agreed (net-15 or net-30; faster payment is a competitive advantage in talent retention)

Operational (day-one productivity):
- [ ] Access: asset storage (Drive/LucidLink/etc.), review tool, communication channel, brand kit
- [ ] Brief template, SOPs, file-naming conventions, example "gold standard" past deliverables
- [ ] One named point of contact and the feedback workflow explained
- [ ] First assignment queued so onboarding flows directly into work

Why the ceremony matters: structured onboarding measurably improves time-to-productivity and error rates, and 69% of contractors report being more likely to stay engaged after a good onboarding experience. Manual onboarding averages ~8 hrs/contractor; templates and e-signature collapse most of it.

### 7.2 Contractor portals

Once you run more than a handful of regulars, a portal (bought or built) should give each contractor self-serve access to: their active assignments and briefs, asset upload/download, status updates and blockers, hours or deliverable logging tied to the pay period, document signing, payment status visibility, and notifications. The operating principles:

- **Contractors see only their own world** — scoped folder access, no all-company visibility (both a security and a professionalism matter).
- **Payment transparency is retention.** "When do I get paid and for what" answered by the system, not by awkward emails.
- **Hours logging should mirror the pay cadence** (e.g., bi-weekly periods aligned to payroll runs) so invoicing is a report, not a negotiation.
- **A short guided tour on first login** beats a PDF handbook nobody reads.

*(Relevance note: Mayday Studio's freelancer portal — dashboard, assignments, submit-work upload, documents/signing, bi-weekly hours, payment profile, onboarding tour — is a textbook implementation of this pattern; the leverage now is in the surrounding process discipline: briefs, ratings, SLAs.)*

## 8. Running the bench over time

- **Tier the roster:** Preferred (proven, first call, gets pipeline previews), Active (in rotation), Prospect (vetted but unproven), Retired (rated no-rehire — keep the record so nobody re-sources them in 18 months).
- **Track per freelancer:** skills, rate, availability window, compliance-doc status, project history, quality rating, rehire flag. This is a spreadsheet until ~20 contractors; then it's software.
- **Stay warm between projects:** a pipeline note every few weeks to preferred-tier freelancers ("two sponsor videos landing next month — hold capacity?") keeps your bench real. Freelancers book 4–8 weeks out; silence means they're booked when you need them.
- **Pay fast and never late.** Across every practitioner source, on-time payment is the top-cited retention factor. Late payment is how small companies lose their best contractors to clients with worse briefs and better accounting.
- **Respect the contractor boundary operationally too:** define deliverables, not methods; don't mandate set hours or required meetings (this is also a classification safeguard — see §9). Micromanaging a contractor is simultaneously bad management and legal evidence against you.
- **Raise rates proactively for keepers.** A 10% unprompted raise for a two-year proven editor is cheaper than one bad replacement cycle (re-sourcing, test projects, 5–10 videos of style ramp-up).

## 9. 1099 compliance basics (US)

*Not legal advice; the framework every operator must know. Get counsel for edge cases.*

### 9.1 The three tests that matter

**DOL / FLSA — 2024 "economic realities" rule (six factors, totality of circumstances, no factor dispositive):**
1. Worker's opportunity for profit or loss depending on managerial skill
2. Relative investments by worker vs. business (does the worker invest in their own equipment/tools?)
3. Degree of permanence of the relationship (indefinite/continuous → employee-leaning)
4. Nature and degree of control (schedule, supervision, ability to work for others)
5. Whether the work is **integral to the employer's business** (an editor at a video company: yes)
6. Skill and initiative (specialized skill used with business-like initiative → contractor-leaning)

**Status note (as of mid-2026):** DOL announced in May 2025 that its enforcement staff would not apply the 2024 rule while it reconsiders, and proposed a replacement rule in February 2026 that is expected to be more classification-friendly. But the 2024 rule remained on the books for **private lawsuits**, and state law is unaffected — so run your practices to the stricter standard, not the news cycle.

**IRS common-law test (three categories):** behavioral control (do you direct how work is done?), financial control (unreimbursed expenses, own tools, market availability), relationship type (contracts, benefits, permanence, integrality). Governs employment-tax exposure.

**State ABC tests (California and others):** worker is an employee unless (A) free from control, (B) work is **outside the usual course of the hiring entity's business**, and (C) worker has an independently established trade. Prong B is brutal for media companies hiring editors — editing *is* the business. California's AB2257 carved out exemptions for certain freelance creative professional services (writers, photographers, some editors/content contributors) under conditions (worker negotiates rates, sets own hours, works for multiple clients); whether a given video editor fits is a counsel question, not a vibe.

### 9.2 The risk profile in plain terms

The dangerous contractor is the one who: works ~full-time hours exclusively for you, on your schedule, using your tools/systems, doing your core product, indefinitely, under your direction. That person is an employee wearing a 1099. Consequences of misclassification: back overtime for hours over 40/week, **doubled (liquidated) damages over a 2–3 year lookback, plus attorney fees**; plus IRS back employment taxes and penalties; plus state penalties. A worker cannot waive employee status by agreeing in writing to be a contractor — the contract label counts for almost nothing against the facts.

**Practical guardrails that keep a contractor a contractor:**
- Pay per deliverable or per project where possible (profit/loss opportunity), not de facto salary
- They use their own equipment and software licenses
- They control when and where they work; you control what and by when
- They serve (or are free to serve) other clients; don't demand exclusivity
- Engagements are defined by SOW, renewed deliberately — not silently permanent
- No employee treatment: no benefits, no required all-hands, no performance reviews framed as employment
- **The honest test:** if you need someone 40 hrs/week, indefinitely, embedded in daily operations — hire them or use an employer-of-record. The classification you want and the working relationship you want may not be compatible; face that early.

### 9.3 Paperwork mechanics

- **W-9 before first payment** from every US contractor; W-8BEN/W-8BEN-E from foreign individuals/entities (generally no 1099 for foreign contractors performing work outside the US, but the form proves it).
- **1099-NEC** filed for US contractors above the reporting threshold, due to contractors and IRS by **January 31**. (The threshold, $600 for years, was raised to $2,000 for payments made after 2025 under 2025 tax legislation — confirm current-year rules with your accountant; collecting W-9s from everyone regardless is the safe habit.)
- Payments via credit card / third-party settlement (some platforms) shift reporting to 1099-K by the processor — know which of your payment rails you report on.
- Classify **before work starts**, document the analysis, and re-run it when scope changes (the 20-hr/week project that quietly became full-time is the classic drift).
- For international volume, an **Agent of Record / Employer of Record** service handles local compliance without entity setup.
- Audit annually: everyone receiving a 1099 gets re-checked against the factors. This is a one-hour meeting that prevents a six-figure problem.

*(Relevance note: this section applies double to a training facility — fitness/sports coaching is a classic misclassification enforcement zone. Coaches who teach on the facility's schedule, at its location, with its clients and equipment, are very hard to defend as 1099s under prong-B-style tests.)*

## 10. Common mistakes

1. **Hiring on portfolio alone, skipping the paid test.** The reel shows their best-ever work under ideal conditions; the test shows their Tuesday.
2. **Vague briefs, then blaming the freelancer for the output.** "Not quite what we had in mind" is a brief failure ~80% of the time.
3. **Unlimited implicit revisions.** No stated revision cap → the client with the most anxiety sets the round count → margins and goodwill evaporate on both sides.
4. **Drip-fed, multi-source feedback.** Three stakeholders sending separate contradictory notes across email, Slack, and calls. Consolidate or suffer.
5. **Budgeting the sticker price.** Real spend runs 1.4–1.65x quotes once revisions, rush, licensing, and repurposing land. The 18-month case: $2,100 budgeted, $2,600 actual.
6. **Hiring junior/cheap to save money on core deliverables.** Heavy-oversight juniors cost more than their rate saves — in redo work, missed deadlines, and your attention. Cheap is expensive on the critical path.
7. **A bench of one.** Your only editor's vacation, illness, or better offer becomes your publishing gap.
8. **Every department inventing its own freelancer process.** The scale-killer isn't complexity, it's volume compounded by inconsistency. One intake, one contract template, one talent database.
9. **No IP assignment in writing before work starts.** Retrofitting rights from a departed contractor is somewhere between expensive and impossible.
10. **Treating retainers as "unlimited work."** Undefined retainer scope erodes margins for the freelancer and quality for you; both sides eventually defect.
11. **Slow payment.** The silent bench-killer. The best freelancers fire bad-paying clients without telling them — they just become "unavailable."
12. **The full-time contractor.** Exclusive, scheduled, permanent, integral — a misclassification suit with a Frame.io login. Convert them or restructure the relationship.
13. **Never sharing outcomes.** Editors who never learn what performed can't develop channel judgment; you pay senior rates forever for junior context.

## 11. Questions Carl should ask

**Diagnosing the current state:**
1. How many freelancers touched your content in the last 90 days, and can you show me a list with rates, ratings, and W-9 status? (If the answer is "let me check some emails," there is no system.)
2. What's your average revision-round count per video? If it's over 2, show me the brief that started the last 4-round spiral.
3. When your main editor disappears tomorrow, who cuts next week's video? How long until they're at full style-match?
4. What did editing actually cost per published video last quarter — including revisions, rush fees, thumbnails, and licensing — vs. what you thought it cost?
5. Where does feedback live? How many channels did notes travel through on the last project?

**Diagnosing quality control:**
6. Is there a written QC checklist a deliverable must pass before "approved," or is approval a feeling?
7. Who consolidates stakeholder feedback before the editor sees it? One name, or a shrug?
8. Do your editors know how their videos performed? When did you last tell one?

**Diagnosing rates and structure:**
9. Which pricing model is each freelancer on, and does it match scope volatility — or is everyone hourly because nobody decided?
10. If you're paying per video: has volume reached the ~4/month threshold where a retainer would cut 20–25% off unit cost?
11. When did your best freelancer last get a raise you offered before they asked?

**Diagnosing compliance:**
12. Does anyone on a 1099 work for you essentially full-time, exclusively, on your schedule? For how long? (Then: what state are they in?)
13. Do all contractor agreements contain a present-tense IP assignment, signed before work started? Including the editor from 2023?
14. Who collects W-9s and when — before first payment, or in a January panic?
15. For the training-facility side: are coaches 1099s, and would their working pattern survive an ABC test?

**Diagnosing the pipeline relationship:**
16. Do freelancers hear about upcoming work before it's urgent, or does every assignment arrive as an emergency?
17. What's your actual payment turnaround from invoice to money-in-account? Have you asked a freelancer whether that's good?
