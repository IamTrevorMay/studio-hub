# Content Unit Economics & Production Budgeting

## TL;DR

- **Every video needs a P&L.** Fully loaded cost per video = direct costs + amortized gear/studio + allocated salaries + your time at a real hourly rate. If a business can't state its cost per video within 20%, it isn't managing a content business — it's funding a hobby.
- **Time is usually the biggest line item.** A typical long-form video absorbs 15–25 hours of labor across planning, filming, editing, and packaging. Price that labor at loaded cost (salary × 1.25–1.4 ÷ productive hours), not zero.
- **Fixed costs are divided by output — so output volume is a margin lever.** A $15K/month overhead base costs $3,750/video at 4 videos/month and $1,250 at 12. The single fastest way to fix per-unit economics is often to publish more from the same fixed base, not to cut the base.
- **Measure revenue per video over its full life, not at 30 days.** Long-form search/evergreen content earns for years; the 30-day snapshot systematically undervalues it and overvalues trend-chasing content.
- **Cheap content frequently beats expensive content on ROI.** A $1,500 clip driving 300 conversions ($5/conversion) beats a $50,000 brand film driving 200 ($250/conversion) by 50x. Judge formats by cost-per-outcome, never by how impressive they look.
- **Quality vs. quantity is a false choice.** The right question: what is the maximum frequency at which you can hold your quality bar? Invest in production value only where the audience punishes its absence (audio first, always).
- **The scaling red flag pattern:** costs growing faster than revenue per video, headcount added before utilization is proven, one revenue stream carrying everything, and payment terms (net-30/60/90) starving cash while the P&L looks fine. MrBeast's content arm lost up to $120M in a year on $224M of content revenue — survivable only because Feastables and outside capital carry it. Small operators don't get that option.

## 1. The Core Unit: Fully Loaded Cost Per Video

### 1.1 The formula

```
Fully loaded cost per video =
    Direct costs                (crew day rates, freelance edit, travel, props, music licenses, thumbnails, paid boosts)
  + Amortized capital costs     (cameras, lenses, lights, computers, studio buildout ÷ useful life ÷ monthly output)
  + Allocated fixed overhead    (rent, software subscriptions, insurance, utilities ÷ monthly output)
  + Allocated salaries          (loaded payroll × % of time spent on this format ÷ monthly output)
  + Founder/creator time        (hours × imputed rate — never zero)
```

Three cost layers, three behaviors:

| Layer | Behavior | Examples | How to allocate |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Direct (variable)** | Scales 1:1 with each video | Freelance editor per project, location fee, guest travel, stock/music license | Attach to the specific video |
| **Semi-fixed** | Steps up in chunks | Full-time editor, studio lease, software seats | Divide by videos produced in period |
| **Fixed/capital** | Bought once, used for years | Camera bodies, lighting, acoustic treatment, edit workstations | Straight-line amortize over useful life, then divide by monthly output |

### 1.2 Amortizing gear — the mechanics

Straight-line is fine for internal decision-making (nobody needs MACRS for a Sony FX3):

- **Monthly cost of a purchase = price ÷ useful life in months.** Camera bodies/lenses: 36–60 months. Computers: 36 months. Lights/audio: 48–60 months. Studio buildout: life of lease.
- **Per-video cost = monthly cost ÷ videos published per month.**
- Subscriptions need no amortization — the monthly fee just divides across output.
- Net out expected resale value on gear that holds value (lenses especially).

Worked micro-example (from the creator gear amortization framework): a $1,200 camera + $450 lens + $120 mic (all 36-month life) + $22/mo editing software = **$71/month kit cost = $8.90/video at 8 videos/month.** At 2 videos/month the same kit costs $35.58/video — 4x worse — with zero change in spending. **Output rate, not purchase price, dominates per-unit gear cost.**

Break-even on a new purchase: *videos needed to justify it = price ÷ target per-video cost*, which equals useful-life months × monthly output. An $800 lens at 8 videos/month over 48 months touches ~384 videos ≈ $2/video. Frame every gear request this way: "this costs $X per video for the next N years — what does it earn per video?"

### 1.3 Hidden costs add 30–50%

Growing channels report $200–500/month of "invisible" spend beyond the obvious gear line: music licensing, thumbnail tools, cloud storage, backup drives, analytics tools, electricity/internet upgrades, insurance. Budget hidden costs as a standing 30–50% uplift on visible tool/gear costs until actuals replace the estimate.

### 1.4 Salaries and time — the line item everyone hides

- **Loaded cost multiplier:** salary × 1.25–1.4 covers payroll taxes, benefits, tools, and management drag.
- **Productive-hours divisor:** a full-time editor gives ~1,400–1,600 truly productive hours/year, not 2,080. A $70K editor is ~$88K loaded ≈ **$55–63/productive hour** — nearly identical to freelance editing market rates of $60–150/hr, which is why in-house only wins at volume (see §7).
- **Founder time is the most-lied-about number in creator businesses.** If the founder-creator spends 20 hours on a video and their opportunity-cost rate is $150/hr, that's $3,000 of cost the "we made it for $500" story omits. Impute it. Decisions change when you do — especially "should I edit this myself."
- Typical time budget per long-form video: **15–25 hours** across planning, filming, editing, packaging, optimization. Track it per format for at least one month per year; the per-format numbers diverge wildly (a talking-head video vs. a documentary can be 6 hours vs. 80).

### 1.5 The studio/facility utilization trap

Facilities (this generalizes directly from recording studios to content studios and training facilities like a baseball lab):

- Example math: $213K of equipment capex on 5-year straight-line = $3,550/month of asset cost. Add engineer salary + rent and total fixed overhead hits ~$15.2K/month → you need **127 billed hours at $120/hr just to break even.**
- **Nobody hits 100% utilization.** At a realistic 70% utilization and $19K total monthly cost, effective cost per available hour is ~$62 — your floor price. Rule of thumb from studio operators: if salaried staff utilization drops below **~60% of available hours**, operating leverage is dangerously high and one soft quarter puts you underwater.
- Facility pricing floor: `(total monthly fixed cost ÷ realistically billable hours)` — then price above it, never at it.

### 1.6 What the accountants do (and the lesson in it)

Netflix capitalizes content onto the balance sheet and amortizes it as cost of revenue on an *accelerated* curve — officially, >90% of a title's cost amortized within four years, front-loaded because viewing concentrates at release. Films amortize faster than series. Content amortization ran 54% of revenue in FY2024, down from 62% in 2019.

The forensic critique (Behind the Balance Sheet) is the cautionary tale: analysts modeling Netflix's actual write-offs find only ~30% amortized in year one and ~50% by year two — much slower than the stated policy — the average useful life of content assets roughly doubled over a few years, impairments are reviewed "in aggregate" rather than per title (masking individual flops), and ~40% of annual content cash spend now relates to unreleased future titles. Every one of those levers pushes reported profit above cash reality.

**Carl's takeaways for small operators:**
1. **Run the business on cash content spend, not amortization.** Capitalizing content is an accounting presentation; the money left the bank when you made the video.
2. If you do amortize internally (e.g., spreading a $30K documentary's cost across the months it earns), front-load it — most content earns most of its lifetime revenue early, evergreen/search content being the honorable exception.
3. **Stretching amortization assumptions to make margins look better is the #1 way media companies lie to themselves.** Watch for it in any content P&L you're shown.

## 2. Revenue Per Video and ROI

### 2.1 The revenue stack, with 2025–2026 benchmarks

**AdSense/RPM (long-form YouTube)** — $0.83–$28 RPM depending on niche; ~$0.018/view (~$18/1K views) is a commonly cited blended average but niche dominates:

| Niche | CPM range | Midpoint |
|---|---|---|
| Finance & business | $20–50 | $32 |
| Technology | $8–22 | $14 |
| Education | $5–18 | $12 |
| Health & fitness | $6–18 | $12 |
| Sports/travel-adjacent | $4–10 | $7 |
| Food | $3–8 | $5 |
| Gaming | $1.5–5 | $3 |
| Entertainment | $1.5–4 | $2.5 |

Finance pays ~33x entertainment per impression. **Niche choice is a unit-economics decision, not just a creative one.** Shorts pay $0.01–$0.07 per 1K views — two orders of magnitude below long-form; Shorts are a discovery expense, not a revenue line.

**Sponsorships** — the margin engine for most mid-size channels. 2025–26 per-video YouTube integration benchmarks: nano (1–10K subs) ~$289; micro (10–50K) ~$1,575 (range $2K–15K per deal in practice); mid-tier (50–500K) ~$14,400 ($15K–75K at the strong end); macro (500K–1M) ~$39K; mega (1M+) $105K+. YouTube rates run ~4x Facebook at every tier. Engagement beats subscriber count: 100K subs at 2% engagement out-earns 500K at 0.2%. Brands increasingly buy on effective CPM of *organic* views — a $40K integration delivering 600K organic views is a ~$67 CPM buy, which brands justify by adding paid amplification at ~$12 CPM to blend the number down.

**Backend (owned) revenue** — memberships, courses, merch, services, facility revenue. This is where margins live but expectations need discipline: Graham Stephan reported $1.6M in a year with only ~$2K attributable to a merch push; merch works with strong persona-product fit (Emma Chamberlain coffee, Feastables), not by default. Ryan Gum's $1.33M creator year split across six streams (courses $765K, sponsorships $182K, affiliates $177K, ads $135K, community $71K) is the healthier archetype: ads were only ~10%.

**Reality check on ads-only economics:** Colin & Samir reported <$17K AdSense on 5.6M views. Ad revenue alone supports almost no professionally-produced content. If the model is "views × RPM pays for production," check the math before anything else — it usually doesn't close.

### 2.2 ROI formulas per content piece

1. **Direct ROI:** `(lifetime attributable revenue − fully loaded cost) ÷ fully loaded cost`. Use a 12-month lifetime window minimum for evergreen formats; 60–90 days for trend formats.
2. **Contribution per video:** `attributable revenue − direct (variable) costs only`. This tells you whether making *one more* video is worth it, independent of the fixed base.
3. **Cost per outcome:** `format cost ÷ outcomes attributed to that format` (subs, leads, trials, facility signups, merch orders). The canonical example: a $50,000 brand film → 200 conversions = $250 each; a $1,500 shoppable clip → 300 conversions = $5 each. The cheap format wins 50x. **Never let production pride override cost-per-outcome.**
4. **Effective CPM earned:** `total revenue per video ÷ (views/1000)`. Compare against what it costs to buy those views with ads (~$10–15 CPM); if your content earns above its "buy-equivalent," the engine compounds.
5. **Payback period per video:** days until cumulative attributable revenue exceeds fully loaded cost. Track the *median* per format. Sponsored videos pay back at publish (or before, if pre-sold); pure-AdSense videos may never.

### 2.3 Attribution honesty

Most content revenue is only loosely attributable. Practical hierarchy: (a) direct — sponsorship on that video, affiliate links, tracked promo codes; (b) assisted — traffic/leads the video demonstrably sent; (c) portfolio — brand/audience growth. Report (a) and (b) per video; treat (c) as a channel-level number. Do not let anyone defend a format purely on layer (c) for more than two quarters.

## 3. Margin Math for a Content Business

### 3.1 The per-video contribution model

```
Contribution margin per video = revenue per video − variable cost per video
Break-even output = total monthly fixed costs ÷ contribution margin per video
```

Worked example — small YouTube-first media company:

- Fixed base: $18K/month (2 salaried staff loaded $13K, studio+software+insurance $3K, gear amortization $2K)
- Format A (sponsored long-form): revenue $6,500/video ($5K sponsor + $800 AdSense lifetime + $700 affiliate), variable cost $1,800 → **contribution $4,700**
- Format B (unsponsored long-form): revenue $1,200, variable cost $1,400 → **contribution −$200** (survives only as audience-growth spend, capped and budgeted as marketing)
- Break-even: $18,000 ÷ $4,700 ≈ **3.8 sponsored videos/month**. Everything above that is margin; every Format B video is a deliberate investment, not "content."

This framing kills the most common self-deception: averaging a profitable format with a money-losing one into a single blended "cost per video" that hides which one is carrying the business.

### 3.2 Pricing your own inventory (sponsorships)

Cost-plus floor from podcast/creator pricing practice: `rate = (production cost + promotion cost + opportunity cost) × 1.5–2.0`. Then check against market CPM comps for your tier/niche and take the higher. If a sponsor's rate doesn't clear your fully loaded cost per video × 1.5, the deal subsidizes the brand with your fixed base.

### 3.3 Portfolio math: content is hit-driven

Per-video returns follow a power law — a minority of videos produce a majority of views and revenue. Implications:

- **Judge formats on cohort medians AND the hit rate**, not single-video anecdotes. A format is healthy if the median video roughly breaks even and 1-in-5 to 1-in-10 significantly outperforms.
- **Budget at the portfolio level:** a quarterly slate of N videos with a total budget beats per-video approvals; give hits fast-follow budget (sequels, cutdowns, paid boost) since success is your cheapest signal.
- Netflix's front-loaded amortization exists because most content earns early; your evergreen search content is the exception — it justifies longer payback windows and steadier reinvestment.

### 3.4 Cash flow ≠ P&L

Even MrBeast runs cash-negative stretches despite nine-figure revenue: AdSense pays 30–60 days after views, brand deals settle net-30/60/90, and production is paid upfront. Standard defenses for smaller operators: pre-sell sponsorships (payment on signature or delivery, not net-90), maintain a pipeline buffer of ~3 videos at different stages so a cash hiccup doesn't stall publishing, and keep 2–3 months of fixed base in reserve before adding any salaried headcount.

## 4. Production Budgeting Frameworks (Film/Agency Lineage)

Useful even for a two-person YouTube operation because they force cost categories into the open.

### 4.1 Above-the-line / below-the-line

- **ATL** (story/rights, producers, director, principal talent): 25–35% of budget on studio projects; 10–20% on indies under $5M. Creator-business translation: the founder-creator's time and any licensed IP.
- **BTL production** (crew, equipment, locations, physical production): 40–50%.
- **Post-production** (edit, VFX, sound, music): 10–20% in film; in creator content post is routinely 25–35% and often the single largest line.
- **Other** (insurance, contingency): 5–10%. **Always carry a 10% contingency line**; productions that don't, raid post to pay for production overruns and ship worse videos.

### 4.2 Phase allocation for commercial/branded video

Pre-production 15–25%, production 40–55%, post 25–35%. Practitioner consensus: **skipping pre-production to save money almost always costs more in reshoots and revisions.** Rush timelines add 25–50% surcharge.

### 4.3 External market rates (2026) — for build-vs-buy checks

- Per-minute finished video: social short-form $500–3K/min; corporate/training $1K–10K/min; live-action explainer $2K–8K/min; animated explainer $3K–15K/min; commercial/brand film $5K–20K+/min. Blended agency average ≈ $3,500/finished minute.
- Freelancers: $600–1,200/day filming; $60–150/hr editing. Agencies: $5K–15K project minimums.
- Per-project: simple social $1.5K–5K; corporate/explainer $4.5K–20K; commercial/brand film $15K–50K+.
- Written content for reference: SEO blog post $400–1,500 (agency), outcome-driven $1.5K–6K; small in-house content team runs $180K–350K/year loaded.

## 5. When to Increase Production Value vs. Volume

### 5.1 The framework: quality-vs-quantity is a false dichotomy

The operative question is **"what is the maximum output at which we can hold our quality bar?"** If you can make two excellent videos a week, publishing one leaves growth on the table. If two means both are mediocre, one great video wins. Corollaries from platform data:

- Channels posting 12+/month see ~53% more views and ~66% more subscribers — but this is heavily confounded (bigger teams, bigger budgets). Don't chase the number; chase *consistency*: a predictable schedule outgrows erratic higher-volume posting, and tracked head-to-heads show twice-weekly posters beating daily posters on average views when quality diverges.
- **Content value > production value.** Viewers forgive imperfect visuals if the video delivers the promised information/entertainment; they do not forgive bad audio.

### 5.2 Production-value investment hierarchy (marginal ROI order)

1. **Audio** — highest ROI, always first. $80–500 in microphones. Garbled audio is the one production flaw that reliably drives click-away.
2. **Lighting & stable exposure** — ~$450 buys a key light + diffusion that outperforms a camera upgrade.
3. **Packaging** — thumbnails and titles move CTR more per dollar than anything on set.
4. **Edit quality/pacing** — retention lever; this is where a good editor pays for themselves.
5. **Resolution/camera body** — 1080p is sufficient for most niches; 4K is rarely the binding constraint. Fund this last.

### 5.3 The niche-relative quality bar

Required production value is set by *comparable successful channels in your niche*, not by an absolute standard. Tech reviews, beauty, and product showcases demand high visual polish; education, commentary, sports analysis, podcasts, and gaming tolerate a much lower visual bar. Audit the top 5 channels you actually compete with for attention; match their bar, don't exceed it until the next test says to.

### 5.4 The marginal-dollar test (decision rule)

For each incremental $1K/month, ask which buys more outcomes:
- **More volume** (another video from the same fixed base) — favored when: fixed costs are underutilized, per-video contribution is positive, the format is proven, and the audience isn't saturated.
- **More production value on existing volume** — favored when: retention/CTR data shows a specific quality gap (bad audio, weak thumbnails, slow edits), sponsors are pushing back on polish, or you're moving upmarket in sponsor tier where the integration itself is the product.
- **Distribution/repurposing** — often beats both: cutting existing long-form into clips has near-zero marginal production cost and multiplies surface area.

Run it as an experiment, not a belief: raise production value on 3–5 videos of one format, hold everything else, compare retention/CTR/revenue against the format's trailing median. If the lift doesn't cover the added cost per video, revert.

### 5.5 When escalating production value is correct

- The format's ceiling is proven (hits keep hitting) and the constraint is repeatability/polish, not ideas.
- Sponsor tier upgrade requires it (a $15K integration buyer expects a different product than a $1.5K buyer).
- Competitor set has visibly raised the niche bar and your CTR/retention is decaying against stable content quality.
- MrBeast is the extreme of this logic — $2.1–3.5M/video, content division running at up to a $120M annual loss on ~$224M content revenue, justified only because content is customer acquisition for Feastables ($251M sales, $20M profit) and the rest of Beast Industries ($473M total revenue, 2024). **The lesson is not "spend more"; it's that spending beyond content-P&L breakeven is only rational when a specific, measured downstream business captures the value.** If you don't have the Feastables, don't run the loss.

## 6. In-House vs. Outsource Thresholds

- Industry rule of thumb: below ~10 videos/month, freelance/agency wins (you avoid carrying fixed overhead); above ~15–20/month, in-house wins. Break-even for an in-house video team is commonly cited at **8–12 videos/month**.
- Hybrid is usually optimal for creator businesses: in-house the judgment-heavy, always-on roles (lead editor, producer/ops) and outsource spiky or specialized work (motion graphics, color, additional shooters).
- Build a **deep bench of proven freelancers reusable across projects** rather than sourcing fresh talent per project — per-project onboarding cost and quality variance are the hidden taxes of outsourcing (a top scaling-mistake finding from agency operators).
- Editing economics: freelance editors $60–150/hr vs. in-house loaded ~$55–63/productive hour — the in-house discount only materializes if you keep the editor >60–70% utilized. Below that, you bought an expensive fixed cost.

## 7. Financial Red Flags in Scaling Content Spend

1. **Cost per video rising faster than revenue per video.** The classic scaling failure: team grows, gear improves, videos get "better," and contribution margin per video shrinks. Plot both lines quarterly; divergence for two consecutive quarters = freeze hiring and audit.
2. **Fixed base added ahead of proven utilization.** Salaried editor at 40% utilization, studio lease used 2 days/week, gear bought for a format that hasn't cleared contribution-positive. Rule: rent/freelance until the recurring need is 3+ months proven, then buy/hire.
3. **Blended averages hiding a losing format.** One profitable format subsidizing two losers looks fine in aggregate. Demand format-level P&Ls.
4. **Revenue concentration.** One sponsor >40% of revenue, or ads/sponsorship >80% combined with no owned backend. Any platform policy change or sponsor churn becomes existential.
5. **Amortization games.** Stretching gear/content useful lives, capitalizing production costs, or reviewing performance "in aggregate" to avoid admitting a flop — the Netflix critique in miniature. If reported margins improve while cash burn worsens, the accounting is doing the work.
6. **Cash timing ignorance.** Net-60/90 receivables + upfront production costs = growth that accelerates a cash crunch. Growth in bookings with shrinking bank balance is a red flag, not a milestone.
7. **Production-value creep with no measured payback.** Each video slightly fancier than the last, no experiment ever run, "brand" cited as justification. Ratchet effect: quality bars only ever go up, costs follow.
8. **Founder-time cost still at zero in the model.** If the plan "works" only because the founder's 25 hours/video are free, it does not work.
9. **Paid amplification quietly becoming structural.** $2–5K/video of ad spend to make content "perform" is fine as a tested tactic; it's a red flag when organic performance decays and paid spend backfills it without anyone re-pricing the format's true cost.
10. **No kill criteria.** Formats and shows should carry pre-agreed sunset thresholds (e.g., median contribution negative for 8 consecutive videos after two iterations). Content businesses die of sentimental attachment to legacy formats.

## 8. Playbooks

### 8.1 Build the per-video P&L (one-time setup, ~half a day)

1. List every recurring cost; tag each fixed / semi-fixed / variable.
2. Amortize all capital: price ÷ useful-life months. Sum into a monthly gear line.
3. Compute loaded salary cost; allocate by % time per format (rough is fine; revisit quarterly).
4. Time-track one production cycle per format; price founder hours at opportunity cost.
5. Compute fully loaded cost per video *per format* at current output.
6. Attach revenue per format: sponsorship actuals + 12-month AdSense/affiliate curves (pull lifetime revenue of videos published 12+ months ago as the proxy).
7. Output: a one-page table — format | videos/mo | fully loaded cost | revenue (12-mo) | contribution | payback days | hit rate.

### 8.2 Quarterly content-economics review (checklist)

- [ ] Cost per video vs. revenue per video trend, per format (two-quarter divergence = intervention)
- [ ] Fixed base ÷ monthly output — is the amortization denominator growing or shrinking?
- [ ] Utilization of every salaried creative and every owned facility/room (>60% or explain)
- [ ] Revenue mix: top sponsor %, ads %, owned/backend % (push backend up every quarter)
- [ ] Payback median per format; kill-criteria check on the worst format
- [ ] Cash: receivables aging vs. production payables; months of fixed base in reserve
- [ ] One production-value or volume experiment concluded, with a keep/revert decision

### 8.3 Green-lighting a new format or spend tier

1. Define the outcome metric and its target cost-per-outcome *before* episode 1.
2. Budget a fixed test slate (6–10 episodes) — enough for the power law to show, small enough to kill.
3. Use rented gear/freelance labor for the entire test.
4. Decision gate at slate end: median contribution, hit rate, cost-per-outcome vs. existing formats. Promote (in-house it, invest), iterate once, or kill. No third iteration.

## 9. Common Mistakes

- **Counting only cash costs** — ignoring gear amortization, overhead allocation, and founder time; per-video "cost" understated 2–5x.
- **Judging content by production spend, not cost-per-outcome** (the $50K film vs. $1.5K clip failure).
- **Averaging formats together** so the blended number hides the loser.
- **Buying gear as a reward, not an investment** — no per-video break-even math attached to purchases.
- **Hiring to aspiration** — full-time roles added before utilization is proven, creating fixed-cost fragility (the <60% utilization operating-leverage trap).
- **Skipping pre-production to "save money"** — pays it back with interest in reshoots and edit-room rescue work.
- **Cheap inputs + expensive fixes**: hiring cheap production help and expecting editing to rescue it; editors can polish decent work into great, not bad into good — and good editors ($30–50+/hr, 1–2 hrs/piece minimum) are scarcer than creators.
- **30-day revenue judgment on evergreen formats** — systematically kills the highest-LTV content.
- **Modeling ads as the business.** RPM math almost never covers professional production cost; sponsorship + owned revenue must carry the model.
- **Copying MrBeast reinvestment without MrBeast's backend.** Near-100% reinvestment and per-video losses are a venture strategy, valid only with a downstream product/business capturing the audience value.
- **Never running the volume-vs-value experiment** — arguing philosophy instead of testing 3–5 videos.
- **No contingency line** — every overrun then cannibalizes post-production, the phase with the most retention leverage.

## 10. Questions Carl Should Ask

**Cost side**
1. "What does one video actually cost you, fully loaded — including your own hours and a slice of the studio and gear? Walk me through the number."
2. "How many hours of whose time goes into each format? What's the loaded hourly rate of each person?"
3. "If output doubled next quarter with the same fixed base, what happens to cost per video? Why isn't that the plan?"
4. "What's the utilization of your salaried creatives and your facility/rooms right now, honestly?"
5. "What did you buy in the last 12 months, and what's each purchase's cost per video over its life?"

**Revenue side**
6. "What does a video earn over 12 months, per format — not 30 days? Show me a video from a year ago."
7. "Which single format pays the bills? What percentage of contribution comes from it?"
8. "What's your effective CPM earned per video, and what would it cost to buy those views with ads?"
9. "Which revenue arrives net-60 or slower, and how many months of fixed costs are in the bank?"
10. "Is any format defended purely on 'brand value'? For how many quarters has that been the defense?"

**Scaling decisions**
11. "You want to raise production value — what specific metric (retention, CTR, sponsor rate) is currently punishing you for not having it?"
12. "What's your kill criterion for the weakest format, agreed in advance?"
13. "That hire you're planning — what's their utilization in month one, and what would renting that capacity cost instead?"
14. "If content itself only breaks even, what downstream business captures the audience value — and is it measured?" *(For a facility business like a training lab: content's job may be lead-gen; then cost-per-signup is the only content metric that matters, and the whole content budget is a CAC line.)*
15. "Show me cost per video and revenue per video as two lines over the last 8 quarters. Which is growing faster?"

## Relevance notes (Mayday-shaped, kept generic)

- A YouTube-first company with sponsors + newsletter + merch should run **format-level P&Ls** with sponsorship as the contribution engine, AdSense as a bonus, and merch held to the Graham Stephan expectation (small unless persona-product fit is real).
- A training facility being built alongside a media company is the **facility-utilization math in §1.5** plus the **§10 Q14 pattern**: content spend for the facility is CAC, judged on cost-per-member/lead, not views — and the facility's pricing floor comes from fixed-cost ÷ realistic billable hours, never aspiration.
