# Editorial Calendars & Content Operations

## TL;DR

- **Consistency beats frequency, in every dataset.** A channel that ships one good video every Wednesday outgrows a channel that ships four one week and zero the next. Pick the cadence the team can hold for 12 months, not the one that sounds ambitious. Over-scheduling relative to real capacity is the #1 documented calendar failure mode.
- **Run the calendar on three horizons:** 90-day strategic (themes, campaigns, seasonal beats), 30-day tactical (specific titles, briefs, owners), 7-day operational (deadlines, publish times, promo). Each horizon has a different update frequency and information density.
- **Batch everything with a setup cost.** Filming, thumbnails, scripting, scheduling each carry 30–60 min of setup/context-switch overhead per unit; batching removes it and reliably cuts per-piece production time 60–70%. One production day can feed 2–4 weeks of publishing.
- **Backlog > calendar.** The calendar only stays full if a triaged, scored idea backlog feeds it. Separate capture (cheap, everyone) from commitment (expensive, editor-gated). Judge ideas title-and-thumbnail-first, MrBeast/Colin & Samir style: if they don't click, they don't watch.
- **Write kill criteria before launching a series, not after it's limping.** Pre-agreed thresholds ("if avg views < 60% of channel median after 6 episodes, we kill or reformat") replace emotional in-the-moment arguments. Most orgs don't do this — only ~44% of product teams have a pivot-vs-sunset framework — which is why zombie series eat calendars.
- **Steal the newsroom morning meeting.** A 15-minute daily standup ("budget meeting") + a weekly planning meeting + a monthly retro is the entire meeting stack a small content team needs. Agenda: what shipped, what's blocked, what's the story list, who owns what by when.
- **A documented strategy is table stakes, not the win.** ~95% of B2B content orgs have one; only 29% call it very effective. The gap is operations — clear goals, a scalable production model (45% lack one), and measurement discipline (only 51% measure effectively). Content ops maturity, not strategy documents, is what correlates with success: 80% of "extremely successful" content orgs operate at maturity level 4–5.
- **Cadence is a burnout-control instrument.** 52% of creators report burnout; 37% consider quitting over it; creative fatigue (40%) and workload (31%) are the top frequency causes. Batching, buffers, and planned off-weeks are operational answers, not soft perks.

## 1. Content operations as a discipline

Content ops is everything between "we decided to make things" and "the thing is published and measured": roles, workflows, tools, standards, governance. Strategy answers *what and why*; ops answers *who, how, when*.

**The maturity ladder (synthesized from Content Science's large-scale content-ops research and the GatherContent/Bynder governance model):**

| Level | Description | Symptoms |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Ad hoc | Content happens when someone feels like it | Missed weeks, hero-dependent, no calendar |
| 2 — Repeatable | A calendar and a rough process exist | Deadlines slip, quality varies by person |
| 3 — Defined | Documented workflow with review gates; governance enforced through the workflow itself | Predictable output, occasional bottlenecks |
| 4 — Measured | Error rates, cycle times, and performance tracked and fed back into planning | Data drives the calendar, not vibes |
| 5 — Optimized | Quality/velocity is a competitive advantage; system self-improves | Kill/scale decisions are routine, near-zero defects |

Content Science's research (the largest study of content operations) found **80% of "extremely successful" organizations operate at level 4 or 5**. The practical read: most small teams sit at 1–2 and get outsized returns just reaching level 3 (a real calendar + defined workflow + one weekly meeting).

**CMI 2025 benchmark numbers worth keeping on hand** (1,186 respondents, mostly B2B but directionally true for media teams):

- 95% have a documented strategy; only **29% rate it extremely/very effective** (58% moderately). Documentation isn't the bottleneck; execution is.
- **45% lack a scalable model for content creation** — the single most content-ops-shaped stat in the study. Of the 35% who have one, only 41% say it produces desired outcomes.
- Top challenges: lack of resources (54%), creating content that prompts action (55%), measuring results (47%).
- 42% of underperformers blame **lack of clear goals** — a planning failure, fixed at the calendar level by attaching a goal to every slot.
- Only **51% believe they measure content performance effectively**; 56% can't attribute ROI.
- Typical dedicated team: **2–5 people** (54% of teams with dedicated staff). Small-team ops discipline is the norm, not the exception.
- 81% now use generative AI, but 54% use it ad hoc; only 19% have it integrated into daily workflows. The advantage goes to teams that wire AI into specific workflow stages (briefs, cutdowns, repurposing), not sprinkle it.

**Carl's stance:** for a team under ~10 people, content ops is four artifacts and one meeting: (1) an idea backlog, (2) a three-horizon calendar, (3) a written per-format workflow with owners and hour estimates, (4) a scorecard with kill thresholds, plus (5) a weekly editorial meeting. Everything else is tooling preference.

## 2. The editorial calendar

### 2.1 Three planning horizons (the core framework)

Run the same calendar at three zoom levels with different refresh rates (framework per Digital Applied's editorial workflow guide, consistent with newsroom forward-planning practice):

**Strategic — 90 days, updated quarterly**
- Content pillars/themes and how slots split across them
- Major campaigns, launches, seasonal/event beats (for a baseball facility: tryout season, off-season training windows, tournament calendar; for a media co: sponsor flight dates, platform events)
- Resource allocation by channel (how many long-form vs shorts vs newsletter slots the team can actually staff)

**Tactical — 30 days, updated monthly**
- Specific working titles and briefs per slot
- Author/editor/owner assignments
- Channel adaptations (which long-form pieces get cut into shorts/newsletter sections)
- Target keyword or packaging concept per piece

**Operational — 7 days, updated weekly**
- Draft/edit/review deadlines and publish date-times
- Promotion plan per piece (community post, newsletter mention, cross-post)
- **Reactive slots** — hold 15–20% of weekly capacity open for trend-reactive or breaking content. A 100%-planned calendar can't respond to anything.

### 2.2 Minimum viable calendar entry

Every slot needs: working title • format/content type • channel • publish date • owner • editor/reviewer • status • goal (why this piece exists) • link to draft/asset. Status vocabulary that works for video and text alike: `idea → approved → brief → in-production → in-review → scheduled → published → measured`.

### 2.3 Workflow stages with honest hour estimates

For written content (adapt multipliers for video — production and edit stages balloon):

1. Topic approved (from backlog) — 0h
2. Brief written — 1–2h
3. Draft/production — 3–8h (video: shoot day + 8–20h edit for a produced long-form piece)
4. Editorial review — 1–2h
5. Revisions — 1–3h
6. Final approval — 0.5h
7. Publish + promotion — 0.5–1h

Two operational rules that prevent most pipeline stalls:
- **Complete briefs before production starts.** Cited effect: ~60% fewer revision cycles when the brief is complete. The brief is the cheapest place to kill a bad idea.
- **Review SLAs and reviewer caps:** 48-hour response SLA, max 2 reviewers per piece, a named backup approver, and "autonomous zones" (short-form social ships without approval). Approval bottlenecks without time limits are a documented top failure mode.

### 2.4 Capacity planning (where calendars die)

The most common calendar failure is scheduling to ambition instead of capacity. The rule from the workflow literature: **"a realistic 2 posts/week executed consistently outperforms an ambitious 5-posts/week plan that collapses under pressure."**

Playbook:
1. Time-track one real production cycle per format to get true hours-per-piece (teams underestimate by 2–3x, mostly in editing and packaging).
2. Compute weekly team content-hours *after* meetings, admin, client/sponsor work.
3. Schedule to **80%** of that number; the 20% buffer absorbs reactive slots, sick days, and re-shoots.
4. If the calendar keeps slipping, cut frequency before cutting quality — audiences forgive fewer uploads far more than worse ones (see §3).

### 2.5 Quarterly planning cycle

A cadence that works for small teams (per Digital Applied, consistent with agency practice):
- **Week 1:** retrospective on last quarter — traffic/views, conversions, publishing velocity vs plan, what got killed or slipped.
- **Week 2:** ideation refresh — backlog grooming, competitor/outlier gap analysis, re-scoring.
- **Week 3:** populate the 90-day calendar; write briefs for the first 4 weeks.
- **Ongoing:** weekly check-ins; write briefs rolling 3–4 weeks ahead so production never waits on planning.

## 3. Publishing cadence and the consistency data

### 3.1 The headline finding

Across every data source reviewed — vidIQ-adjacent consulting data, AIR Media-Tech's 2025 cadence analysis, platform-watcher analyses — the same pattern: **the fastest-growing channels are not the most frequent uploaders.** Predictable schedule + strong average view duration + a clear content system beats raw volume. The algorithm rewards session-quality signals (watch time, retention, engagement), and audiences build habits around predictability ("every Wednesday at 3pm"), not volume.

### 3.2 Frequency benchmarks (YouTube long-form, per Spicer's consulting data)

| Channel size | Recommended cadence |
|---|---|
| 0–1K subs | 1–2/week |
| 1K–10K | 1–2/week, prioritize quality gains |
| 10K–100K | 2–3/week |
| 100K+ | 1–3/week per audience expectation |

By niche (production overhead is the real driver):
- Gaming/commentary/talking-head: 3–5/week viable
- Education/tutorial (research-heavy): 1–2/week
- News/finance (time-sensitive): 3–5/week
- High-production documentary/film-style: every 2–4 weeks

Key per-video economics: channels at **1–2/week achieve the highest average views per video relative to subscriber count**. 3–5/week channels get more total channel views but lower per-video performance and inconsistent retention — fine if you're monetizing volume (news), bad if each video carries sponsor value.

### 3.3 The daily-upload decay curve

Documented pattern for creators who move to daily uploads: views begin declining by **weeks 3–4**, audience fatigue and declining CTR by **month two**. Daily posting drives rushed packaging (weak titles/thumbnails), thinner ideas, and viewer overwhelm — early abandonment then gets flagged as low quality and suppresses distribution. "Daily uploads" as a growth strategy is effectively dead for produced content as of 2025; it survives only in formats with near-zero marginal production cost.

### 3.4 The 2025-era cadence stack (AIR Media-Tech)

- **One weekly "anchor upload"** — the high-quality long-form piece the schedule is built around; a second weekly video only if it doesn't degrade the anchor.
- **1–2 Shorts/week** for surface-area visibility (not a growth engine on its own for most channels).
- **Community posts, especially polls,** massively over-index on engagement: polls hitting ~7.7K votes / 215 comments vs ~1–1.5K likes / 30–50 comments for standard posts on the same channel. Cheap habit-maintenance between uploads.
- **24/7 looped livestreams of existing catalog** as passive distribution for catalog-rich channels: case data — kids' channel DONA English got 20% of revenue from 24/7 streams in 10 days, 60% by month two; Lesnoy pulled 115M+ views in 60 days from streaming existing catalog. Format-dependent (works best for kids/music/ambient), but the general lesson is: sweat the archive.

### 3.5 Cross-channel cadence table

| Channel | Cadence | Notes / lead time |
|---|---|---|
| YouTube long-form | 1–2/week, fixed day+time | 3–6 weeks lead for produced pieces |
| Shorts/Reels/TikTok | 3–7/week viable when batched | Same-day to 3-day lead |
| Email newsletter | Weekly or bi-weekly, Tue–Wed AM strongest | 1 week lead |
| Blog/SEO | 2–4/week if that's the engine; otherwise 1/week | 1–2 weeks lead |
| Pillar guides/long docs | Monthly–quarterly | 4–8 weeks lead |
| Podcast | Weekly, seasonal breaks acceptable if announced | Batch-record 2–4 episodes |

**Carl's rule of thumb:** announce the schedule to the audience only once you've privately held it for 4+ weeks. A public cadence is a promise; a broken promise costs more than a slower one. And when downsizing cadence, communicate it as a format upgrade ("bigger videos, every other week"), never as a retreat.

## 4. Batching production

### 4.1 The economics

Every production activity carries fixed setup cost: lighting/camera/audio setup, getting camera-ready, loading the editing project, getting into "writing brain." That overhead runs **30–60 minutes per video** when filming one-at-a-time. Batching amortizes it: creator-workflow analyses consistently report **60–70% reduction in per-piece production time** (some claim 60–80% when combined with AI-assisted editing for silence removal/clip selection), and **4–10 hours/week saved** for creators publishing 5–7 pieces weekly. Batching also measurably reduces overwhelm — stress and time-pressure drop for most who switch.

Real small-team example (Ridge Films client): a Sydney recruitment agency runs a **one-hour Monday ritual** where the whole team films the entire week's videos in one session around a fixed desktop studio. The fixed studio is the enabler — zero setup cost makes the batch trivially cheap.

### 4.2 Batch by stage, not by piece

The mature version isn't "film 4 videos in a day" — it's running each workflow stage in batch mode:

- **Ideation batch:** one session generating/scoring 20–50 ideas (Colin & Samir sell an exercise generating 50 high-viewership ideas in 5 days — the point is volume then filtration, never one-at-a-time ideation).
- **Scripting/brief batch:** write 3–5 briefs or scripts in one deep-work block.
- **Filming batch:** one shoot day = 2–6 videos. Change shirts between videos; shoot all A-roll for everything, then all B-roll.
- **Edit batch:** editor works a themed queue; templates and reusable graphics packages amortize.
- **Packaging batch:** all thumbnails/titles in one session — this also forces side-by-side comparison, which raises packaging quality.
- **Scheduling batch:** load 2–4 weeks of publishes and promo in one sitting.

### 4.3 Batch-day playbook

1. Lock scripts/briefs 48h before shoot day; no writing on set.
2. Print a shot list ordered by *setup*, not by video (all desk shots, then all whiteboard shots, etc.).
3. Wardrobe changes per video to fake different days.
4. Capture thumbnail stills during the shoot while lighting is up — don't schedule a separate thumbnail shoot.
5. Immediately after: label footage, log to the calendar, note re-shoot needs while memory is fresh.
6. Target buffer: **2–4 weeks of finished, scheduled content at all times.** The buffer is the burnout insurance and the thing that makes vacations/illness/sponsor fire-drills survivable.

### 4.4 Batching failure modes

- **Staleness:** batched reactive/news content dies on the shelf. Batch only evergreen and format-driven content; serve trends from the 15–20% reactive slots.
- **Quality monotony:** 6 videos shot in one energy state can feel identical. Cap batches at the on-camera person's real energy budget (usually 3–4 produced videos/day).
- **Batch-and-forget:** scheduling a month out and skipping first-48h monitoring/community engagement. Publishing is automated; presence isn't.

## 5. Content sprints and agile content ops

A **content sprint** is a time-boxed period (typically 1–2 weeks) where the team commits to a defined set of content deliverables, with a planning session at the start and a retrospective at the end. The pattern that actually fits content teams is the hybrid documented across the agile-marketing literature: **Scrum-shaped commitment + Kanban-shaped daily flow**:

- **Sprint planning (bi-weekly, 45–60 min):** pull from the scored backlog into the sprint; estimate with rough effort points (S/M/L is fine — story-point theater is overkill for teams of 3–6); confirm every item has a brief and an owner.
- **Kanban board for daily flow** with the calendar's status columns, and **WIP limits per column** (e.g., max 2 items "in review") — the WIP limit is the mechanism that exposes the real bottleneck, which in content teams is almost always review/approval or editing, not writing/filming.
- **Daily standup** (see §8) to coordinate writers/editors/designers.
- **Retro (bi-weekly, 30 min):** what shipped vs committed, where did items stall, one process change to try.

When sprints fit: campaign pushes, launches, backlog blitzes (e.g., "build 8 weeks of Neptune social content before facility opening"), website/channel overhauls. When flow-only Kanban fits better: steady-state weekly publishing where the cadence itself is the timebox. Most small media teams should run steady-state Kanban and reserve true sprints for bursts.

## 6. Idea backlog management

### 6.1 The pipeline: capture → triage → develop → schedule

- **Capture (everyone, zero friction):** one shared inbox (a single Notion/Airtable table, a Slack channel that gets swept — anything, as long as it's ONE place). Rule: capturing costs nothing and implies nothing. Volume is the goal; systematic ideation from audience questions, comments, competitor/outlier analysis, and search data should generate more ideas than the team can execute — "which is exactly the right problem to have."
- **Triage (editor, weekly, 15 min):** score and sort. Most ideas get parked, not developed. Ideas untouched for 90 days get archived — a 400-item backlog is a graveyard, not an asset.
- **Develop (only what's near-scheduled):** brief-writing is the gate between "idea" and "commitment." Never put an unbriefed idea on the operational calendar.
- **Schedule:** briefed ideas fill calendar slots by pillar mix and capacity.

### 6.2 Scoring: packaging-first

The strongest practitioner consensus (MrBeast's leaked guide, Colin & Samir's playbook) is to judge ideas by their *packaging* before their substance:

- **Colin & Samir's 3 Rules of YouTube:** (1) *If they don't click, they don't watch* — the idea must have an obvious title+thumbnail before it's greenlit; (2) *Respect their time* — the idea must support a hook in the first 30 seconds; (3) *Do you want more stuff like this?* — the idea should fit a repeatable format that converts viewers into repeat audience.
- **MrBeast's pipeline:** a dedicated ideation team tracks trends, drafts titles and thumbnails *first*, records large numbers of concepts, and narrows through internal review — only a small share ever reaches the final decision layer, and producers then gate on cost/feasibility. The leaked production guide names titles and thumbnails "critical components" and optimizes against CTR, average view duration, average view percentage, and **first-minute retention**. Also his greenlight filter: "If I'm not excited to get in front of that camera and film the video, it's just simply not going to happen" — founder-energy is a real scoring criterion for creator-led businesses.
- **A practical scoring rubric for small teams** (score 1–5 each): Clickability (can we write the title/thumbnail right now?) • Audience fit (does our defined viewer want this?) • Effort (inverse) • Strategic value (pillar/sponsor/funnel fit) • Team excitement. Anything scoring <3 on Clickability doesn't get developed regardless of the rest.

### 6.3 Backlog hygiene rules

- One list, not five (docs + Slack + heads = no backlog).
- Every idea gets a one-line "why now / why us."
- Tag by pillar and format so calendar-filling is a filter operation.
- Re-score quarterly during the week-2 ideation refresh; kill stale items without ceremony.

## 7. Kill criteria: sunsetting underperforming series

### 7.1 Why pre-agreed thresholds

The core move (borrowed from paid-creative decision frameworks): a **documented set of rules that determines when a piece/series is killed, kept, iterated, or scaled, based on metric thresholds defined *before* launch** — replacing subjective, in-the-moment judgment with pre-agreed criteria. Context on how rare this is: research cited in the product literature has ~72% of product initiatives failing objectives while only **~44% of teams have a clear pivot-vs-sunset framework**; high-performing teams decide from data 78% of the time vs 42% for underperformers. Content teams are worse, because series accrue emotional attachment ("it's our show").

### 7.2 The Kill / Iterate / Keep / Scale grid

At every series review, each series lands in one box:

| Verdict | Trigger | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Scale** | ≥130% of channel median on the north-star metric, trending up | More slots, more budget, spin-off formats |
| **Keep** | 80–130% of median, stable, strategic value intact | Continue, minor optimizations |
| **Iterate** | 50–80% of median, OR strong retention but weak CTR (packaging problem), OR strong CTR but weak retention (delivery problem) | One defined change per cycle (packaging, length, host, hook) with a deadline: "2 more episodes with new packaging; if still <70%, kill" |
| **Kill** | <50–60% of median after fair sample, AND no iterate hypothesis left, AND no offsetting strategic value | Sunset (see 7.4) |

Set the actual percentages per channel; the numbers above are sane defaults. The non-negotiable part is writing them down at series launch.

### 7.3 Fair sample and confounders

- **Minimum sample before judging a series: 4–6 episodes.** Episode 1 of anything underperforms; algorithms need repetition to find the audience, and audiences need repetition to build the habit.
- Diagnose before sentencing: low CTR + high retention = packaging problem (iterate, don't kill). High CTR + low retention = promise/delivery mismatch. Low both after 6 episodes = idea problem (kill).
- Check **portfolio effects** before killing: a low-view series can still be the top sponsor-revenue vehicle, the newsletter-growth engine, or the top-of-funnel feeder for the series that converts. Evaluate contribution to the system, not just the series' own line. (This is the podcast-measurement literature's warning: low direct lift ≠ no value.)
- Distinguish **dead** from **solvably struggling**: wrong day/time, cannibalization by a sibling series, packaging drift, host fatigue are all fixable. One iterate cycle with a deadline costs little; a zombie series with no deadline costs a calendar slot forever.

### 7.4 Sunset playbook

1. Announce internally with the data; thank the owner explicitly — killing a series must be safe or nobody will ever propose ending anything.
2. Decide the audience story: quiet stop (usually fine), finale episode (if the series had a devoted core), or fold-in (absorb the best segment into a healthier show).
3. **Harvest:** archive learnings (what the retention graphs said, which episodes over-performed and why), repurpose the back catalog (cutdowns, evergreen re-promotion, 24/7 stream fodder per §3.4).
4. Reallocate the slot deliberately — a kill without a reallocation plan just becomes a missed upload.
5. Log it. A "graveyard doc" of killed series + reasons prevents re-litigating and re-launching the same dead idea in 18 months.

**Carl's stance:** the healthiest content portfolios kill something roughly every one to two quarters. If nothing has been killed in a year, the review process is decorative.

## 8. Newsroom-style standups and the meeting stack

Newsrooms solved daily content coordination decades ago; small content teams should copy the shape, not the size.

### 8.1 The morning budget meeting (daily, 10–15 min)

In newspaper practice, the day starts with the **morning budget meeting**: review what happened overnight, assess developing stories, plan the day's coverage, and decide placement. Editors meet daily with the chief editor on what leads. The small-team translation, standing, timeboxed:

1. **Shipped/overnight:** what published, first-24h signals worth noting.
2. **Today's budget:** what moves stages today, who owns each.
3. **Blockers:** anything stalled >48h gets an owner and a deadline on the spot.
4. **Reactive check:** anything trending worth burning a reactive slot on?

Newsroom guidance stresses meetings must be **inclusive and agenda-setting** — the meeting exists to generate and assign *original* story ideas and keep staff motivated, not to read statuses off a board people can read themselves. If the meeting is just status, kill the meeting and keep the board.

### 8.2 The rest of the stack

| Meeting | Cadence | Length | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standup / budget meeting | Daily (or M/W/F for teams ≤4) | 10–15 min | Ship list, blockers, reactive calls |
| Editorial planning | Weekly | 45–60 min | Next 2 weeks of calendar, brief approvals, backlog triage |
| Performance review | Monthly | 60 min | Scorecard vs goals, series grid (§7.2), cadence health |
| Retro + quarterly planning | Quarterly | Half-day | §2.5 cycle: retrospective, ideation refresh, 90-day calendar |

### 8.3 Other newsroom practices worth stealing

- **The news diary / forward-planning file:** known future events (launches, seasons, anniversaries, industry dates) logged months ahead so planned coverage is cheap and breaking coverage is the exception. Balance planned vs breaking deliberately.
- **A designated quality gate:** newsrooms have a producer/copy desk role ensuring "material that is published is of the highest quality." Someone (not the creator) must own final-pass review — even in a 3-person team, a second pair of eyes on packaging catches most publish-day errors.
- **Breaking-news protocol:** a written who-does-what-when for drop-everything moments (platform change, sponsor crisis, viral moment) so reactive content doesn't torch the whole calendar.
- **Leadership norms:** lead by example, be present for the big moments, praise publicly / correct privately, and continuously assess whether output is meeting audience needs and business targets.
- **Converged desk model:** bigger operations run a central "command and control" desk owning all intake/production/output across platforms — the ancestor of the modern "make once, publish everywhere" repurposing desk.

## 9. Sustainability: cadence as burnout control

Billion Dollar Boy's 2025 study (1,000 creators + 1,000 senior marketers, US/UK):

- **52% of creators have experienced burnout; 37% have considered quitting over it.** Among full-time creators, some 2025–26 figures run as high as 63%.
- Causes by frequency: creative fatigue 40%, demanding workload 31%, constant screen time 27%. Ranked by *severity*, **financial instability is #1 (55%)** — meaning revenue diversification is itself a burnout intervention.
- Impact: 59% report career damage, 58% wellbeing damage; 52% report work-linked anxiety. Secondary analyses estimate mid-tier creators ($50–100K/yr) lose **$15–25K annually** to burnout and see 25–35% reach decline within 60 days of burning out.
- What creators say would help: work-life boundaries (38%), regular time off (34%), **AI and scheduling tools to reduce workload (32%)** — i.e., a third of creators are asking for exactly the ops systems in this document.

Operational translation: the buffer (§4.3), the honest capacity plan (§2.4), the batching system (§4), and pre-announced schedule breaks (podcast-style "seasons") are the mechanisms. For a creator-led business, the founder's on-camera energy is the scarcest input in the whole system — MrBeast's "if I'm not excited to film it, it's not going to happen" is a capacity constraint, and the calendar must be built around it, not in denial of it.

## 10. Common mistakes

1. **Scheduling to ambition, not capacity** — the top documented failure. Fix: time-track, plan to 80%.
2. **Calendar without backlog** — the calendar runs dry in week 6 and gets filled with panic content.
3. **No kill criteria** — zombie series occupy slots for years because nobody wants the fight. Write thresholds at launch.
4. **Judging a series on episodes 1–3** — killing before the fair-sample minimum (4–6 episodes) wastes every launch.
5. **Daily-upload volume worship** — documented decay by weeks 3–4; quality of packaging and retention collapses first.
6. **Erratic cadence** — four uploads then silence. Consistency beats frequency in every dataset reviewed.
7. **Approval bottlenecks without SLAs** — unbounded review time, >2 reviewers, no escalation path. Content dies "in review."
8. **Over-engineering the tooling before the habit** — building a 14-field Airtable before the team reliably runs a weekly meeting. Process adoption first, tooling second.
9. **Batching reactive content** — trend content pre-produced two weeks out publishes dead. Batch evergreen; hold reactive slots.
10. **Status-reading standups** — meetings that recite the board. The meeting is for decisions, assignments, and original ideas.
11. **Ignoring performance data at planning time** — measuring but never letting the scorecard change next quarter's calendar (the 51%-measure-effectively gap in CMI's data).
12. **Killing by feelings in either direction** — sunsetting a low-view series that's actually the sponsor vehicle, or protecting the founder's pet series that fails every threshold. The grid (§7.2) plus portfolio check exists to prevent both.
13. **Treating the documented strategy as the finish line** — 95% have one, 29% find it very effective; the delta is ops.

## 11. Questions Carl should ask a client

**Calendar & capacity**
- "Show me your calendar for the next 30 days. How many of those slots have a written brief?" (Unbriefed slots = fiction.)
- "How many hours does one episode of your main format actually take, measured, not guessed?"
- "What percentage of planned pieces shipped on their planned date last quarter?" (Below ~80% = capacity mismatch.)
- "Where's your reactive slack? If something big happens in your niche Tuesday, what gets bumped?"

**Cadence & consistency**
- "When did you last miss a scheduled publish, and why?" (Answer reveals whether the failure is capacity, process, or founder energy.)
- "If you cut frequency in half and put the saved hours into packaging, do you believe views would go up or down?" (Tests whether they've internalized consistency > frequency.)
- "How many weeks of finished, scheduled content do you have banked right now?" (Zero buffer = one illness from a broken promise.)

**Backlog & ideation**
- "Where do ideas live? One place or five?"
- "For your next three planned videos, tell me the title and describe the thumbnail right now." (Can't = greenlighting substance without packaging.)
- "When did you last archive or kill ideas from the backlog?"

**Kill criteria & portfolio**
- "What series or format have you killed in the last 12 months?" (None = decorative reviews.)
- "For your weakest current series: what number, by what date, keeps it alive? Is that written anywhere?"
- "Which of your series makes the money, and which makes the audience? Do you know the difference?"

**Meetings & roles**
- "Walk me through your Monday. Is there a meeting where the week's content gets decided, and does it end with owners and dates?"
- "Who looks at a video's title and thumbnail, other than the person who made them, before it goes live?"
- "Who can veto publishing? How long can they sit on it?" (No SLA = future bottleneck.)

**Sustainability**
- "How many on-camera days per month can the founder actually sustain for two years? Is the calendar built on that number or a fantasy number?"
- "What happens to the schedule when the founder takes two weeks off?" (Tests batching + buffer + formats that don't require the founder.)

## 12. Notes for the Mayday / Neptune context

- Mayday's YouTube-first model with sponsor flights maps directly to the **anchor-upload cadence** (§3.4): one strong weekly long-form as the sponsor vehicle, shorts + community posts as habit maintenance, newsletter on a fixed weekly day. Per-video view economics (§3.2) matter more than total volume when each video carries sponsor value.
- Neptune Performance content (training breakdowns, facility buildout, athlete progress) is heavily **evergreen and seasonal** — ideal batching material (§4), with the baseball calendar (tryouts, off-season, tournament season) as the 90-day strategic layer (§2.1) and a news-diary of known dates (§8.3).
- The existing Mayday Studio app (sprints, projects pipeline, research trends, idea inbox) already implements much of §5–§6; the most likely gaps to probe are written kill criteria per series (§7), a fair-sample rule before judging new Neptune formats, and an enforced content buffer metric.
