# Diversified Media Revenue: Memberships, Merch, Courses, Licensing

Reference for everything a creator-led media business earns *beyond* AdSense and sponsorships. Covers the economics, benchmarks, and playbooks for the four big non-ad streams: recurring memberships/subscriptions, merchandise, digital products/courses, and licensing/syndication — plus how the revenue mix should evolve as the business matures.

## TL;DR

- **The resilient creator business runs 3+ revenue streams.** Professional creators average ~3.2 channels; the industry-wide mix is roughly sponsorships 31%, ad revenue share 27%, subscriptions 18%, merch 11%, courses/licensing/other 7%, crowdfunding 5%. If one stream is >60% of revenue, that's a fragility flag, not a strength.
- **Membership conversion is small and predictable: plan on 1–3% of your true audience paying.** YouTube channel memberships convert 0.5–1% of subscribers (3–5% only in hyper-engaged niches); newsletters convert a median ~0.6–3% free→paid. Anyone modeling 10% is fantasizing. Model at 1–2%, price at $5–10/mo entry with a 2–3x tier ladder, push annual billing hard.
- **Churn, not acquisition, decides whether a membership compounds.** Healthy paid-community churn is 3–6%/month; above 5% you have a retention problem, above 8% churn is your #1 business risk. Annual plans retain ~92% over 12 months vs ~68% for monthly. The single biggest churn lever is a "first win" in week one of onboarding.
- **Merch: start print-on-demand, graduate winners to bulk inventory.** POD = zero inventory risk, ~25–40% net margin target. Bulk = 2–3x per-unit margin but real capital risk. Fourthwall-style platforms take no revenue cut on physical merch (flat product cost only). Run merch as event-driven *drops* tied to content moments, not an always-on catalog.
- **Courses are the highest-margin stream (85–95%) but the most operationally deceptive.** Self-paced completion is 10–20%; cohort-based is 85–96% and commands 3–5x the price. Kajabi creators average ~$37K/yr vs Udemy's ~$3.3K — owning the platform and the price matters enormously.
- **Licensing/syndication is the found-money stream for a mature library.** FAST-channel ad splits start around 50/50–60/40; aggregators (Mirage-style) handle distribution for a rev cut. Only worth pursuing with a deep episodic library; requires clean rights (music, contractor IP) from day one — retro-clearing kills deals.
- **Sequence matters: don't launch everything at once.** Ads → sponsors → membership → merch → course/digital product → licensing is the standard maturity ladder. Each new stream costs founder attention; launch a new one only when the previous is systematized.
- (Relevance note: this ladder maps directly onto a YouTube-first business like Mayday Media — and the membership/course economics here also apply to a training facility like Neptune, where "membership" is the physical-world analog with far lower churn tolerance and higher price points.)

## 1. The revenue-stack model: why diversify at all

**The core problem:** AdSense is volatile (CPMs swing seasonally and algorithmically) and sponsorships are lumpy and market-dependent (brand budgets are the first thing cut in a downturn). Both are *rented* revenue — a platform or a brand can turn them off. Memberships, merch, products, and licensing are *owned* revenue: the audience relationship monetizes directly.

**Framework: the four-layer revenue stack** (synthesis of standard creator-economy strategy):

| Layer | Streams | Character | Margin | Volatility |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Attention rent | AdSense, platform rev-share | Passive, scales with views | ~100% of what's paid, but you keep 45–55% of gross ad spend | High |
| 2. Attention resale | Sponsorships, brand deals | Active sales, lumpy | 70–90% (after agency/production) | High |
| 3. Direct audience | Memberships, merch, courses, digital products | Recurring or repeatable, compounding | 25–95% depending on stream | Low–medium |
| 4. IP & library | Licensing, syndication, formats, music | Passive once set up, library-dependent | 50–90% | Low |

Layers 1–2 fund the business while layers 3–4 are built. A mature creator business shifts weight down the stack over time. MrBeast is the extreme case: Feastables (a layer-3 owned product) is projected at $180–520M/yr vs $70–100M from AdSense across all channels — the owned product out-earns the platform 3–5x.

**Industry mix benchmark (2025, whole-market):** brand sponsorships 31.5%, ad rev-share 26.8%, subscriptions 18.4%, merch 11.3%, other (courses, licensing) 7.2%, crowdfunding 4.8%. Individual mature creator businesses typically look different — direct-audience streams over-index once the audience trusts you.

**The 1,000-true-fans math still governs everything.** Every direct-audience stream is (audience size) × (conversion %) × (price) × (retention). Conversion is the least controllable variable (it clusters in a narrow band); price and retention are where operators actually win.

## 2. Memberships & subscriptions

The workhorse of layer 3. Three sub-flavors with different economics: platform-native memberships (YouTube), patronage platforms (Patreon), and paid newsletters/communities (Substack/beehiiv/Circle).

### 2.1 YouTube channel memberships

- **Split:** creator keeps 70%, YouTube takes 30%. On iOS, Apple stacks another 30% — a $4.99 membership nets ~$3.49 on web but ~$2.45 via the iOS app. Push web signup in CTAs.
- **Conversion benchmarks:** 0.5–1% of subscribers is average; 1–3% is strong (community-driven channels); 3–5% is exceptional and confined to high-engagement niches (finance, education, fitness, creative). Broad entertainment channels sit at the bottom of the range. A 50K-sub channel should model 250–1,000 members, not more.
- **Tier design (standard three-tier ladder):**
  - Entry $2.99–4.99 — badges, emoji, members-only posts. ~70% of members land here.
  - Mid $10–14.99 — exclusive/early videos, BTS. ~20%.
  - Premium $24.99–49.99 — direct access, calls, credits. ~10%.
  - A three-tier ladder roughly doubles revenue vs. a single tier at the same member count (one modeled example: 1,000 members across three tiers ≈ $7.7K/mo net vs ~$3.5K single-tier).
- **Churn:** 5–10%/month is normal for channel memberships; the perk treadmill (consistent members-only content) is what keeps it at the low end.
- **When to prefer it over Patreon:** frictionless (viewers already logged in), surfaced in the watch experience. When to avoid: 30% platform take, no email list ownership, perks limited to YouTube surface.

### 2.2 Patreon (patronage model)

- **Platform scale (2026):** ~286K–335K creators with at least one paying member; ~17.8M paid memberships; ~$25M+/month in visible payouts; $2B+/yr paid to creators cumulative pace.
- **ARPU:** average pledge ~$6.10/month (up from ~$5.40 early 2025). Standard tier architecture: $3–5 base, $7–12 mid, $20–30 premium.
- **Earnings distribution is brutally skewed:** typical creators earn $315–1,575/month; the median is closer to a few hundred dollars; the top ~2% clear $25K+/month. Patreon is a power-law platform — treat "average creator earnings" claims with suspicion.
- **Category economics:** podcasts are the highest revenue-per-creator category (7.7% of creators, 14.8% of payments) — audio audiences are habitual and the paywall (bonus episodes, ad-free feeds) is clean. Video is the largest category by volume.
- **Patreon's fee:** 8–12% of earnings depending on plan (plus payment processing) — better than YouTube's 30%, worse than owning the stack, and you *do* get the member email relationship.

### 2.3 Paid newsletters (Substack / beehiiv)

- **Free→paid conversion — the honest numbers:**
  - Substack's official guidance says 5–10%. Real-world: **median ~3%** across public Substack data; only ~1 in 5 publications clears 5%. beehiiv's 2026 dataset shows a median of just **0.62%**, with top-10% performers in finance/investing hitting 18–20%.
  - Casey Newton (Platformer) started ~5% and stayed under 10% after a year. Lenny Rachitsky runs 4–5%. Tech content tends toward ~2%; political content runs 4–7% above average with outliers >15%.
  - **Rule for modeling:** 2–3% for a good niche publication, 1–2% for broad-audience, 5%+ only with proof.
- **Pricing:** the market anchor is **$10/month or $100/year** and has been stable since 2024. By vertical: investing $27/mo ($292/yr), finance $20, business $15, lifestyle ~$9, travel $7. List size barely affects the price ceiling — *niche* does. Never price below $5/mo; processing fees and low-price churn eat it.
- **LTV:** $83–230 per paid subscriber depending on vertical (investing highest). Subscriber lifetime 6–20 months.
- **Churn by vertical:** ~5%/mo at the good end (food & drink 5.06%) up to 16.7%/mo (money/trading — high-churn tourist subscribers). Annual billing dramatically flattens churn.
- **Solo-newsletter revenue math (Simon Owens' model):** to hit $100K/yr at $100/yr and 5% conversion you need ~20,000 free subs. A hybrid model (native ads at ~$50 CPM on the free list + paid tier) hits $100K with only ~14,000 free subs — **ads-plus-subscriptions beats subscriptions-only for almost every mid-size newsletter.**
- **Launch timing:** median paid launch happens 45 days after newsletter creation on beehiiv — earlier than most people think. Don't wait for a "big enough" list; the free list keeps growing after you turn on paid.

### 2.4 Owned paid communities (Circle-style)

From Circle's 55-community pricing dataset:
- **Most common price band: $29–99/month**; overall average ~$48/mo. Creative/hobby $10–39; business/professional $47–97; cohort programs $500–9,000 for 6–12 weeks; lifetime deals $297–3,499.
- **Pricing principles that hold up:**
  1. Work backward from an income target ($100K/yr = 500 members @ $17, 200 @ $42, or 100 @ $83 — pick the audience shape you can actually retain).
  2. Price signals quality — underpricing from insecurity is the most common error, and it breeds resentment toward your own members.
  3. Anchor to a measurable outcome, not to content volume.
  4. 2–3 tiers max; 4+ creates decision fatigue.
  5. Review pricing every 6 months; grandfather existing members when raising.
- **Conversion tactics with evidence behind them:** $1 first-month trials that renew at full price; $99 gateway products (mini-audits) with price credited toward membership ("converted almost 100% of the time" in one case); free monthly workshops with a 48-hour join window (~20% conversion); price anchoring against a high-ticket alternative.
- **Billing structure is a retention weapon:** annual plans retain ~92% of members over 12 months vs ~68% monthly. Quarterly billing is the sleeper tactic — one operator reported average retention stretching to 18 months after switching to quarterly-only. Standard practice: monthly + annual with annual discounted 15–20%.

### 2.5 Churn: the numbers that decide everything

- **Benchmarks:** elite membership sites 1–2%/mo; good 3–4%; content/creator memberships average 3–6%; e-learning 5–8%; paid-community market average 5–10%. **>5%/mo = fix retention now; >8%/mo = churn is your #1 business risk.** For scale: 5% monthly churn = 46% annual loss; 10% monthly = 70%+ annual.
- **Where churn concentrates (Elena Verna's data):** ~25% of customers churn after their *first* term regardless of billing cycle — the first renewal is the cliff. Of auto-renew cancellations, ~50% happen immediately post-signup and ~50% in the final week before renewal.
- **Involuntary churn is huge and fixable:** payment failures drive ~40% of first-term churn on annual plans; dunning/retry logic should recover 50%+ of failed payments. This is free money — check it before touching content strategy.
- **The retention playbook (in priority order):**
  1. Engineer a "first win" in week one — members who don't get one cancel quietly before month two.
  2. Aggressive payment-failure recovery (email + in-product).
  3. Convert monthlies to annual (target: 20% of the base per year).
  4. Offer pause instead of cancel; one-click reactivation (target 10% winback).
  5. Show accumulated usage/value in the cancellation flow.
  6. Churn is an *outcome* — the root causes are activation and engagement, so fix onboarding before adding perks.

## 3. Merchandise

### 3.1 The two production models

| | Print-on-demand (POD) | Bulk / owned inventory |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront capital | ~$0 | Significant (wholesale MOQs) |
| Per-unit cost | Higher | 2–3x lower per unit |
| Inventory risk | None | Full — dead stock is a real loss |
| Quality/packaging control | Limited | Full (custom packaging, premium blanks) |
| Fulfillment | Platform handles it | You or a 3PL |
| Best for | Testing, first launches, long-tail catalog | Proven evergreen sellers, premium drops |

- **Concrete POD math (Fourthwall catalog):** Bella+Canvas tee costs $11.75; sell at $22 → $10.25 profit; sell at $50 → $38.25. Fourthwall takes **no revenue cut on physical merch** — you pay product cost + shipping + card fees only, which is why it's the default for YouTube creators over Shopify+Printful stacks.
- **Margin targets:** healthy POD net margin is **25–40% after all costs**; treat 30% net as the floor before a product deserves shelf space.
- **The graduation rule:** start POD to test designs → once a design shows repeat demand and you have stable sizing data, move it to bulk for the margin uplift. This hybrid is the consensus recommendation, not a compromise.

### 3.2 The drops model (how good creator merch actually operates)

Always-on catalogs go stale. The operating model that works is **event-driven drops**:
1. **Tie the drop to a content moment** — an inside joke, a series finale, a milestone. Merch tied to a specific video/moment outsells generic logo merch because it's a badge of membership, not apparel.
2. **Build anticipation before the cart opens** — teasers, countdowns, early-access lists for members. "Most failed merch drops don't fail because of bad products — they fail because fans didn't know or care it was happening."
3. **Scarcity honestly applied** — limited windows or limited runs; POD makes limited *windows* easy with zero risk.
4. **Members get first/exclusive access** — merch and membership cross-sell each other; early access is a top-tier membership perk that costs nothing.
5. **Stagger designs across the year** rather than dumping the whole line at once — one big launch spikes then dies; a drop cadence builds a repeatable revenue rhythm.

### 3.3 Merch failure modes (from post-mortems across the industry)

- **Designing in isolation** — merch reflecting the creator's taste, not the community's. Fix: poll colorways/styles/product types before production; the poll itself is pre-launch marketing and demand validation.
- **Logo-on-a-tee syndrome** — 2025+ fans expect merch that works as standalone apparel/identity, not billboard cotton.
- **Ignoring fit/sizing** — a great design on a bad blank is a bad product and a refund stream; fit-test across the full size range before bulk.
- **Cheap blanks to protect margin** — quality problems convert directly into brand damage; the unboxing IS the brand experience for a direct-audience business.
- **No professional product photography** — phone photos signal amateur operation and depress conversion.
- **Structural failure:** "most creator merch doesn't fail from lack of demand — it fails because the setup gets too heavy, the launch takes too long, or the risk feels bigger than the upside." Bias toward the lightest viable setup (POD, existing platform) and fast launch.
- **Strategic note:** merch is dual-purpose — high-margin revenue *and* walking advertising. Under-priced merch worn widely can be a rational marketing spend, but decide that explicitly, don't back into it.

## 4. Digital products & courses

### 4.1 The economics

- **Margins: 85–95%** — courses, templates, presets, ebooks, coaching. Nothing else in the stack comes close.
- **Realistic launch math:** a creator with 50K followers selling a $49 digital product at a 2–4% buyer conversion generates ~$5–10K on launch. Scale linearly with audience and price.
- **Platform choice dominates outcomes:** Kajabi (own-platform, own-price) creators average ~$37,000/yr; Udemy (marketplace, race-to-$13-sale) instructors average ~$3,300/yr. **Own the platform and the pricing power.** Marketplaces are for lead-gen at best.
- **A LearnWorlds fitness-niche case:** 3.5% conversion, zero ad spend, $3,115 first launch — representative of a competent small first launch, not a failure.

### 4.2 Self-paced vs cohort — the decision that sets price and outcomes

| | Self-paced | Cohort-based |
|---|---|---|
| Completion rate | 10–20% | 85–96% |
| Price (same niche) | 1x | 3–5x |
| Operational load | Low (evergreen asset) | High (live delivery, fixed calendar) |
| Testimonials/results | Weak (few finishers) | Strong (results engine) |

- Adding **community support** to a self-paced course lifts completion from 10–15% to 70%+ — the cheapest hybrid.
- **Price affects completion, not just revenue:** students who pay $997+ complete at ~4x the rate of $97 students. Higher price = higher commitment = better outcomes = better testimonials = easier selling. Underpricing a serious course sabotages the product itself.
- **Results sell, curriculum doesn't:** courses showing documented student results convert ~380% better than courses showing only a syllabus. Run the first cohort cheap/free explicitly to harvest results, then price up.

### 4.3 Digital product ladder (standard sequencing)

1. **$0 lead magnet** — builds the email list (the list is the asset; every stream downstream keys off it).
2. **$20–50 template/preset/guide** — validates willingness to pay; impulse-price, no support burden.
3. **$100–300 self-paced course** — evergreen; accept low completion; funnel to community.
4. **$500–2,000 cohort or workshop** — the results engine; run 2–4x/yr.
5. **$2K+ coaching/consulting/done-with-you** — capped by founder time; use to fund and inform everything below, then productize out of it.

(Neptune Performance relevance: a training facility runs this exact ladder in physical form — free assessment → paid evaluation → program membership → premium coaching — and can layer *digital* rungs (remote programming, video analysis) at software margins on top of facility margins.)

## 5. Licensing & syndication

The least-used stream among creators and the most purely incremental for anyone with a real library.

### 5.1 What's licensable

- **Back-catalog episodic content** → FAST channels (Roku, Tubi, Pluto, Sling, Fubo, Plex, Fire TV) and social re-syndication (Snapchat, Facebook).
- **Clips/footage** → news, documentaries, compilation channels (per-clip fees or blanket deals).
- **Formats** → show concepts licensed to other markets/producers.
- **Music/audio** you own → sync licensing.
- **Brand/IP** → the Feastables path: the audience brand licensed or extended into physical product categories.

### 5.2 FAST-channel economics

- FAST ad revenue was projected to reach ~$6B by 2025 — a real, growing pool for library content.
- **Deal structures:** ad-inventory revenue splits typically open at **50/50 to 60/40**; alternatives are fixed license fees. Infrastructure vendors (Amagi, Wurl) sell distribution and ad-insertion à la carte if you want to keep more of the split and do more of the work.
- **Aggregator model (Mirage Digital as the case study):** founded 2021; ~300 creators on its roster; 60+ shows produced/repurposed across Snapchat, Facebook, Tubi, Fire TV; building a 10,000+ hour library drawing ~4B views and 20M subscribers. Mirage handles licensing/distribution "A to Z" and takes a cut of platform ad revenue. Creators get incremental money from content they already made, with zero new production load.
- **Performance signal:** Team Liquid's Fire TV expansion generated 1M+ watched minutes in two months — modest per-minute money, but pure margin on sunk-cost content.
- **Counter-thesis worth knowing (London Alley):** with YouTube now bigger on TV screens than Netflix, some studios argue creators should treat *YouTube itself* as the syndication endpoint ("indie television") rather than licensing off-platform. Both can be true: license the back catalog, premiere the new stuff natively.

### 5.3 Rights hygiene — the gating requirement

Licensing deals die on unclear rights. Non-negotiables from day one:
- **Music:** only licensed-for-redistribution or owned tracks in anything you might ever syndicate. Platform-library music (YouTube Audio Library etc.) often does NOT clear for off-platform distribution.
- **Contractor IP:** every editor/animator/composer contract assigns work-for-hire rights to the company, not the individual.
- **Talent/appearance releases** for recurring guests and featured people.
- **Brand-deal carve-outs:** sponsored segments may need cutting for syndication — keep clean masters (or an edit decision list) without integrations.
- Retroactively clearing a 500-video library is expensive-to-impossible; a rights checklist in the production pipeline costs nothing.

### 5.4 Sponsorship-adjacent licensing

Brand deals are shifting from one-off posts to **recurring content contracts** — 10+ pieces over ~3 months with multi-channel repurposing rights. When a brand wants to reuse your content in their own channels/ads, that's a *usage license* priced separately from the integration (commonly +25–100% of the integration fee depending on term and media spend). Never bundle usage rights in for free.

## 6. Revenue mix and sequencing for a maturing creator business

### 6.1 The maturity ladder

| Stage | Revenue reality | What to add next |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Pre-monetization | $0; building library + list | Email capture from day one |
| 2. Platform rent | AdSense dominant | First sponsorships (even small) |
| 3. Sponsor-led | Sponsors 50–70% of revenue | Membership (the recurring floor) |
| 4. Diversifying | Sponsors + ads + membership | Merch drops; first digital product |
| 5. Mature | No stream >40%; 4–6 streams | Courses/cohorts; licensing the library; owned brands |

- **Rule of thumb:** launch a new stream only when the current ones run without daily founder attention. Every stream is a small business; three half-run streams earn less than two well-run ones.
- **The membership-first argument (2026 consensus):** memberships have moved from "one option among many" to the *primary revenue foundation* for community-led creator businesses — the predictable base that de-risks everything else and the warm pool that buys every subsequent offer. Most paid communities charge in the $26–50/mo band.
- **Cross-stream flywheel:** members get merch early access → merch buyers get course discounts → course grads join the community → community feeds testimonials that sell the course → all of it grows the list that raises sponsorship value (first-party audience data is a sponsorship pricing lever).

### 6.2 What a mature mix looks like (directional targets)

For a YouTube-first media business at meaningful scale, a healthy diversified profile is roughly: sponsorships 30–40%, ad rev-share 15–25%, memberships/subscriptions 15–25%, merch 10–15%, digital products/courses 5–15%, licensing/other 5–10%. The precise split matters less than two tests: **no single stream >50%**, and **recurring (membership + subscription) revenue covers fixed payroll**. The MrBeast trajectory shows the end-state at the extreme: the owned consumer brand (Feastables, $180–520M/yr) dwarfing platform revenue ($70–100M) inside a ~$600–700M gross business where content functions as the marketing engine for owned products.

### 6.3 Quick modeling template

For any proposed stream, force these five numbers before greenlighting:
1. **Addressable audience** for this specific offer (not total subs — the engaged core).
2. **Conversion %** — use the benchmarks in this doc; take the *low* end.
3. **Price** — anchored to the niche tables above, not to gut feel.
4. **Monthly churn / repeat rate** — recurring streams live or die here.
5. **Founder-hours per month to run it** — divide projected profit by this; kill anything under ~3x the founder's effective hourly value unless it's strategic (list growth, brand).

## 7. Common mistakes

1. **Modeling conversion at platform-marketing numbers.** Substack says 5–10%; the median is ~3% (beehiiv median 0.62%). Underwriting a launch at 10% conversion is the most common spreadsheet fantasy in the space.
2. **Launching membership before there's a perk engine.** A membership without a sustainable members-only cadence churns at 8%+ and quietly dies. Design the recurring deliverable before the launch, and make it something already produced (BTS, early access, ad-free) — not new production load.
3. **Monthly-only billing.** Leaves the 92%-vs-68% annual retention gap and quarterly-billing gains on the table. Always offer annual at 15–20% off; consider quarterly default.
4. **Ignoring involuntary churn.** ~40% of first-term annual churn is failed payments. Turning on proper dunning is often worth more than any content improvement, and takes an afternoon.
5. **Bulk-ordering unproven merch.** Dead inventory is the classic creator-commerce capital destroyer. POD-test first, always; graduate winners only.
6. **Merch as catalog instead of drops.** Evergreen stores go stale; fans buy moments. Also: launching without pre-launch hype — drops fail from silence, not from bad product.
7. **Underpricing courses "to be accessible."** $97 students complete at a quarter the rate of $997 students; low price degrades outcomes, testimonials, and future pricing power simultaneously.
8. **Selling courses on marketplaces.** Udemy-style platforms average ~$3.3K/yr per instructor vs ~$37K own-platform. Marketplace = losing the customer relationship AND the price.
9. **Dirty rights in the library.** Unlicensed music and unassigned contractor IP make the entire back catalog unlicensable. Cheapest fix is prospective, not retroactive.
10. **Launching all streams at once.** Each stream is an operating business. Spreading founder attention across four half-launched streams is the standard way diversification *reduces* total revenue.
11. **Averaging power-law data.** "Average Patreon creator earns $X" is meaningless on a platform where the top 2% earn $25K+/mo and the median earns hundreds. Benchmark against the median of your niche and size, never the platform mean.
12. **Treating diversification as the goal instead of resilience.** The test isn't stream count; it's whether losing your biggest stream tomorrow leaves a viable business.

## 8. Questions Carl should ask

**Portfolio diagnosis**
- What % of trailing-12-month revenue is your largest stream? (>50% = concentration risk conversation.)
- Does recurring revenue cover fixed payroll? If not, what's the gap in members-at-current-ARPU?
- Which stream earns the most per founder-hour? Which earns the least — and why is it still running?

**Membership**
- What's your true engaged core (returning viewers / email openers), not subscriber count? What conversion % is your model assuming, and what's the niche benchmark?
- What's monthly churn, split voluntary vs. payment-failure? Is dunning turned on?
- What does a member get in week one? Can you name the "first win"?
- What % of members are on annual? What's the plan to move monthlies over?
- Is the members-only deliverable a byproduct of existing production, or new work?

**Merch**
- POD or inventory — and if inventory, what's the sell-through evidence that justified the capital?
- When was the last drop tied to a content moment? What was the pre-launch sequence?
- Net margin per SKU after all fees — which SKUs are under 30% and why do they exist?

**Courses/digital**
- Self-paced or cohort — and does the price match the format (cohort should be 3–5x)?
- What's the completion rate, and what documented student results exist to sell with?
- Do you own the platform and the customer email, or does a marketplace?

**Licensing**
- How many hours of episodic-shaped library exist? Is the music cleared for off-platform use? Do contractor agreements assign IP?
- Have you priced usage rights separately in your last three brand deals?

**Sequencing**
- Which existing stream is not yet systematized? (That's the argument against launching the next one.)
- If your top stream went to zero next quarter, what's the runway?
