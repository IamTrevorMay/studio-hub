# Film & Video Production Management

Production management is the discipline of converting a creative intention into a plan that survives contact with reality: a schedule that fits the money, a crew that fits the schedule, and paperwork that keeps 5–150 people synchronized to the minute. The core insight of a century of film practice: **every hour of pre-production buys roughly three hours on set and in the edit.** Everything below is in service of that trade.

## TL;DR

- **The shoot day is the most expensive unit of production.** Everything in pre-pro exists to reduce the number of shoot days and make each one denser. Budget shoot-day cost first, then work backward.
- **One document chain, no exceptions:** script/concept → breakdown → stripboard/shooting schedule → shot list → call sheet. Each derives from the one before. If a shoot has no call sheet, it has no plan — even a 3-person YouTube shoot benefits from a one-page version.
- **Scheduling math you can trust:** disciplined low-budget crews shoot 4–6 script pages/day and 20–35 setups/day; a simple two-person dialogue scene takes 30–90 minutes; every company move costs you 3–6 shots. Under-schedule on purpose and protect ~1 contingency day per 5 shoot days.
- **Budget skeleton:** Above-the-line 25–35%, below-the-line production 40–50%, post 10–20%, insurance/contingency 5–10%, with a hard-minimum 10% contingency. Real crew cost is 1.25–1.4× quoted day rates once kit fees, overtime, and payroll taxes land.
- **The line producer/1st AD split is the load-bearing wall:** one owns money and logistics, one owns time on set. On small teams these collapse into one "producer" — but the two functions must still both be explicitly assigned, or the director absorbs them and creative quality drops.
- **Pre-production kills more projects than production does.** The most common failure mode across indie film, corporate video, and YouTube is a rushed or skipped pre-pro: unlocked script, no tech scout, no shot list — which cascades into overtime, reshoots, and blown budgets.
- **Film discipline scales down to creator content.** MrBeast's leaked handbook is essentially line-producing doctrine translated to YouTube: identify critical components and check them daily, always have backup days, lock title/thumbnail (the "concept") before production, minimize crew, and never let one bottleneck silently stall a video.

## 1. Who does what: the production management stack

The film industry solved the org-chart problem decades ago. Even if one person wears four hats, know which hat is on.

| Role | Owns | Reports to |
|---|---|---|
| **Executive Producer** | Money in — financing, deals, top-level approvals | Financiers/company |
| **Producer** | The project — hires director & keys, owns delivery end-to-end | EP |
| **Line Producer** | Money out — builds and polices the budget line by line, hires BTL crew, negotiates vendors, signs off on the schedule's cost implications. The "COO of the production." | Producer |
| **Unit Production Manager (UPM)** | Day-to-day logistics execution — deals, permits, transport, payroll paperwork. On smaller shoots the LP and UPM are one person. | Line Producer |
| **Production Coordinator** | The production office — paperwork flow, travel, distribution of documents, keeping everyone's info current | UPM |
| **1st Assistant Director (1st AD)** | **Time on set.** Builds and owns the shooting schedule, runs the set on the day, calls the roll, keeps the day on schedule. The director's enforcer. | Director (creatively), LP (logistically) |
| **2nd AD** | The paperwork of people — call sheets, wrangling cast, background, next-day prep | 1st AD |
| **Production Assistant (PA)** | Whatever is needed — lockups, runs, distribution | Everyone |

Key dynamics:

- **The line producer is the bridge between above-the-line and below-the-line.** ATL = story rights, producers, director, principal cast (the creative deals). BTL = crew, equipment, locations, physical production. The LP translates creative ambition into a number, then defends the number.
- **The 1st AD is not a creative role; it is a throughput role.** A good 1st AD's product is a day that ends on time with the day's work complete. Directors who self-AD consistently blow schedules because the person deciding "one more take" is also the person supposed to say no.
- **Small-team collapse rule:** on a 3–8 person shoot (typical YouTube / corporate scale), you need at minimum two explicitly assigned functions besides the director/talent: (1) a *producer* function (budget, bookings, locations, releases) and (2) an *AD* function (schedule for the day, "we're moving on," time-of-day awareness). If nobody is named, the director does both badly.
- MrBeast's org proves this scales to creator content: a "head of creative" (concept quality) and a "head of production" (logistics and scheduling) as distinct named owners, with producers described as generalist problem-solvers who coordinate contractors, schedule crews, manage budgets, and troubleshoot on site.

## 2. The pre-production spine

The canonical document chain. Each artifact is generated from the previous one — never freelance a downstream document while an upstream one is unlocked.

1. **Locked script / locked concept.** In film: the numbered shooting script. In YouTube terms: locked title + thumbnail + beat outline *before production begins* (MrBeast rule — the title/thumbnail is the promise; production exists to fulfill it exactly, down to "yellow bouncy castle, not red").
2. **Script breakdown.** Scene-by-scene tagging of every element with a cost or logistics implication: cast, extras, props, wardrobe, vehicles, stunts, SFX, animals, special equipment, locations. Scenes are measured in **eighths of a page** (a 1-page scene = 8/8). AI breakdown tools (e.g. Filmustage) now auto-tag elements with ~95% claimed accuracy — useful first pass, still needs human review.
3. **Stripboard / shooting schedule.** Each scene becomes one strip carrying scene number, INT/EXT, DAY/NIGHT, location, cast IDs, page count. Traditional color code: **white = day interior, yellow = day exterior, blue = night interior, green = night exterior, black = day separator.** The 1st AD sequences strips into shoot days.
4. **Day Out of Days (DOOD).** A matrix of which cast member works which day (Start / Work / Hold / Finish). Drives cast cost — actors on "hold" days often still get paid. On small shoots this becomes "which contributor/guest is needed when."
5. **Shot list (and storyboards/previs for complex work).** Director + DP's per-scene list of setups. This is the crew's checklist on the day.
6. **Budget** (see §5) — built alongside the schedule, iterated against it. Schedule and budget are one system: days × burn rate.
7. **Call sheet** (see §4) — the daily output document, issued the night before each shoot day.
8. **Tech scout.** Keys walk every location before the shoot: power, light, sound conditions, parking, load-in, bathrooms, cell coverage, sun path. Skipping the tech scout is a classic low-budget error — discovering on the day that the lighting package doesn't match the location's power eats hours.

Timeline benchmarks: breaking down a feature script to a locked stripboard takes a focused scheduler **1–2 weeks**; a short film or commercial takes **1–3 days**. Read-through with department heads happens early to surface problems while they're still cheap.

**Pre-production checklist (any scale):**

- [ ] Script/concept locked and versioned; everyone on the same draft
- [ ] Breakdown complete; every scene's elements tagged
- [ ] Locations confirmed **in writing**, with permits/insurance certificates where required
- [ ] Tech scout done at each location (power, sound, light, logistics)
- [ ] Cast/talent confirmed with call times; releases prepared; wardrobe guidance sent (no busy patterns, bright whites, or third-party logos)
- [ ] Crew booked with deal memos (rate, hours, OT terms, kit fee)
- [ ] Equipment list quoted and reserved; backup for single-points-of-failure (media, batteries, audio)
- [ ] Shooting schedule circulated to department heads for feedback, then locked
- [ ] Shot list finished and shared — not in the director's head
- [ ] Budget approved with ≥10% contingency
- [ ] Meals/craft services arranged ($35–75/person per catered meal; $8–15/person/day craft services)
- [ ] Weather plan / cover set for exteriors
- [ ] Call sheet drafted; distribution list confirmed
- [ ] Data workflow decided: media, backup redundancy, who is DIT, naming convention

## 3. Shooting schedule: the math and the ordering logic

### Throughput benchmarks (memorize these)

- **Pages/day:** studio features shoot 1.5–3 pages/day; disciplined low-budget shoots 4–6; simple content days can hit 5–8+. A 90-page micro-budget feature at 5 pages/day = 18 days.
- **Screen minutes/day:** across reference low-budget films, ~**5.5 finished minutes per day** is a realistic average.
- **Setups/day:** 20–35 distinct setups on an efficient, controlled-location day. 12–16 for a lean indie dialogue day is comfortable; 20+ complex setups pushes into overtime.
- **Scene timing:** a simple two-character, single-room dialogue scene = **30–90 minutes** (4–6 such scenes fit a 10–12 hour day); a complex dialogue scene = 90–180 minutes (2–4 scenes/day).
- **Page-time conversion (granular):** one-eighth of a page ≈ 15 minutes of shooting time — a coarse but usable estimator for unfamiliar material.
- **Company moves cost 3–6 shots each.** Treat every move as buying a location with shots.
- **Night work halves your expected pace**, and stacked night blocks need reset days after.
- **Shooting ratio planning:** narrative ~7:1–25:1; documentary 20:1–80:1+ (often quoted ~60:1); talking-head YouTube can run 3:1–5:1. Ratio drives both media budget and edit time — a doc-style YouTube shoot with 3 roaming cameras generates edit hours far beyond a scripted piece of the same runtime.

### How the 1st AD orders the strips

The schedule starts chronological, then gets reorganized by these forces (roughly in priority order):

1. **Location groupings** — shoot everything at a location consecutively; location count is the #1 cost driver after cast.
2. **Cast availability & DOOD efficiency** — minimize hold days; shoot expensive/limited talent out first.
3. **Day/night turnaround** — you cannot night-shoot someone and call them the next morning; turnaround minimums (typically 10–12h) are contractual on union shows and sane everywhere.
4. **Complexity placement** — big stunts/effects/emotional scenes early in the day and early in the week while crew is fresh; never schedule the hardest scene last on a Friday.
5. **Equipment windows** — specialty gear (crane, Steadicam, drone op) grouped into the fewest rental days.
6. **Weather/season/sun** — exteriors front-loaded with a cover set (an interior you can jump to) on standby.
7. **Geography-once rule** — when at a location, shoot all the wide "geography" coverage in one lighting state, then punch in.

### Schedule-protection rules

- **Under-schedule deliberately.** Plan the day to ~85% of theoretical capacity; the surplus absorbs reality.
- **One contingency day per five shoot days.** Cheap insurance vs. reshoot mobilization cost.
- **12-hour day maximum** as policy. Beyond that: OT at 1.5× after hour 10, 2× after hour 12, plus fatigue-driven quality and safety decay. Meal every 6 hours or you owe meal penalties (union) or resentment (everywhere).
- **Backup days beat perfect locations.** MrBeast doctrine, but pure line-producing: if you can't secure a backup day at a location, prefer a more flexible partner over a prettier space.
- **Compress the calendar with weekend blocks.** The "8-day feature" pattern — 4 weekends × 2 days, one location per weekend, review footage between blocks — is a strong template for any small team shooting around day jobs; it builds in edit-informed course correction.

## 4. The call sheet

The call sheet is the single daily source of truth. It is generated from the shooting schedule + breakdown, built by the 2nd AD (approved by the 1st AD) or, on small shoots, by whoever holds the producer hat. **Send once, the night before, with delivery confirmation** — chasing "did you see the update?" texts on the morning of the shoot is a failure state.

### Anatomy (full-scale)

**Header block:**
- Production title, company, agency/client if applicable
- Shoot date + "Day X of Y"
- **General crew call** (big, unmissable) and individual cast call times
- Weather forecast incl. sunrise/sunset (drives exterior planning)
- **Nearest 24-hour emergency room** with address — non-negotiable, even on tiny shoots
- Parking instructions, load-in notes, special announcements
- Key contacts: producer, coordinator, director, UPM, 1st AD (names + phones)

**Body:**
- Location(s) with exact addresses and any access notes
- The day's schedule: scenes in shoot order with scene numbers, INT/EXT, D/N, page counts, cast required per scene, and meal breaks
- Cast grid: actor, character, status (SWF), pickup/HMU/on-set times
- Crew list by department with call times
- Department notes (art, wardrobe, G&E, camera specials)
- Company move logistics if any; walkie channels on larger shows
- Advance schedule: tomorrow's scenes (lets departments prep)

### Call sheet lite (creator/corporate scale, one page)

Date & day-of-days · call time & wrap target · location + parking + bathroom note · schedule blocks with times · who's needed when · shot list reference · gear checklist owner · meals plan · weather · nearest ER · two phone numbers. Ten minutes to make; saves an hour of morning chaos and is the difference between a "shoot" and "some people with cameras."

## 5. Budgeting a shoot

### The top sheet skeleton

| Section | % of total | Contents |
|---|---|---|
| Above-the-line | 25–35% (16–21% on micro-budgets) | Story/rights (1–5%), producer (2–4%), director (2–8%), principal cast (10–25%) |
| Below-the-line production | 40–50% | Crew, equipment, locations, art, transport, catering |
| Post-production | 10–20% (never below 10%) | Editorial, sound, color, VFX, music |
| Other | 5–10% | Insurance (1.5–3%), completion bond (2–3% where required), overhead, **contingency (min 10% of production+post)** |

Reference tier ($1M indie): ATL 22%, BTL 42%, post 12.5%, other 12.7%, fringes 10.8%.

### Department-level BTL rules of thumb

- **Grip & electric: 8–15% of BTL** — often the largest BTL department; generators chronically underestimated.
- **Camera: 4–8%** — body rentals from ~$3–10K/week (Alexa-class).
- **Art/production design: 5–20%**; **locations & permits: 5–15%** (police/fire/parking are the hidden lines); **transport: 5–10%**; wardrobe 2–5%; HMU 1–4%; production sound 1–3%.
- **Fringes: union pension/health/FICA/unemployment add 35–45% on top of every union rate**, ≈8–12% of the whole budget. A budget with no fringe line is understated by that much.
- Post lines (indie scale): editorial $60–200K, color $10–40K, sound design/mix $25–80K, music $15–100K+; VFX from $2–5K per simple composite.

### Red flags in any budget

1. ATL over ~50% — the shoot itself is starved.
2. Post under 10% — the edit will be rushed or the money found later at 2× pain.
3. No fringe/payroll-burden line — real labor cost is 25–45% above the rate card.
4. No contingency, or contingency being "spent" in the plan.
5. Equipment/locations priced from memory instead of written quotes.

### Small-shoot budget template (creator / corporate / facility-content scale)

For a 1–3 day branded or YouTube shoot, the same structure compresses to: **crew labor (biggest line, price at 1.25–1.4× day rates)** · gear rental · location/permits · talent/appearance fees · art/props/wardrobe · meals · travel/transport · post (edit days × editor rate + music licensing + color/mix) · 10–15% contingency. Music licensing and OT are the two most-missed lines in this tier.

The film "2.5 rule" (a theatrical film must gross ~2.5× budget to break even) has a creator analog Carl should apply: know the revenue a video must clear (AdSense + sponsor allocation) before green-lighting production spend, and treat any single video whose cost exceeds its P50 revenue as a strategic/portfolio bet, priced deliberately.

### Where the money actually leaks

- Overtime cascades: one blown schedule day triggers OT (1.5×/2×), extended rentals, and rebooked locations simultaneously.
- Kit fees discovered at invoice time (see §6).
- "While we're here" scope creep on set — extra setups no one costed.
- Reshoots caused by audio failure or missing coverage — the cheapest insurance is a dedicated sound person and a shot list that someone checks off.

## 6. Crewing up

### 2025–26 non-union day-rate benchmarks (10–12h day, mid-size US markets; LA = 1.0×)

| Role | Range/day |
|---|---|
| Director of Photography | $800–3,000 (commercial verified bookings $900–2,400) |
| 1st AD | $700–1,500 |
| Gaffer / Key Grip | $600–1,200 |
| Grip / Electrician | $400–700 |
| Sound Mixer | $600–1,200 |
| Boom Op | $400–700 |
| 1st AC | $500–900 |
| HMU / Art / Wardrobe roles | $400–2,000 by seniority |
| PA | $150–400 |
| Editor | $500–1,500 |
| Colorist | $700–2,500 |

City multipliers: NYC 0.95–1.0×; Atlanta/Chicago/Austin 0.8–0.9×; Toronto/Vancouver 0.9–0.95×. Union adds 15–40% plus benefits and strict OT rules.

**The 1.25–1.4× rule:** budget 1.25–1.4× the quoted rate per head to cover kit fees ($25–750/day for owner-op gear — roughly 10–15% of crew cost), OT contingency (10–20%), and payroll taxes/processing. Day rates alone are only ~60–70% of true crew cost.

### Minimum viable crew configurations

- **Solo+ (2):** shooter-director + 1 utility (audio, slate/logging, safety, driving). The floor for anything with a schedule.
- **Doc/YouTube standard (4–6):** director/host · DP · sound mixer · producer-AD · PA (+ 2nd camera on interview or event days). Dedicated sound is the first hire that pays for itself — audio failure is the #1 unfixable defect.
- **Commercial/narrative small unit (8–15):** adds gaffer, key grip, 1st AC, HMU, art, dedicated 2nd AD/coordinator.
- MrBeast counter-lesson: **minimize crew size — only people who are necessary on set**, and cross-train (their production staff are all expected to competently operate a camera, which removes the marginal-specialist cost of overflow scenes).

### Hiring practice

- Deal memos in writing for everyone: rate, guaranteed hours, OT terms, kit fee, credit, payment timing. Ambiguity here is where relationships die.
- Hire keys (DP, gaffer, sound) who have worked together before; a crew with shorthand is worth 10–20% schedule speed (the explicit basis of every 2-to-4-day feature ever pulled off).
- Feed people well. Catering is the highest-ROI morale line on any budget.

## 7. On-set efficiency playbook

**Shape of a good day:**
1. Crew call → safety/blocking meeting (5 min, 1st AD runs it)
2. **First shot within 60–90 minutes of call.** Time-to-first-shot is the single best leading indicator of the whole day.
3. Block → light → rehearse → tweak → shoot; talent in HMU/wardrobe while lighting happens (parallelize, never serialize)
4. 1st AD calls the schedule out loud all day ("this is the martini" / "moving on") — everyone always knows where the day stands
5. Meal by hour 6; afternoon re-attack; wrap call honored
6. **Wrap ritual:** media backed up twice before anyone leaves; gear checked against the list; 10-minute production debrief; call sheet for tomorrow confirmed

**Speed techniques (all sourced from working ADs/DPs):**
- Shoot all coverage in one lighting direction before turning around; group all setups sharing a look.
- Pre-light the next scene while shooting the current one (needs a gaffer with a swing PA).
- Performance-first coverage — master, singles, occasional insert — is the fastest pattern that still cuts.
- Reusable coverage templates for repeating scene types (interview A-cam/B-cam grid, product tabletop layouts) — decide once, execute many times. This is the film version of the YouTube "format."
- Limit takes by policy (2–3 unless the director states why); "one more for safety" is a tax that compounds.
- B-camera for cross-coverage on dialogue halves setup count — the workhorse of every fast schedule.
- Director must know what coverage is *not* needed; default "full coverage of everything" is the slowest possible plan.
- Never let talent/contestants sit idle in bad conditions (>3h waiting is a MrBeast hard line; also a talent-relations rule everywhere).
- If something goes wrong, ask if it's content (doc/creator shoots) before treating it purely as a loss.

**Safety & compliance floor:** ER on the call sheet, water/shade, weapons/stunts/vehicles get a dedicated professional and a safety meeting, minors have strict hour limits, certificates of insurance for locations and rentals. Cheap shoots that skip insurance are betting the company.

## 8. Applying film discipline to YouTube-scale production

This is where Carl earns his fee with a client like a creator-led media company. The failure mode of creator teams is not lack of creativity — it's producing every video as an improvisation. The fix is not "hire a film crew"; it's adopting the *documents and roles* at 10% of the weight.

**Translation table:**

| Film artifact | Creator equivalent |
|---|---|
| Locked script | Locked title + thumbnail + beat outline (before shooting — MrBeast treats this as inviolable) |
| Script breakdown | Elements list per video: props, guests, locations, builds, b-roll needs |
| Stripboard/schedule | Batch calendar: which videos shoot which days, grouped by location/setup |
| DOOD | Guest/collaborator availability grid |
| Shot list | Beat-by-beat shot plan + standing coverage templates per format |
| Call sheet | One-page call sheet lite (§4) |
| Line producer | Producer/ops lead owning budget-per-video and bookings |
| 1st AD | Whoever calls "moving on" — named per shoot |
| Dailies/wrap report | Same-day media backup + 5-line shoot recap in the ops tool |

**Doctrine worth stealing directly from the MrBeast handbook (it is line-producing in creator clothing):**
- **Critical components:** identify the element without which the video doesn't exist; check it daily ("10× a day" in their telling), always hold a backup, and never let it ride on a coin flip. This is completion-bond thinking applied to a bouncy castle.
- **Bottleneck protocol:** name the blocking person explicitly, agree a deadline together, then check in daily — not at the deadline. Videos are assigned 45+ days out so this cadence has room to work.
- **Creativity saves money:** before spending, spend thinking (their example: a year of Doritos, $1,825, beat a $20,000 cash prize as content). Corollary: if you're spending >$10K and it won't be visible on camera, re-examine it.
- **Backup days > perfect locations.** Schedule risk is bought down in pre-pro, not heroics.
- **Video everything in pre-pro** — scouts, builds, decisions — stored where the team can find it. (Tech scout culture, digitized.)
- **Communication escalates with stakes:** text < call < in person; confirm receipt; go up-then-over in the chain, never silently around it.
- **Coverage insurance for unscripted content:** when moments are unrepeatable, cameras roll continuously (their extreme: 300 cameras on 100 contestant cubes). The generic rule — match camera redundancy to the repeatability of the moment. Interview: 2 cameras. Once-ever stunt: every angle you can afford.

**Batching is the creator's stripboard.** Grouping videos by location/setup/look and shooting 2–4 per day is exactly the film logic of grouping strips by location — one commercial team shot 14 deliverables in a day this way. For a hybrid business (e.g., a media company that also runs a training facility), standing sets + fixed lighting + coverage templates turn facility content into a near-zero-marginal-cost production line: a permanently rigged corner of a baseball facility is, in film terms, a pre-lit standing set.

**Shooting-ratio discipline in post:** unscripted creator content trends toward doc ratios (20:1+), which silently transfers cost from the shoot to the edit. Manage it at the source: log and select on shoot day, have editors assemble during multi-day shoots (the MrBeast practice), and track edit-hours-per-published-minute as a KPI just like pages/day.

## 9. Common mistakes

1. **Rushed or skipped pre-production** — the root cause behind most other failures; unlocked scripts and missing shot lists cascade into every department.
2. **No tech scout** → power/light/sound surprises that eat half a shoot day.
3. **Scheduling to 100% of theoretical capacity** with no contingency days; first slip triggers OT, extended rentals, and morale collapse simultaneously.
4. **No named AD function** — nobody empowered to say "moving on," so the day is run by the person least incentivized to stop shooting.
5. **Budgeting raw day rates** — forgetting kit fees, OT, fringes/payroll burden (real cost 1.25–1.4×; union fringes +35–45%).
6. **Post under-budgeted (<10%)** and music licensing forgotten entirely.
7. **Audio treated as an afterthought** — the classic unfixable defect; exposure drift and undisciplined coverage are the other silent killers.
8. **Full-coverage-by-default shooting** — burning setups nobody will cut in, while the schedule dies.
9. **Company-move blindness** — schedules that hop locations without pricing the 3–6 lost shots per move.
10. **Night shoots planned at daytime pace**, and night blocks stacked without reset days.
11. **Verbal deals** with crew, locations, or talent — no deal memos, no written location confirmations, no releases.
12. **Call sheet churn** — multiple revisions on shoot-day morning, or no call sheet at all.
13. **Single points of failure unprotected** — one media card, one battery charger, one irreplaceable prop with no backup plan and no daily check-in.
14. **Waiting until the deadline to discover a bottleneck** — no daily-cadence follow-up on critical-path items.
15. **Cast/talent left idle for hours** — burns goodwill and (in unscripted work) on-camera energy you can't fake.

## 10. Questions Carl should ask

**Diagnosing a client's production maturity:**
1. Show me the last call sheet you sent. (No call sheet = no system; the answer tells you everything.)
2. Who, by name, decides "we're moving on" on your set? Who owns the budget number?
3. What's your time from crew call to first shot rolling?
4. What did your last shoot cost per finished published minute, and per shoot day, all-in (incl. OT, kit fees, meals, post)?
5. Is the title/thumbnail/concept locked before you shoot? What percentage of shoots start without it?
6. What's your shooting ratio, and how many edit-hours per published minute? Is that trending up?
7. When something slips, where does it show up — overtime, reshoots, missed uploads, or editor crunch?
8. What are the critical components of your next shoot, who checks on each one daily, and what's the backup for each?
9. How many setups/videos do you get from one location day? Could batching double it?
10. Do you have written deal memos, location agreements, releases, and insurance — or handshakes?
11. What's your contingency — in money (≥10%?) and in schedule (a backup day)?
12. What happens to the media in the first hour after wrap? How many copies exist before anyone sleeps?
13. Which recurring formats have a standing coverage template, lighting plan, and gear cart — and which get reinvented every time?
14. If your best shooter got sick tomorrow, does the shoot happen? (Cross-training / documentation test.)
15. For a facility or standing-set business: what's rigged permanently vs. rebuilt every shoot?
