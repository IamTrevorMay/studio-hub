# Brand Strategy & Positioning

Positioning craft for media brands and local businesses: April Dunford's method, Ehrenberg-Bass distinctiveness science, category design, personal-vs-company brand architecture, naming, and brand promise.

## TL;DR

- **Positioning is a decision, not a tagline.** Use Dunford's 5 components: competitive alternatives → differentiated capabilities → value → who cares most → market category. Run it as a workshop, in that order, never backwards from a slogan.
- **List the status quo as competitor #1.** B2B/service vendors lose ~50% of deals (some 80%+) to "do nothing / keep doing what we're doing" — a training facility competes with a dad and a bucket of balls, a media brand competes with the viewer's default watch habits.
- **Differentiation gets the first purchase; distinctiveness gets the repeat purchase.** Ehrenberg-Bass: buyers satisfice and compare only ~2 options. Build distinctive brand assets (colors, logo, catchphrase, character, intro sound) and *never* change them; build mental availability around category entry points.
- **For a small player, "big fish, small pond" beats category creation 9 times out of 10.** Category design (Play Bigger) is a multi-year, capital-hungry play; category kings capture ~76% of category economics, but a mis-timed category rename (see DocuSign's "Agreement Cloud") confuses a market that already worked.
- **Personal brand + company brand should be a hybrid, deliberately architected.** Founder/athlete face = top of funnel (trust, attention); company brands = the sellable assets. ~45% of company reputation is tied to its CEO — use the face, but design around key-person risk from day one.
- **A niche name and niche positioning are worth real money for a local facility:** niche-positioned studios show ~23% lower churn than generalists, and 41% of members will travel farther for a specialization they can't get nearby — proximity (62% top factor) is beatable with specificity.
- **Name things by construct, not vibes:** descriptive / suggestive / abstract each trade off clarity vs. ownability. Prescreen USPTO before falling in love. Suggestive names are the most common winner for new brands.
- **When a team says "we have no differentiation," it's almost always differentiation blindness, not truth.** Diagnose with win analysis (not loss obsession) and tighter segmentation before touching the product.

## 1. What positioning actually is (and isn't)

**Definition (Dunford):** Positioning is deliberately defining how you are *the best at something that a specific set of customers cares a lot about*. It is context-setting: the frame of reference the buyer uses to understand what you are, who it's for, and why it matters.

What positioning is **not**:
- Not a tagline, mission statement, or vision statement (those are downstream outputs).
- Not messaging (messaging is how you express the position; positioning is the strategic input).
- Not a fix for a bad product. Dunford: "Positioning can't fix your crummy product." If there is genuinely no differentiated value, the fix is product/pricing/service design, not words.
- Not permanent. It's a living decision that must be revisited when the product, competitors, or customers shift. Position "what's on the truck" — current capabilities, not the roadmap.

**Why it matters:** weak positioning makes every downstream function harder — ads convert worse, sales cycles lengthen, content gets clicked by the wrong people, pricing gets anchored to the wrong comparison set. A single shift in positioning can be the difference between a product that flops and one that breaks through. Positioning is the cheapest growth lever most small companies never deliberately pull.

## 2. The Dunford framework (Obviously Awesome)

### 2.1 The Five (+1) Components — in strict order

The order matters because each component is derived from the previous one. Most positioning failures come from starting at #5 (market category) and working backwards.

1. **Competitive alternatives** — what would the customer do if you didn't exist? Includes status quo, DIY, spreadsheets, "hire a kid," "do nothing." NOT just lookalike competitors.
2. **Unique attributes/capabilities** — features, delivery model, expertise, business model elements that the alternatives *don't* have. Facts, provable.
3. **Value (and proof)** — what those attributes *enable* for the customer. Translate features into outcomes. In B2B everything ultimately reduces to making money or saving money — but don't over-abstract; keep the causal chain visible ("1,000x faster queries" → "answer customers before they churn," not just "saves money").
4. **Target market characteristics** — who cares *a lot* about that value. Not a demographic; a set of characteristics that make the value urgent (e.g., "small team, no dev resources, strategic about time").
5. **Market category** — the frame of reference you declare so buyers can slot you into a mental shelf and infer everything else (pricing norms, feature expectations, competitors).
6. **(Bonus) Relevant trend** — optional accelerant that answers "why now." Dangerous when the trend has no obvious connection to the product (trend-washing: slapping "AI" on everything).

### 2.2 The 10-step process (workshop form)

1. Understand the customers who **love** the product — study best-fit customers, not average ones.
2. Form a cross-functional positioning team — marketing + sales (≥2 experienced AEs) + product + CS + founder. Positioning done solo by marketing gets rejected by the org.
3. Align vocabulary and **let go of positioning baggage** — the market frame you started with is a hypothesis, not an identity.
4. List true competitive alternatives — from the *customer's* shortlist, grouped (status quo / DIY / general tools / dedicated tools).
5. Isolate unique attributes — provable capabilities alternatives lack.
6. Map attributes to **value themes** — cluster into 2-4 themes max (10+ themes = you've said nothing).
7. Determine who cares a lot — segment characteristics, tighten until the value is obviously urgent.
8. Find a market frame of reference that puts your strengths at the center — and pick a positioning style (below).
9. Layer on a trend — only if it genuinely connects.
10. Capture it in a shareable positioning canvas — so sales, product, and content all execute from the same document.

### 2.3 The three positioning styles

| Style | When | Risk |
|---|---|---|
| **Head-to-head** ("we're the best X") | You can plausibly win the existing category leader fight — rarely true for small players | Outspent and out-defaulted by the incumbent |
| **Big fish, small pond** (subsegment the category: "X for Y") | Default best move for small companies; existing demand + carve-out you can dominate | Pond too small, or subsegment doesn't actually value your differentiators |
| **Create a new category** | The problem is genuinely new and no frame exists; you have capital + years | Expensive market education; buyers have no mental shelf; see §4 |

### 2.4 Worked case: Userlist (SaaS, tiny team)

Textbook 10-step application, useful as the pattern to copy:
- **Alternatives found (4 groups):** manual follow-up, in-house build, general email tools (Drip/ConvertKit), dedicated messaging platforms (Intercom/Customer.io).
- **Value themes (3):** reduce implementation effort; know each user's situation; improve adoption/retention.
- **Who cares most:** small SaaS teams, DIY-minded, strategic about time.
- **Move:** shifted category from "email automation" to "customer messaging for SaaS" — big fish, small pond. Named competitors directly in copy: "more efficient than building it yourself; less complex than Intercom or Customer.io."
- **Result:** immediate prospect clarity, better launch conversion. Note the honesty of the sentence — it concedes what it isn't. Conceding is a positioning power move; it earns believability.

**Carl-relevance:** the identical pattern works for a baseball facility ("velocity development for high-school pitchers, more individualized than a big-box academy, more data-driven than a private coach's garage") and a media company pitching sponsors ("more engaged than a podcast ad, cheaper CPM than a mid-tier MCN packaged buy, with an athlete's credibility no lifestyle channel has").

## 3. Advanced positioning: the four roadblocks & the "no differentiation" illusion

### 3.1 Dunford's four roadblocks (why positioning projects stall)

1. **Disagreement about competitive alternatives.** Each function has a bias: marketing overweights loud competitors (share of voice ≠ share of lost deals), product lists "horizon competitors" not yet in any deal, sales has whale bias (one painful lost deal dominates the list), founders carry outdated views. Fix: ask "if we didn't exist, what would the prospect do?" and stay in near-term reality.
2. **Product pessimism.** Negative product teams infect sales/marketing with false "we always lose on features" narratives. Fix: bring evidence from wins; require ≥2 AEs in the room.
3. **Poorly defined differentiated value.** Symptoms: assuming prospects infer why a feature matters; over-abstracting ("saves money"); 10+ value themes; confusing value (reason to buy) with objection handling (reason not to buy).
4. **Unclear what's being positioned.** Product vs. portfolio vs. company — each needs its own positioning; conflating them produces mush. (Directly relevant to a founder juggling a personal brand, a media co, and a facility — three positioning exercises, not one.)

### 3.2 The status quo is the real competitor

- B2B vendors typically lose ~50% of opportunities to whatever the prospect currently does; some see 80%+. Roughly half of those are *indecision*, not preference.
- Sales logs these as "no decision," so the status quo never makes the competitor list — and every downstream positioning choice is calibrated against the wrong baseline.
- Practical consequence: your first messaging job is usually to sell *change* (cost of inaction, risk-of-doing-nothing) before selling *you*.

### 3.3 "We have no differentiation" — the illusion

When a team claims no differentiation, either (rare) it's true — fix the product — or (common) it's **differentiation blindness**. Dunford's six root causes:

1. **Value blindness** — execs never talk to customers; the more sales exposure marketing gets, the smarter the positioning.
2. **Product illiteracy** — nobody can explain why the advanced capability matters.
3. **Inside-out competition thinking** — obsessing over competitors that never appear on customer shortlists.
4. **Loss obsession** — studying only lost deals; wins carry the real signal about what resonates.
5. **Product pessimism** — see above.
6. **Sloppy segmentation** — value is contextual; a broad target market averages away the segments where your differentiators are decisive. (Her example: an $80M company growing 35%/yr whose execs claimed the product was unsellable — segmentation work revealed strong differentiation.)

**Diagnostic sequence Carl should run:** true shortlist competitors → list capabilities they lack → map to value → tighten segment until "who cares a lot" is obvious. If the chain breaks at step 2, it's a product problem, not a positioning problem.

## 4. Category design basics (Play Bigger) — and when NOT to do it

### 4.1 Core claims

- **Category king economics:** the company that defines and dominates a new category captures ~70-80% of the category's total economics (the widely cited figure is **76% of market cap** in the space). Everyone else splits the remainder.
- Category design = simultaneously designing the **category** (the problem/market), the **company**, and the **product** — the "design trio." All three must reinforce each other.
- **POV (point of view):** a written, emotionally resonant narrative that (a) names the problem, (b) contrasts today's broken state with a new possibility, (c) frames your company as the inevitable answer. Fact-finding → problem statement → vision → evangelization. Written down so the whole org says the same thing.
- **Lightning strikes:** concentrated, high-visibility launch events ~3-6 months after POV approval that reveal the category (blueprint, taxonomy, use cases, ecosystem) — deliberately overwhelming force at one moment rather than dribbled announcements.

### 4.2 When category design is the wrong move

- It's a **multi-year, capital-intensive** market-education program. Small companies almost always do better with big-fish-small-pond inside an existing category (buyers already have a mental shelf and budget line).
- **Cautionary case:** DocuSign's rebrand from "e-signature" (a category it owned, buyers understood) to "Agreement Cloud" triggered market confusion and pushback — the existing category *already worked*. Rule: never abandon a category you're winning to chase a bigger abstract one.
- Lightweight version for small players: don't create a category — create a **named methodology** inside an existing one. A facility can own "The [X] Protocol"; a media brand can own a named show format. You get category-king-like ownership at niche scale, trademark-ably, without the education bill. (See §7 fitness data: named signature methodologies are a proven differentiator.)

## 5. Distinctiveness vs. differentiation (Ehrenberg-Bass / Byron Sharp)

### 5.1 The two concepts — precisely

- **Differentiation** = *meaning*: standing for something functionally/emotionally different than competitors. Lives in the value proposition.
- **Distinctiveness** = *memory*: the buyer can recognize the brand instantly and attribute the impression to the right brand. Lives in sensory identity.

Sharp's *How Brands Grow* (2010) argument: most buyers don't deeply care about differentiation. They **satisfice** — research shows most buyers compare only the ~2 most popular options rather than surveying the market. Brands grow primarily through **mental availability** (being thought of in buying situations) and **physical availability** (being easy to find/buy), driven by reaching light and non-buyers, not by loyalty programs aimed at heavy buyers. Mark Ritson's gloss: "it's more about salience than it is about image" — and this holds across B2C, B2B, services, and SaaS.

### 5.2 Distinctive Brand Assets (DBAs)

Sensory cues that identify the brand *without the name*: color (Tiffany blue), logo shape (golden arches), character/mascot, catchphrase, sonic logo/intro sound, typeface, layout pattern, packaging shape.

Two tests (Romaniuk), and the bar is high:
- **Fame** — what % of category buyers link the asset to you.
- **Uniqueness** — what % link it *only* to you (most important).
An asset is only investable when high on both. Unlike differentiation claims, DBAs are trademark-able and compound with repetition — which means the cardinal sin is *refreshing* them. Consistency over decades is the strategy.

### 5.3 Category Entry Points (CEPs)

The buying situations/triggers in which a brand can come to mind ("Friday night, tired, kids hungry" → pizza brands). Growth = being linked to *more* CEPs than competitors. Practical shift: instead of one positioning statement, map the 5-15 situations in which your buyer's need arises and build memory links to each.

- Media brand CEPs: "bored on a lunch break," "big game just ended and I want analysis," "my kid asked how pitchers train."
- Facility CEPs: "son got cut from the team," "travel-ball tryouts in 8 weeks," "velocity plateau," "coming back from elbow soreness (post-clearance)," "college recruiting video needed."

### 5.4 Reconciling Dunford and Sharp (Carl's synthesis)

These schools *feel* opposed but operate at different altitudes and buying contexts:
- **Dunford wins** in considered, high-involvement purchases (B2B SaaS, a $2-5k training program, a sponsor's $50k package): a shortlist exists, differentiation is evaluated, positioning determines whether you make the shortlist framed favorably.
- **Sharp wins** in low-involvement, repeat, memory-driven choice (which video to click, which channel to subscribe to, which gym ad gets remembered): salience and distinctive assets dominate; nobody reads a positioning statement before tapping a thumbnail.
- **Both at once for a creator business:** the *content brand* competes on distinctiveness (recognizable thumbnails, intro, catchphrase, consistent visual system — never redesign for novelty's sake), while the *monetization products* (sponsorship offering, training programs, memberships) compete on Dunford-style positioning.
- Sequence rule of thumb: **differentiation earns the first sale; distinctiveness earns the ten-thousandth impression.** A small brand needs a sharp position first (it can't buy salience), then locks distinctive assets and spends years refusing to change them.

## 6. Brand architecture: personal brand + company brand + facility

### 6.1 The three classical architectures (Aaker's spectrum)

- **Branded house** — one master brand over everything (Virgin, FedEx). Cheapest to build, every activity feeds one memory structure; but one scandal/pivot hits everything, and sub-businesses can't be sold cleanly.
- **House of brands** — independent brands per business (P&G). Maximum separability/sellability and per-audience precision; most expensive — each brand needs its own awareness budget.
- **Endorsed / hybrid** — separate brands visibly endorsed by a parent or a person ("Neptune Performance, founded by [athlete]"). Trust transfers, assets stay separable. This is usually right for creator-led multi-business setups.

### 6.2 Personal vs. business vs. hybrid (decision framework)

| | Personal brand | Business brand | Hybrid ("personality-driven business brand") |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trust/attention | Fastest — people prefer people; faces get noticed; parasocial loyalty survives pivots | Slowest — faceless entities struggle for attention | Fast — founder is the visible champion |
| Sellability / exit | Effectively unsellable; value inseparable from the person | Clean exits; value in separable assets | Sellable if founder is architected as "on-air talent," not the product |
| Naming freedom | Stuck with your legal name | Full freedom, optimizable | Full freedom for the entity + human differentiation |
| Delegation/scale | Hard — everything routes through the person | Easy — hire leadership | Medium — design non-founder faces early |
| Identity cost | Locked to the niche; life/work entangled | Pivots hard (audience attached to offering, not person) | Best of both, IF roles are explicit |

Key stat: ~**45% of a company's reputation is tied to its CEO** — the founder's face is a real asset; the design problem is capturing it without creating fatal key-person risk. Examples of the hybrid done well: Noah Kagan/AppSumo, Ryan Serhant (brokerage), Ryan Trahan (Joyride candy — YouTube fame funneled into a separable product brand).

### 6.3 The athlete + media co + facility architecture (the specific case)

Recommended structure — an **endorsed hybrid, three-layer system**:

1. **Athlete/founder personal brand** = top of funnel. Owns: attention, credibility, story, relationships, podcast/guest appearances. Never the legal wrapper for anything valuable. Its positioning is a *narrative* ("player who X, now building Y"), not a product position.
2. **Media company** = the audience asset. Own name, own DBAs (thumbnail system, intro, colors, catchphrase). Positioned to *sponsors and partners* with full Dunford treatment (alternatives = other channels/podcasts/MCN packages; differentiators = athlete credibility, audience composition, engagement; value = qualified reach + halo). Positioned to *viewers* via distinctiveness + CEPs.
3. **Facility/training business** = the local, physical asset. Own name, endorsed by the founder ("founded by…"), positioned as a local service business (§7). Must be built to survive the founder's absence: named methodology, non-founder coach faces, systematized curriculum.

Cross-linking rules:
- **Endorsement flows down, never sideways-mandatory:** founder endorses media co and facility; media co features the facility as *content* (build-out series, athlete transformations) — the single cheapest customer-acquisition channel a facility can have. But each entity must be able to stand alone.
- **One brand promise per entity.** Don't force a shared tagline across a YouTube channel and a training facility; force shared *values* and visual family resemblance instead (endorsed architecture, not branded house).
- **Transition/insurance mechanics** (from the personal→business playbook): migrate IP into the entities, reissue products under company brands, 301-redirect personal-domain assets, and progressively reposition the founder as on-air talent/endorser so any entity can be sold, funded, or run without him.
- **Failure mode to warn about:** the "everything is me" trap — founder's name on the facility, founder in every video, founder closing every sale. It maximizes year-1 revenue and minimizes terminal value.

## 7. Positioning a local training facility (playbook)

Local service positioning is Dunford + geography. The data says specificity beats proximity more often than owners believe:

- **62%** of gym members cite proximity as the top choice factor — *but* **41%** would travel farther for a specialization they can't get nearby (IHRSA 2023).
- Niche-positioned studios: ~**23% lower churn** than generalists (Mindbody 2024).
- Consumers hold ~**1.8 fitness memberships** simultaneously — a niche facility is often an *additional* membership, so "they already have a gym" is a weak objection. **84%** of US consumers prioritize wellness but are fragmented across gyms/apps/boutiques (McKinsey).

**Three viable positions:** niche (modality/demographic/outcome — a baseball performance lab is inherently this), luxury (justify price via environment + small caps ≤12 + service, typically 4,000-8,000 sq ft), accessible (own affordability confidently — hours, no contracts, simplicity). Pick one; the middle is death.

**Process:**
1. Map every local competitor on a 2×2: price × specialization.
2. Find white space with demand signals (search volume, travel-ball rosters, waitlists elsewhere).
3. Choose direction **before** spending on marketing — operators who position first consistently outperform those who build beautiful facilities then wonder why traffic is soft.
4. Test with trials; target **≥35% trial→member conversion** as the positioning-fit signal.
5. Refine from how members *describe you* and referral patterns.

**Expression tactics with proven lift:**
- **Name signals position instantly** — "Peak Performance Functional Training" vs "Studio Bloom" make different claims; generic names ("FitLife") underperform.
- **Named signature methodology** ("the 60-Day Reset," a proprietary velocity protocol) — competitors can copy exercises, not your named system. This is mini-category-design (§4.2).
- **Signature coaches** as personalities — positioning authenticity competitors can't clone (and a facility's version of DBAs).
- **Educational content as authority** — workshops, clinics, technique labs reduce reliance on discounting. Frequent discounting = positioning weakness, treat it as a diagnostic alarm.

## 8. Naming

### 8.1 Taxonomy (approach × construct)

Two dimensions — *approach* (descriptive → suggestive → abstract) and *construct* (real word, compound, coined) — give the working grid:

| | Descriptive | Suggestive | Abstract |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real word** | Pizza Hut, The Container Store | Twitter, Dove | Apple, Virgin |
| **Compound** | GameStop | Zipcar | Everlane |
| **Coined** | Acuvue | Febreze | Dasani, Zapier |

Plus: abbreviations (IBM), foreign-language (Prego), founder/geographic names.

Trade-offs:
- **Descriptive:** instant clarity (good for B2B/local where buyers want to know what you sell); nearly impossible to trademark strongly, easily boxed in when you expand.
- **Suggestive:** evokes the right feeling with room to grow — the **most popular type for new brands** and usually the right default.
- **Abstract/coined:** maximum ownability and trademark strength; you pay in marketing dollars to fill the empty vessel with meaning. Wrong choice for a marketing-budget-poor local business.

### 8.2 Process (7 steps, 4-12 weeks for a thorough project)

1. Discovery & strategy → 2. **naming brief** (audience, naming territories, constructs allowed, tonality, competitor-name audit, domain requirements, trademark parameters) → 3. research (pros spend 4+ hrs before ideating) → 4. generation (pros produce **100+ candidates per round**; techniques: metaphor mining, etymology, compounds, prefix/suffix play, idioms) → 5. **trademark prescreen** → 6. evaluation → 7. presentation & alignment.

**Evaluation — "Name Radar," three axes:** Strategic (fits positioning), Creative (distinctive, memorable, pronounceable), Technical (trademark clear, domain viable, linguistically safe). A name must clear all three; a beloved name that fails Technical is a lawsuit or a rebrand.

**Trademark prescreen (do before attachment forms):** USPTO search for identical marks all classes → similar marks in relevant classes → international registers → live commercial-use search → domain-use check. Cheap prescreen specialists exist; a formal knockout search from an IP attorney before committing.

**Presentation discipline:** show finalists in neutral type, grayscale; explain rationale; ask for positives before rejections (kills the "loudest person hates it" dynamic).

**Common naming mistakes:** ideating before a brief exists; personal taste over strategy; over-weighting exact-match .com (modifiers and alt-TLDs are fine); falling in love pre-legal-clearance; sloppy presentation creating consensus chaos.

**Naming within architecture (§6):** the media co and facility should NOT share a name family so tightly that one's failure brands the other, but a subtle family resemblance (shared tonality, shared visual identity elements) supports the endorsed model.

### 8.3 Domain/handle reality (2026)

Exact-match real-word .coms are mostly gone; evaluate name + modifier ("…performance," "…training," city name) and social-handle availability *as a set* on day one. A name you can't get consistent handles for fractures your DBAs.

## 9. Brand promise

**Definition:** the single commitment at the intersection of *what you do best* and *what customers value most* — the standard every interaction is judged against. It's internal-operational as much as external: if the front desk can't keep it, it isn't a promise, it's copy.

Reference examples: Apple — technology for people who see the world differently (about what tech *enables*); Disney — magical, joyful experience everywhere the brand shows up; Tesla — not "cars" but a mission (sustainable energy) fronted by a founder personality (a hybrid-architecture example, with its key-person risk on full display).

**How to write one (from the positioning canvas, never before it):** take the #1 value theme (§2, step 6) + the target who cares most (step 7) → one sentence, testable, kept by operations. Facility example shape: "Every athlete leaves every session knowing exactly what improved and what's next." Media example shape: "You'll understand the game the way players actually experience it." Test: can a staff member break the promise? If not, it's too vague to be one.

**Consistency payoff:** brands with consistent omnichannel expression see ~23% higher customer retention (Gartner, cited 2025) — the promise is what makes cross-channel consistency possible.

## 10. Playbooks & checklists

### 10.1 Half-day positioning sprint (small team version)

1. Assemble founder + whoever touches sales + whoever touches product/service delivery (2-6 people). 90 min max per block.
2. Block A — alternatives: "If we vanished tomorrow, what would our 10 best customers do?" Group into status quo / DIY / general / dedicated.
3. Block B — capabilities the alternatives lack (facts only, provable).
4. Block C — value: for each capability, "so what?" twice. Cluster into ≤3 themes.
5. Block D — who cares a lot: list the characteristics of customers for whom the themes are urgent. Tighten until it's uncomfortable.
6. Block E — pick frame + style (default: big fish, small pond). Draft: "For [who], [name] is the [frame] that [top value theme], unlike [primary alternative]."
7. Ship the canvas to everyone; revisit every 6-12 months or on any major product/competitor shift.

### 10.2 Distinctive-asset audit (media brand)

- Inventory candidate assets: logo, color, thumbnail style, intro sound/phrase, host catchphrase, recurring segment names, mascot.
- For each, score fame ("would a subscriber know it's us without the name?") and uniqueness ("does anyone else in the niche use it?").
- Kill/park weak assets; pick 2-3 to run everywhere, unchanged, for years.
- Write a "do not touch" list; the urge to refresh is the enemy.
- Map 5-15 CEPs and check content calendar coverage against them quarterly.

### 10.3 Architecture decision checklist (multi-entity founder)

- [ ] What is each entity's exit/sellability requirement? (Sellable → own brand.)
- [ ] Where does the founder's face create value vs. key-person risk?
- [ ] Is the endorsement explicit ("founded by…") and consistent?
- [ ] Does each entity have its own positioning canvas, promise, and name?
- [ ] Are non-founder faces being developed in every entity?
- [ ] Is IP held in the entities (not the person)?
- [ ] Does content cross-promotion flow founder → entities without making entities dependent?

## 11. Common mistakes

1. **Positioning by default** — inheriting the market frame you launched with and never re-deciding it (Dunford's "positioning baggage").
2. **Omitting the status quo** from the competitor list — calibrates everything against the wrong baseline; ~50% of losses are to "do nothing."
3. **Backwards process** — starting with a tagline/category and rationalizing; the 5 components are sequential for a reason.
4. **Over-abstraction of value** ("saves time and money") and **theme sprawl** (10+ value points = zero).
5. **Confusing value with objection handling** — a reason not to buy is not a negative value prop; treat separately.
6. **Trying to serve everyone** (local business version) — excludes no one, positions nothing; frequent discounting is the telltale symptom.
7. **Category creation vanity** — small company burns cash educating a market instead of subsegmenting an existing one; or abandons a working category name for an abstract bigger one (DocuSign/Agreement Cloud).
8. **Confusing differentiation with distinctiveness** — a sharp value prop nobody can recognize in-feed, or gorgeous consistent assets wrapping an undifferentiated offer. You need the right one for the right buying context (§5.4).
9. **Refreshing distinctive assets** for internal boredom — resets years of memory-building; consistency is the asset.
10. **Pure personal brand for a sellable business** — unsellable, undelegable, identity-locked. Conversely, faceless company brand for a creator business — throws away the founder's attention advantage.
11. **Naming before briefing / before trademark prescreen** — taste-driven names, legal surprises, domain-tail-wagging-dog.
12. **One promise stretched across unrelated entities** — a media brand and a facility need family resemblance, not a shared tagline.
13. **Loss obsession** in diagnosis — wins tell you what actually resonates; losses over-index on edge cases and whales.
14. **Positioning the roadmap** — sell what's on the truck.

## 12. Questions Carl should ask

**Positioning diagnosis**
- "If your business disappeared tomorrow, what would your ten best customers actually do?" (surfaces true alternatives incl. status quo)
- "Walk me through your last five *wins* — why did they pick you, in their words?" (counters loss obsession)
- "What can you prove you do that none of those alternatives can?" (attributes, facts only)
- "So what? …and so what?" (drives features to value)
- "Who cares about that so much it's urgent? Describe them without demographics."
- "What mental shelf does a stranger put you on in the first five seconds? Is that the shelf where you win?"
- "When did you last deliberately re-decide your positioning, versus inherit it?"

**Distinctiveness / media brand**
- "Cover the channel name — would a viewer know this thumbnail/intro/phrase is you?"
- "What have you changed about your visual identity in the last two years, and why?" (refresh-itis check)
- "List the situations in which someone would think of your content. How many do you actually own?" (CEPs)

**Architecture**
- "Which of these businesses do you ever want to sell, raise on, or step back from? " (drives brand separation)
- "If you were unavailable for six months, which entity survives? What breaks first?"
- "Where is the IP legally held — you, or the entities?"
- "Who is the second face of each business?"

**Local facility**
- "Plot your local competitors: price vs. specialization. Where's the empty quadrant with demand?"
- "What's your trial-to-member conversion? (Under ~35% = positioning-fit problem, not a sales problem.)"
- "How do members describe you to friends, verbatim?"
- "How often do you discount to close? " (positioning-weakness alarm)
- "What's your named methodology? Could a competitor copy your programming tomorrow?"

**Naming / promise**
- "Does the name signal the position in one read, and did it clear a USPTO prescreen?"
- "Can your front-line staff break your brand promise? If they can't, it's not a promise."
