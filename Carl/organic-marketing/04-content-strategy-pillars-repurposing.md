# Content Strategy: Pillars, Formats & Repurposing Systems

## TL;DR

- **Define 3–5 content pillars first** (topics the brand wants to be *known for*, derived from mission + audience data — not vibes). Everything else — formats, series, calendar — hangs off them. More than ~6 pillars means you haven't decided what you are.
- **Run the Hero/Hub/Help (3H) portfolio**: roughly 10% hero (big swings), 30% hub (episodic series that retain), 60% help/hygiene (evergreen search-answering content). Most small teams over-invest in hero and starve help.
- **Adopt a one-to-many pipeline, not one-to-one production.** One 45–60 min pillar recording (podcast, keynote, long video) should reliably yield 15–30+ derivative assets: clips, threads, newsletter sections, quote graphics, blog posts. This is the GaryVee reverse-pyramid model and it's the only way a small team competes on volume.
- **Distribution > creation.** Ross Simmonds' "Create Once, Distribute Forever" thesis: budget a dedicated line item for distribution; repurpose, remix, reshare existing winners before making anything new. 67% of marketers report reusing successful posts in new formats beats publishing net-new content on the same topics (CMI).
- **Formats/series are the compounding unit on video platforms.** A repeatable, nameable format gives viewers a reason to binge and gives you a refinement loop every episode (MrBeast's core lesson). Invent formats, not videos.
- **Target ~70–80% evergreen / 20–30% timely** in the content mix; updating old winners often beats new content (updating high-performers boosts traffic ~146%; bloggers who update old posts report ~2.5x stronger results).
- **Never copy-paste across platforms.** Repurposing means re-contextualizing per platform (native aspect ratio, native hook, zero-click value on-platform). Identical cross-posting is the #1 way repurposing programs die.

---

## 1. Why architecture beats output

Content programs fail from lack of *structure*, not lack of effort: content created without a defined audience, goal, or distribution plan; promotion underfunded; channels chosen by habit. Meanwhile the economics of structured programs are strong — content marketing generates ~3x more leads than outbound at ~62% lower cost per lead (Demand Metric benchmark, replicated by HubSpot/CMI through 2025–26), and average programs return ~$7.65 per $1 spent, with email (the natural terminus of a repurposing chain) returning $36+ per $1.

The architecture stack, top to bottom:

1. **Pillars** — the 3–5 topics you want to own. Strategic, mostly invisible to the audience.
2. **Portfolio model (3H)** — how effort is split across hero / hub / help tiers.
3. **Formats & series** — the repeatable containers (shows, segments, newsletter sections).
4. **Repurposing pipeline** — the one-to-many machine that multiplies each pillar asset.
5. **Calendar & cadence** — when things ship, evergreen/timely balance.
6. **Measurement loop** — what feeds back into pillar and format decisions.

Everything below is reference for each layer.

## 2. Content pillars & buckets

### Definitions (people conflate these constantly)

- **Content pillars** = the finite set of topics/ideas the brand wants to be known for. Deborah Carver (The Content Technologist): pillars incorporate mission, vision, values, business model, and audience data into a topical structure that guides content decisions *across all channels*. They are brand-level (not per-channel), iterative (reviewed every few years), measurable monthly/quarterly, and largely **invisible to the audience** — they're an internal decision-making tool, not navigation or campaign names.
- **Content buckets** = the recurring day-to-day categories a social calendar rotates through (educational / behind-the-scenes / promotional / community, etc.). Buckets are tactical instances of pillars.
- **Pillar page** (SEO usage) = a long hub article that links to cluster content. Different thing entirely; don't let the client confuse the two.

### How many

- Consensus across sources: **3–5 for most brands** (Mailchimp et al.); Content Technologist allows 3–9 with **4–6 ideal** regardless of org size. Below 3 you're repetitive; above 6 you're unfocused and can't measure ownership of any topic.

### Derivation process (workable in one workshop + one data pass)

1. **Brand inputs**: mission, what the business actually sells, what it credibly knows better than competitors.
2. **Audience inputs**: top questions from comments/DMs/support, search demand (keyword tools), what existing top-performers have in common. Use qualitative + quantitative — pillars picked purely by gut drift toward founder hobbies.
3. **Intersection test** per candidate pillar: (a) audience demonstrably cares, (b) we have unfair authority/access, (c) it ladders to revenue (sponsors, product, facility bookings). Kill anything failing two of three.
4. **Name them internally, color-code them, tag every piece of content** with its pillar so performance can be read *by pillar* quarterly. (Content Technologist runs six color-coded pillars this way.)
5. **Review cadence**: revisit pillars every 12–24 months or on business-model change (e.g., adding a second business line = candidate for a new pillar, not a new brand-wide identity crisis).

### Idea-generation frameworks that sit under pillars

- **The 4A Framework** (Dickie Bush / Ship 30 for 30): any pillar topic can be expressed four ways — **Actionable** (here's how: tips, guides, resources), **Analytical** (here are the numbers: breakdowns, frameworks, teardowns), **Aspirational** (yes you can: stories, lessons, mistakes), **Anthropological** (here's why: human nature, fears, paradoxes, observations). 4 angles × 5 pillars = a permanent 20-cell idea matrix. Bush's claim: this is how you say "a handful of core ideas in 1,000 unique ways."
- **Justin Welsh's Content Matrix**: same shape — pillars on one axis, content styles on the other; he credits it for consistently producing 6–12 quality pieces weekly as a solo operator.
- Practical rule: when a client says "we're out of ideas," they almost never are — they lack a matrix. Rebuild pillars × angles before brainstorming new topics.

## 3. The Hero / Hub / Help model (3H)

Origin: developed by Google/YouTube for brand video strategy; now applied across blogs, podcasts, social, email. Also called Hero/Hub/Hygiene.

### The three tiers

| Tier | What it is | Cadence | Share of output | Resourcing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hero** | High-production, emotionally resonant big swings for mass reach: launches, flagship films, tentpole events | 2–4 per year | ~10% | Highest budget; strategist + creative director + production |
| **Hub** | Regular episodic/themed content that nurtures and retains the existing audience: shows, podcast, newsletter series | Weekly–monthly, predictable | ~30% | Content manager + writer/editor + designer |
| **Help/Hygiene** | Evergreen, search-driven content answering everyday questions: how-tos, FAQs, tutorials | Weekly/biweekly, driven by keyword demand | ~60% | SEO writer + social manager; templatable/outsourceable |

(Ratios and resourcing per Equinet Academy's implementation guide.)

### Metrics per tier — measure each tier by its own job

- **Hero**: reach, impressions, earned media, brand sentiment, share of voice.
- **Hub**: engagement rate, subscriber growth, CTR, repeat visits, return-viewer %.
- **Help**: organic rankings, search traffic, time-on-page, helpfulness signals.
- **Cross-layer question (the important one)**: does Help traffic convert to Hub subscribers, and does Hub engagement prime Hero moments? If the tiers don't feed each other, you have three separate programs, not a model.

### Implementation sequence (Equinet's 8 steps, compressed)

1. Define pillars + personas → 2. Audit existing content, tag every piece H/H/H → 3. Benchmark competitors' coverage and SERP gaps → 4. Map topics across the three tiers (keyword research drives Help; engagement patterns drive Hub series; cultural moments/milestones drive Hero) → 5. Color-coded editorial calendar → 6. Assign roles per tier → 7. Distribute & cross-pollinate (break Hero into Hub posts; expand Hub into downloadables) → 8. KPIs per tier, refine quarterly.

### The 2020s critique — 3H+2 ("the tree model")

Jon Mowat (Hurricane Media) argues classic 3H is outdated: it was YouTube-native, ignores bottom-of-funnel selling, and predates TikTok-style trend content. His additions:

- **Volume content**: low-cost, snackable, on-trend output that "feeds the algorithm" — organic TikTok/Shorts/Reels trend participation that adds personality. High frequency, near-zero polish.
- **GO! content**: explicitly conversion-focused material — offers, codes, direct CTAs — at the base of the model.

The shape becomes a tree, not a pyramid: massive volume at the bottom, rare hero moments at the top. **Carl's usable synthesis**: for a creator-led business, plan five lanes — Hero (2–4/yr), Hub (the weekly show/newsletter), Help (evergreen search/answer content), Volume (daily-ish short-form, much of it repurposed), GO! (deliberate promotional slots, ~1 in 5–10 posts so the ask doesn't get lost or become the whole feed).

## 4. Format–market fit

The format is not a container choice made after the idea — it *is* a strategic choice. Format–market fit = the match between (a) the idea, (b) the format's native strengths, (c) the platform's distribution mechanics, and (d) where the audience is in its relationship with you.

### Discovery vs. retention formats (the core split)

- **Short-form = discovery engine**: algorithmic distribution to cold audiences (TikTok, Reels, Shorts). Success metrics: reach, 50%+ completion rate, profile CTR, new-follower rate.
- **Long-form = retention vehicle**: deepens relationships with people who already know you (YouTube long-form, podcast, newsletter). Success metrics: average view duration, subscriber conversion, return-viewer %, brand lift.
- YouTube uniquely serves both (Shorts for discovery, long-form for retention) — which is why a YouTube-first business should treat Shorts as a funnel into the main channel, not a separate content line.
- Budget note from the creator-partnership world: allocate ~10–15% of effort to testing formats *outside* your native lane — some long-form-native creators dramatically outperform in clips and vice versa; you only learn by testing.

### Format selection heuristics

- **Match format to information density**: step-by-step processes → written/how-to video (Help); personality and opinion → podcast/talking-head (Hub); spectacle and emotion → produced video (Hero); single insights → zero-click social posts (Volume).
- **Zero-click content** (Amanda Natividad, SparkToro): design social posts to deliver full value *on-platform* with no click required. Platforms suppress outbound links; native value earns "algorithmic capital" you occasionally spend on a link post. Natividad's own result: 1,000 → 10,000 followers in ~18 months on native-value posting, feeding a 15k newsletter. Practical ratio: mostly zero-click value posts, occasional link "spends."
- **Don't launch a new platform; launch a repurposing lane.** A new platform should only ever be adopted as a *destination for existing pillar content in a new format*, never as a net-new production burden.

## 5. Series design (formats as compounding assets)

On video platforms, the unit of brand-building is the **repeatable format**, not the individual video.

### Why series win

- **Binge behavior**: a viewer who connects with one episode of a format has a reason to watch ten more — each format video merchandises the whole back catalog (Creator Handbook on MrBeast's "$1 vs $X" series).
- **Refinement loop**: every episode of the same format is an A/B test on the same template — hooks, pacing, thumbnails get measurably better. "Finding a format is the path to consistency," and consistency over time is what builds *brand* (Colin & Samir; the longest-running YouTube successes — Dude Perfect, Rhett & Link — are format machines).
- **Production efficiency**: known structure = reusable rundowns, graphics packages, edit templates, and predictable crew time. Series are the industrialization of creativity.

### Series design checklist

1. **Nameable premise in one line** ("$1 vs $1,000,000 hotel", "We test X so you don't have to"). If it can't be titled as a template, it's not a series.
2. **A repeatable engine**: fixed structure with a variable slot (the guest, the price gap, the challenge). The fixed part builds familiarity; the variable slot supplies novelty.
3. **Escalation or variation path**: plan episodes 5–10 before shipping episode 1. If you can't imagine ep. 8, kill it.
4. **Test comprehension before launch** — MrBeast pre-tests whether audiences understand complex game concepts before publishing. A confusing premise dies in the first 30 seconds.
5. **Innovate inside the format** to avoid staleness (Creator Handbook lesson 7): keep the skeleton, refresh the flesh. Retire formats when episode-over-episode retention decays for 3+ consecutive episodes despite iteration.
6. **Give every series a home in a pillar and a tier** (usually Hub). A series that maps to no pillar is a hobby.

### Series economics rule of thumb

A format is validated when episodes 3–5 outperform episode 1 (returning-viewer lift). Most teams cancel at episode 2 — too early; format familiarity typically needs 4–6 episodes to compound. Conversely, don't greenlight more than 1–2 new formats per quarter on a small team; each unvalidated format is a full production line.

## 6. One-to-many repurposing systems

### The GaryVee reverse-pyramid model (the canonical version)

Structure: one piece of **pillar content** — "something on a meaty topic you can make 30–60 minutes of content about": an interview, keynote, Q&A show, or episodic doc — is atomized into **30–64+ pieces of micro-content**, each *contextualized to its destination platform*. The famous deck is literally titled "How to Create 64 Pieces of Content in a Day."

The six-step loop (per chopcast's breakdown):

1. **Create pillar content** on a resonant pillar topic.
2. **Generate micro-content** — a dozen+ pieces minimum: clips, quotes, memes, articles, mashups — chosen around the *moments* most likely to resonate, not evenly sliced.
3. **Distribute strategically**, micro-content pointing back to the pillar piece.
4. **Listen**: community feedback, timestamps, direct questions about what landed.
5. **Second-round micro-content** testing hypotheses from performance data (re-cut the same pillar asset around what worked).
6. **Redistribute** refined content.

Key insight for small teams: the model is a *feedback loop*, not a volume stunt — every derivative piece teaches you which platforms/formats/angles work before you scale effort. And micro-content must be tailored per platform "as much as for the audience": native aspect ratio, native caption style, native hook conventions.

### Ross Simmonds — Create Once, Distribute Forever

The thesis: **how and where you distribute work matters more than the work itself.** Stop defaulting to net-new creation; invest in high-value assets and run them through **Repurpose / Remix / Reshare**:

- **Repurpose**: change the format (webinar → blog post → guide → newsletter series).
- **Remix**: change the angle/framing of the same idea for a different audience or moment.
- **Reshare**: redistribute the same asset again — most audiences never saw it the first time; a piece's first publication is the *start* of its distribution life, not the whole of it.

Operational implication Carl should push: **a dedicated distribution line item** — explicit hours/budget for promotion and re-promotion, separate from creation. Most programs spend ~90%+ on creation and treat distribution as an afterthought; inverting even to 60/40 is a step-change.

### The podcast → everything playbook (concrete asset map)

From one 45–60 min recorded episode (Foundation's "Content Relay" + Content Allies + ContentBuck workflows):

**Workflow**: (1) plan repurposing at episode *inception* — book guests/topics knowing which derivatives you want; (2) record with distribution in mind (video + audio, clean framing for vertical crops); (3) AI transcript with speaker ID (Descript/Castmagic-class tools); (4) timestamp standout moments — strong claims, numbers, stories — as the production queue; (5) build the asset set; (6) space releases over 2–4 weeks; (7) track and re-cut winners.

**Standard asset yield per episode** (~30+ pieces):

| Asset class | Typical output |
|---|---|
| Full episode | 1 YouTube upload + audio feeds |
| Vertical clips | 4–7 curated (auto-tools can generate 25+/hour; curation is the filter) — Shorts/Reels/TikTok; square variants for LinkedIn |
| Written | 1 SEO blog post from transcript + 2–3 derivative angle posts |
| Newsletter | 1 recap/insert section (summary + best quote + clip embed) |
| Social text | 3–5 quote posts / takeaway threads / discussion prompts |
| Graphics | 2–4 quote cards / carousel |
| Gated/long-form (quarterly, from multiple episodes) | ebook, webinar, report compiled from episode themes |

**Evidence this works**: Tonkean's Modern Business Operations podcast grew unique listeners 174% in one quarter via systematic atomization; 6sense turned a 17-minute episode into an SEO page, embedded video, LinkedIn clips and takeaway posts; Adobe survey — 78% of companies with podcasts report meeting/exceeding ROI expectations. Also note **39% of podcast consumption now happens on YouTube** (vs 21% Spotify) — video capture is non-negotiable.

### Repurposing benchmarks worth quoting to clients

- 67% of marketers: reusing successful posts in new formats outperforms net-new content on the same topics (CMI).
- Repurposing improves content ROI ~32% on average; systematic programs report 300%+ reach gains; a Buffer case study hit 400% reach expansion into new platforms.
- Adoption reality check: 48% of social marketers repurpose with minor adaptation, 34% create per-platform from scratch, **17% cross-post identically** (HubSpot) — that last group is the cautionary tale.
- 46% of marketers name repurposing their single best-performing strategy (ReferralRock); 48% of B2B marketers cite *insufficient repurposing* as a scaling bottleneck (CMI/MarketingProfs).
- AI-assisted repurposing: ~60–65% time savings per adapted asset; ~40% production-cost reduction at 3x output in tool-vendor studies — treat vendor numbers skeptically but the direction is real.

## 7. Content flywheels

A flywheel is a repurposing pipeline whose outputs *feed back into inputs*, so the system accelerates instead of just fanning out:

**Design pattern**: pillar asset → derivatives → audience signals (comments, questions, clip performance) → next pillar asset topics + second-round cuts → growing library → each new piece merchandises the back catalog (binge/series effect) → bigger audience → better guests/access → better pillar assets.

Concrete loops to build:

1. **Signal loop**: clip performance dictates which segments get re-cut and which topics get full episodes (GaryVee steps 4–6 institutionalized).
2. **Search loop**: Help content captures search intent → CTAs into Hub (newsletter/show) → Hub retention primes Hero launches (Equinet's cross-layer metric).
3. **Library loop**: quarterly "greatest hits" compilations, updated evergreen posts, and resharing of past winners (Simmonds: a piece is never "done" being distributed).
4. **Community loop**: audience questions become Help content; audience stories become Hub segments — the audience co-authors the calendar.

The anti-flywheel smell: a calendar that requires the same effort every week forever with no accumulating advantage. If deleting last quarter's content wouldn't change this quarter's results, there's no flywheel.

## 8. Evergreen vs. timely mix

- **Consensus target: 70–80% evergreen / 20–30% timely.** Common operationalization: of 4 monthly posts, 3 evergreen + 1 timely/seasonal. Fast-moving niches (tech, news-adjacent, sports seasons) shift toward 60/40 or 70/30.
- **Why evergreen dominates**: it compounds (search + back-catalog binge), it's the raw material for endless resharing, and it aligns with how people research decisions. Timely content buys relevance spikes and algorithm favor but decays to ~zero.
- **Updating beats creating**: refreshing high-performing old posts boosts traffic ~146%; bloggers who systematically update old content report ~2.5x better results (Orbit Media). Institutionalize a quarterly "refresh sprint" — top 10 evergreen assets by lifetime traffic get updated, re-promoted, re-clipped.
- **Timely content rule**: only ride trends that intersect a pillar (trend × pillar = on-brand relevance; trend alone = noise). Mowat's "Volume" tier is where trend content lives — cheap, fast, disposable by design.
- Seasonal/timely content that recurs annually (playoffs, holiday gift guides, "season prep") is really **perennial** — build it once, refresh yearly. Cheapest "timely" content there is.

## 9. Operating the system on a small team

Minimum viable content operation for a 2–6 person team:

1. **One pillar recording session per week** (podcast/show/keynote-style). This is the only "blank page" creation in the system.
2. **One atomization day**: transcript → timestamps → clip queue → written derivatives. Batch it; context-switching kills throughput.
3. **A standing calendar skeleton**: fixed weekly slots per platform (e.g., 1 long video, 4–5 shorts, 1 newsletter, 3–5 text posts), each slot pre-mapped to a tier (Hub/Help/Volume/GO!) and filled from the atomization queue — the calendar is a *filling problem*, not an ideation problem.
4. **Tag everything** (pillar, tier, format, source asset) so quarterly reviews can answer: which pillar is growing? which format converts viewers to subscribers? which source episodes over-yield?
5. **Roles by tier, not by platform** (Equinet's staffing model scales down): one person owns Hub production, one owns Help/SEO + repurposing, founder owns Hero + being the face. Outsource/automate Help and clip-cutting first — they're the most templatable.
6. **Quarterly review agenda**: pillar performance, format retention trends, refresh-sprint targets, one format to kill, at most one format to pilot.

## 10. Common mistakes

1. **Mechanical cross-posting** — identical content on every channel. Audiences detect copy-paste instantly; engagement craters. Repurposing = re-contextualizing (native hook, format, caption conventions per platform).
2. **Output as a proxy for performance** — AI makes production cheap, so teams produce more undifferentiated content; volume without differentiation earns nothing. The bar is *would anyone miss this if it didn't exist*.
3. **AI drift in derivatives** — summaries that add claims the source never made, or strip a caveat that mattered. Every derivative needs a human pass against the source; the subtle errors are the dangerous ones.
4. **All hero, no help** — founder-led brands love big swings and neglect the 60% evergreen base that actually compounds. Inverted portfolios burn budget on reach with nothing to retain it.
5. **Too many pillars / no pillars** — 8+ "pillars" is a topic list, not a strategy; zero pillars means the calendar is founder-mood-driven. Either way, un-measurable.
6. **Canceling formats at episode 2** — before format familiarity can compound (needs ~4–6 episodes). Equally: never killing a decayed format out of sentiment.
7. **No distribution budget** — publishing is treated as the finish line. Simmonds: distribution deserves its own line item; most content dies from under-promotion, not low quality.
8. **Optimizing for algorithms over audiences** — chasing trend mechanics while ignoring what the audience emotionally needs (reassurance, validation, clarity); vanity metrics over business impact.
9. **New platform = new production line** — adopting a channel without a repurposing lane to feed it. Every channel added should be net-near-zero marginal creation cost.
10. **No measurement by pillar/tier** — only 36% of marketers can measure content ROI despite 83% calling it a priority; tagging discipline is the cheap fix.
11. **Trend-jacking off-pillar** — timely content that doesn't intersect a pillar buys impressions from people who will never care about the business.
12. **Treating repurposing as an intern task** — moment selection (which 60 seconds of a 60-minute episode) is the highest-leverage editorial judgment in the whole system.

## 11. Questions Carl should ask

Diagnostics for a client engagement:

**Pillars**
1. "Complete this: we want to be known for ___, ___, and ___. Can everyone on the team give the same answer?"
2. "Show me last month's content tagged by pillar. Which pillar got zero pieces? Which got half of everything?"
3. "Which pillar most directly feeds revenue (sponsors/product/bookings), and is it getting proportional effort?"

**Portfolio**
4. "Bucket your last 90 days into Hero/Hub/Help/Volume/GO!. What's the actual ratio vs. the ~10/30/60 target?"
5. "What's your evergreen vs. timely split? When did you last update (not just reshare) a top evergreen piece?"
6. "Does your Help content have a CTA into your Hub (newsletter/show)? What's the conversion?"

**Formats & series**
7. "Name your formats. Which is on episode 5+? What's returning-viewer trend by episode?"
8. "If I asked your audience to describe your show in one sentence, could they? Could they title the next episode themselves?"
9. "What format are you emotionally attached to that the data says to kill?"

**Repurposing pipeline**
10. "Walk me from one recorded episode to everything it became last month. How many assets? Who decided which moments got clipped, and based on what?"
11. "What percentage of your short-form is derived from pillar content vs. created from scratch?" (High from-scratch % on a small team = the leak.)
12. "When a clip overperforms, what happens next — re-cut, follow-up episode, nothing?"
13. "How many hours/week go to creation vs. distribution/promotion? What's the ratio?"

**Operations & measurement**
14. "Is your calendar an ideation problem or a filling problem each week?"
15. "Can you tell me which *source episodes* over-yielded derivatives? Which pillar grew subscribers fastest last quarter?"
16. "What would break if the founder took two weeks off? " (Tests whether the system exists outside one person's head.)

## Relevance notes (Mayday / Neptune)

- A YouTube-first creator business is the archetypal reverse-pyramid operation: the weekly long-form upload/podcast is pillar content; Shorts/Reels/TikTok are Volume-tier derivatives; the newsletter is the Hub retention layer and the highest-ROI terminus ($36+/$1 email economics); sponsor integrations are GO! content and should be rationed (~1 in 5–10 slots).
- A training facility (Neptune) is a Help-content machine waiting to happen: parent/athlete search questions ("how to add velo safely," "what age for weighted balls") are hygiene content with local-intent gold; a facility show (athlete progress series) is natural Hub; seasonal content (tryout prep, offseason plans) is perennial-timely — build once, refresh yearly. Format–market fit favors before/after and process content in short-form for discovery, long-form for trust with parents.
- Both businesses can share one atomization pipeline (record → transcript → clips → newsletter → posts) with separate pillar sets — the *system* is the shared asset, not the content.
