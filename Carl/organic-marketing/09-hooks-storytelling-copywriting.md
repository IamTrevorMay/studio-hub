# Hooks, Storytelling & Copywriting for Attention

## TL;DR

- **Attention is a funnel with four gates: click → hook → hold → payoff.** Packaging (title + thumbnail) decides whether the video gets a chance at all — a 10/10 video with a 3% CTR loses to a 7/10 video at 8%. Fix packaging before fixing production quality.
- **Every hook that works triggers at least one of four cognitive responses**: curiosity gap, pattern interrupt, self-relevance, or emotional arousal. The best hooks stack two or more in one sentence. The same script with a different opening can produce a ~5x difference in reach.
- **The first 30 seconds is where videos die.** Platform-wide, fewer than 45% of viewers make it past the first minute; a 40%+ drop in the first 30 seconds means the hook failed or the open didn't deliver the title's promise. Target: 70%+ still watching at 0:30.
- **Story beats must connect with "but" or "therefore," never "and then"** (Parker/Stone rule). If beats are only sequenced, not caused, retention decays linearly. Roughly 70% conflict beats ("but") to 30% consequence beats ("therefore").
- **Write to the audience's awareness stage** (Schwartz): unaware people need story and problem-naming; problem-aware need agitation; solution-aware need mechanism; product-aware need proof; most-aware need an offer. Most creator content is written for product-aware viewers who don't exist yet.
- **One idea per piece, one CTA per piece.** Multiple CTAs, buried CTAs, or no CTA are the three most common conversion killers in organic content. The curse of knowledge — assuming the audience knows what you know — is the most common clarity killer.
- **Package first, produce second.** Write the title and sketch the thumbnail before scripting. If you can't package it compellingly, the concept is weak; no amount of production saves it.

## 1. The attention stack: how organic content actually earns viewers

Think of every piece of organic content as a four-gate funnel. A failure at any gate makes everything downstream irrelevant.

| Gate | Instrument | Metric | Failure signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Click | Title + thumbnail (or first frame on feed platforms) | CTR / thumb-stop rate | Impressions without views |
| 2. Hook | First 3s (short-form) / first 30s (long-form) | Retention at 0:03 / 0:30 | "Cliff" retention curve |
| 3. Hold | Structure, pacing, open loops | Average % viewed, mid-curve slope | Steady bleed, mid-video exits |
| 4. Payoff + action | Ending, CTA | End retention, session continuation, conversions | Viewers leave at 80–90%, no next action |

Key operating principles:

- **CTR is the dictator.** A video with perfect retention still fails if nobody enters the funnel. But CTR and retention are coupled: an exaggerated thumbnail that the video can't cash inflates clicks and craters retention. **Packaging must match the product** — the promise made must be the promise kept.
- **Don't compare retention percentages across lengths.** 35% retention on a 30-minute video = 10.5 minutes watched, which beats 60% on a 3-minute video (1.8 minutes) algorithmically. Absolute watch time drives distribution.
- **The order of operations for improvement**: topic selection → packaging → hook → structure → production polish. Most creators invert this and polish first.

### Benchmarks worth memorizing

- Platform-wide average YouTube retention: roughly 23–35% of a video watched (varies by study year; 2025 platform average ~23.7%, broader estimates 35–45%).
- Healthy retention by length (long-form YouTube): **<5 min: 50–70%; 5–15 min: 40–55%; 15–30 min: 30–45%; 30+ min: 25–35%**.
- Viewers decide within roughly **8 seconds** on long-form and **1.5–1.7 seconds** on short-form whether to stay.
- CTR: **4–8% is average** for most niches; above 8% is strong; below 4% signals a packaging problem worth re-titling. A 2-point CTR improvement can roughly double a video's lifetime views.
- Short-form: **60%+ retention at the 3-second mark** is the distribution threshold to aim for. Reels ≤15s average ~72% completion vs ~46% for longer ones; TikToks of 15–30s have the highest completion and share rates.
- Faces in the first 3 seconds of short-form: ~35% higher retention. A storytelling hook or jump cut in the first 3 seconds correlates with materially higher viral probability.

## 2. The psychology under all of it

Everything in this doc reduces to a handful of well-documented mechanisms. Know these and you can generate hooks instead of copying them.

### 2.1 Information-gap theory (Loewenstein)

Curiosity arises **when attention focuses on a specific gap in one's knowledge** — not general ignorance, but awareness of a bounded, specific unknown. The curious brain experiences the gap as mild deprivation and is motivated to close it. Implications:

- **Open the gap before you fill it.** The instinct is to lead with facts; instead, first make the viewer realize there's something they don't know. The Heath brothers call this "Huh? before Aha!"
- **The gap must be specific and bounded.** "There's a lot to learn about hitting" opens no gap. "There's one cue that fixes 80% of youth swing flaws, and it has nothing to do with the hands" opens a precise one.
- **Gaps decay.** An opened loop that goes unaddressed too long turns from curiosity into frustration and exit. Re-tease loops mid-content ("we're getting to the number one thing in a second, but first you need this context").

### 2.2 The four hook triggers

Every effective hook fires at least one of:

1. **Curiosity gap** — unresolved information the brain wants closed.
2. **Pattern interrupt** — a sudden change in visual, audio, or expectation that halts the scroll/skim reflex.
3. **Self-relevance** — the viewer feels personally addressed ("if you're a parent of a 12-year-old pitcher…").
4. **Emotional arousal** — surprise, awe, anger, fear, joy. High-arousal emotions drive attention and sharing; low-arousal ones (contentment, sadness) don't.

The strongest hooks **stack two or more** triggers in one sentence. "I spent $4,000 on private hitting lessons so you don't have to — here are the only 3 things that mattered" = curiosity gap + self-relevance + concrete stake.

### 2.3 Made to Stick — SUCCESs (Heath brothers)

The checklist for whether an idea will survive in someone's head:

- **Simple** — find the core; strip until nothing is left to remove. One idea per piece.
- **Unexpected** — break a pattern; be surprising yet logical in retrospect.
- **Concrete** — sensory, tangible language. "A ball exit velo of 94" beats "improved performance."
- **Credible** — vivid details, statistics made human-scale, demonstrable expertise, or testable claims ("try this tonight").
- **Emotional** — connect to what they already care about; one individual trumps statistics for emotional force.
- **Story** — narrative is mental rehearsal; people simulate stories and remember them.

And the enemy: the **Curse of Knowledge** — once you know something, you cannot imagine not knowing it. This is why expert-made content skips the setup the audience needed, uses jargon, and answers questions nobody asked. Antidote: write for the specific person you were before you learned the thing.

### 2.4 Loss aversion & specificity

- Warning/mistake framings ("Stop doing X") outperform equivalent gain framings because losses loom larger than gains.
- Specific numbers beat round numbers beat no numbers. "$4,127" reads as true; "$4,000" reads as marketing; "a lot of money" reads as nothing. Numbers in titles lift CTR an estimated 20–30%.

## 3. Hook craft

### 3.1 Anatomy of a short-form hook (0–3 seconds)

Three simultaneous layers — the top performers fire all three at once:

1. **Visual interrupt (0–1s):** motion, zoom, unexpected framing, a face, an object mid-action. Never a static logo or slow establishing shot.
2. **Text overlay + audio (1–2s):** the on-screen text states or teases the promise; audio hook lands.
3. **Verbal promise (2–3s):** the spoken line resolves the visual tease into a specific reason to stay.

Spoken hooks should be **10–14 words max**. Strip every non-essential word. First frame matters as much as first sentence — feed platforms show the frame before the audio.

### 3.2 Hook formula library (organized by trigger)

Rotate 5–10 formulas that fit your voice; testing the same script with different openings routinely produces multi-x differences in reach.

**Curiosity-driven**
- *Contrarian claim*: "Everything you've been told about [topic] is wrong."
- *Unfinished story*: "I almost [bad outcome] — until I found this."
- *Secret reveal*: "Here's what [experts/insiders] won't tell you about [topic]."
- *Countdown tease*: "Number one on this list literally changed how we [do X]."
- *Impossible result*: "I [extreme outcome] in [absurd timeframe]."
- *Cliffhanger question*: "What if I told you [unexpected claim]?"

**Authority-driven**
- *Credential lead*: "As a [profession] with [X years/results], here's the thing I see everyone miss."
- *Social proof*: "This exact [system] got [number] of my [clients/players] to [result]."
- *Myth buster*: "Stop [common practice]. Here's what actually works."
- *Behind-the-scenes*: "Let me show you how we actually [process] — no polish."

**Emotion-driven**
- *Empathy hook*: "If you're stuck at [frustrating plateau], this is for you."
- *Relatable frustration*: "Why does nobody talk about [annoying truth]?"
- *Confession*: "I've never admitted this publicly, but…"
- *Aspiration*: "Imagine [specific dream scenario]. Here's the path."

**Direct/tactical**
- *How-to promise*: "Here's exactly how to [specific result], step by step."
- *Mistake warning*: "You're making this mistake with [topic] and it's costing you [stake]."
- *Before/after*: show the split-screen contrast immediately, explain after.
- *Tool reveal*: "This free thing does what I used to pay $[X]/month for."

**Engagement/pattern-break**
- *Hot take*: "Unpopular opinion: [bold claim]."
- *List tease*: "[N] things about [topic] I wish someone told me earlier."
- *Physical pattern break*: unexpected on-screen action (tearing paper, dropping the object, mid-motion start).

The three most consistently high-performing across platforms in recent testing: **contrarian claim, mistake warning, list tease** — because each stacks curiosity + self-relevance + a clear promise in one line.

### 3.3 Long-form YouTube intro structure (0–30s)

The intro's only job is to convert the click into a commitment. Standard high-retention pattern:

1. **Re-state and sharpen the title's promise (0–5s).** The viewer clicked on a promise; confirm they're in the right place immediately. Never open with channel branding, "hey guys welcome back," or throat-clearing — the "Cliff" retention pattern is almost always caused by this.
2. **Raise the stakes / open the gap (5–15s).** Why this matters, what's surprising, what they'll be able to do by the end. Optionally a proof flash (result, credential, receipt).
3. **Preview the path without spoiling the payoff (15–25s).** "First we'll X, then the part nobody expects, then the exact numbers." This is loop-setting.
4. **Hard cut into content (by 30s).** The single most reliable retention fix is deleting the first 20–60 seconds of most creators' scripts.

Rule of thumb: **deliver on the title's promise, or visibly begin delivering, inside the first 30 seconds.** Viewers punish bait faster than they reward production value.

### 3.4 Open loops: the retention engine

- **Primary loop**: the title/hook promise, resolved only near the end (but progress toward it must be visible throughout).
- **Secondary loops**: opened and closed every 60–120 seconds in long-form. Each section should end by opening the next section's question, not by summarizing.
- **Loop discipline**: every loop you open must close. Unclosed loops feel like betrayal and kill rewatch/subscribe intent. Track loops in the script margin like a checklist.
- **Payoff placement**: put the payoff at ~80–90% of runtime, then a short, valuable denouement that bridges into the next video or CTA. Payoff at 50% = second-half exodus.

## 4. Story structure for creators

### 4.1 But/Therefore (Parker & Stone)

The single most useful story rule for content: between any two beats you must be able to say **"but"** (conflict, reversal, complication) or **"therefore"** (causal consequence). If the connective is **"and then,"** the beats are disconnected and momentum dies. Origin: Trey Parker and Matt Stone's NYU lecture — "if 'and then' fits between your beats, you're f***ed."

- Practical mix: roughly **70% "but" beats, 30% "therefore" beats**. Too many "therefores" = predictable; too many "buts" = exhausting whiplash.
- Audit method: write your outline as one-line beats, then literally write the connective between each pair. Rewrite every "and then."
- This applies to a 30-second Reel exactly as it does to a 30-minute documentary. Even a tutorial can be causal: "You'd think you fix this with X — *but* X makes it worse — *therefore* you need Y."

### 4.2 Compressed story spines

**Short-form (15–60s):** Hook (0–3s) → escalation/body (3s to ~80% of runtime) → payoff (last 15–25%) → loop or CTA (last 3–5s). Ending on a beat that flows back into the opening frame boosts loop rate, which Shorts/Reels algorithms reward.

**Long-form narrative:** the working-creator version of three acts —
1. *Stasis + disruption*: what was normal, what broke it (this is usually the hook).
2. *Escalating attempts*: tries and failures, each one a "but." Failures are the content; competence without struggle is boring.
3. *Turn*: the insight/discovery (the "therefore" that reframes everything).
4. *Payoff*: the result, with receipts.
5. *Transfer*: what the viewer takes away — turn "my story" into "your playbook." This is what separates indulgent vlogging from valuable storytelling.

**Educational content disguised as story:** frame the lesson as a case ("How a 14-year-old added 6 mph of exit velo in 8 weeks") rather than a lecture ("Exit velo training principles"). Same information, radically different retention — the story format gives it stakes, chronology, and a protagonist. One individual trumps the masses (Heath).

### 4.3 Reading the retention curve as a story diagnostic

- **The Cliff** (sharp drop 0–30s, then flat): hook failure or intro throat-clearing. Restructure the open; lead with the key insight.
- **Gradual decline**: normal attrition; healthy if midpoint retention >40%. Steeper than expected = pacing problem or insufficient visual/beat variety — usually too many "and thens."
- **The Bump** (spike): a rewatched high-value segment. Extract it as a Short; add chapters; study why it worked.
- **The Flat Line**: the ideal; replicate the topic, structure, and pacing.
- Mid-video exit clusters mark exactly where a loop closed with nothing new opened, or where a tangent broke causality. Fix at the beat level, not with music/editing polish.

## 5. Thumbnail & title psychology (YouTube packaging)

### 5.1 The five thumbnail patterns (from analysis of MrBeast-tier packaging)

1. **Single focal subject.** Exactly one thing the eye lands on; it must be the brightest, most saturated, most in-focus element. A viewer should identify what the video is about in ~0.5 seconds.
2. **One exaggerated emotion.** A face showing one emotion dialed to 11 (shock, triumph, fear, joy). Face ≥40% of thumbnail height; legible from across a room. Faces are processed faster than any text — the face is the emotional anchor, and its gaze direction steers the viewer's eye (looking at the object of interest creates an attention vector).
3. **A visible number or stake.** "$1,000,000," "100 Days," "24 Hours" — time, scale, opposition, or consequence. Present in nearly every top-performing thumbnail.
4. **Mobile-first saturation and contrast.** 70%+ of views are on phones where the home-feed thumbnail renders around 200×113 px. Complementary color pairs (red/cyan, yellow/purple); muted tones vanish at that size. **The Zoom-Out Rule: if it isn't readable at 10% size, delete elements until it is.**
5. **Minimal, specific text — 2–4 words max**, bold sans-serif, stroke/drop-shadow. Text is a hook, not a caption; it must add information the image doesn't already carry (never repeat the title).

Note the failure mode: creators who copy one element (shocked face + bright colors) without the full discipline plateau around ~3% CTR. The system is the value, not any single trick. Serious channels make many variants (MrBeast's team reportedly tests up to 20 per video) and will swap a thumbnail within hours of upload if CTR underperforms.

### 5.2 Title formulas (with the reasons they work)

- **Specific number**: "7 Investing Mistakes That Cost Beginners $10,000" — numbers create concrete expectations; 20–30% CTR lift is typical.
- **Curiosity gap**: "The Drill That Doubled His Bat Speed in 7 Days" — implies the surprising fact without revealing it.
- **Transformation promise**: "How I [result] in [time] with [method]" — vicarious, personal, provable.
- **Warning/mistake**: "Stop Doing This If You Want [result]" — loss aversion.
- **Year + action**: "[Keyword] in 2026 (What Actually Works Now)" — recency signal.
- **Versus frame**: "[A] vs [B]: Which Is Actually Better?" — binary comparisons pull viewers seeking validation of a choice they already lean toward.
- **Achievement story**: "How [person] Got to [impressive number]."
- **Bracket/parenthetical suffix**: "(Step-by-Step)," "[Full Breakdown]," "(It's Not What You Think)" — bracketed titles measurably outperform in aggregate studies; the bracket adds a second micro-promise.

Mechanics:
- **40–65 characters**; over ~65 truncates on mobile. Put the compelling part and the keyword in the first 40 characters — YouTube search weights early-title keywords.
- Title and thumbnail carry roughly equal CTR weight and **must not duplicate each other** — thumbnail shows, title tells; together they should form one open loop, not two copies of the same statement.
- **Re-title underperformers.** If CTR sits below ~4%, write a new title with a different formula; YouTube re-evaluates updated packaging and old videos routinely revive. Documented creator A/B tests show CTR improvements of 37–110% from title/thumbnail testing alone — the cheapest views in the business, since no new content is produced.
- A/B testing (YouTube's native Test & Compare): wait 48–72h minimum for signal; 2–4 weeks for real significance on modest traffic.

### 5.3 The packaging-first workflow

1. Ideate as packages, not topics: write 5–10 title + thumbnail-sketch pairs per concept **before scripting**.
2. Kill concepts that can't be packaged compellingly — a weak package means either a weak idea or the wrong angle on a good idea.
3. The chosen title becomes the script's thesis. Every scene either advances the title's promise or gets cut.
4. Produce 2–3 thumbnail variants; test; be willing to swap within days.

## 6. Direct-response copywriting applied to organic content

The direct-response canon (Schwartz, Halbert, Ogilvy, Caples) was built on measured results from paid media. Organic content is the same game with a different currency: instead of buying attention you must earn it, which makes these principles *more* binding, not less.

### 6.1 Schwartz's five stages of awareness (Breakthrough Advertising, 1966)

Match the message's entry point to what the audience already knows. This is the highest-leverage framework in this document.

| Stage | Viewer's state | Content entry point |
|---|---|---|
| **Unaware** | Doesn't know they have the problem | Story, identity, dramatization; name the problem they can't articulate ("Suffering from launch paralysis?") |
| **Problem-aware** | Feels the pain, doesn't know solutions exist | Agitate + empathize; show the cost of inaction; promise a way out |
| **Solution-aware** | Knows solutions exist, not yours | Lead with the mechanism — *how* yours works differently |
| **Product-aware** | Knows you, unconvinced | Proof: case studies, demonstrations, comparisons, receipts |
| **Most aware** | Ready, needs a nudge | The offer, the deal, the deadline — just the CTA |

Application to organic: **top-of-funnel content (Shorts, Reels, broad YouTube) must be written for unaware/problem-aware viewers** — story-first, problem-naming, zero jargon. Mid-funnel (long-form, newsletter) serves solution/product-aware. Most creator content fails by speaking product-aware language (features, insider terms, "as you know") to unaware audiences.

Companion concept — **market sophistication**: how many similar claims the market has already heard. In a fresh market, a direct claim works ("Lose weight fast"). In a saturated one, you need bigger/more specific claims, then mechanism ("…through mud-burning ketones"), then identity and story. Fitness, business advice, and baseball training are all *highly* sophisticated markets — mechanism and proof beat raw claims.

### 6.2 Schwartz's headline intensification moves (the useful core of the 38)

Groupable into eight moves — run a draft hook/title through this list:

1. **Quantify**: measure the size, the speed, or compare ("3x more…", "in 48 hours").
2. **Vivify**: metaphor, sensory language, demonstration ("She launched at 11 PM. Woke up to $12K.").
3. **Dramatize/paradox**: "How I 10x'd output by working less."
4. **Borrow authority**: association, credential, case-history quote ("Ex-Google engineer reveals…", "'Made back the $2K in 9 days'").
5. **Newness/exclusivity**: "Just launched…", "Only we…"
6. **Challenge the reader**: "Can you spot the AI-written email?"
7. **Condense/symbolize the mechanism**: "A CFO in software form," "Delete half your copy — watch conversions double."
8. **Frame the need, warn, or address directly**: "Stuck at $5K/month? You're missing this metric," "Don't launch until you read this," "To the freelancer tired of feast-or-famine months." Even exclusion works: "Already booked solid? Close this tab" (qualification-by-repulsion — sharpens self-relevance for everyone else).

### 6.3 Copy structure formulas

- **PAS (Problem–Agitate–Solution)** — the workhorse for captions, newsletter intros, and script sections. State a specific, relatable problem → amplify its consequences and emotional cost → present the solution. Agitation is the step amateurs skip; without it the solution has no perceived value.
- **AIDA (Attention–Interest–Desire–Action)** — better for announcement/offer content where there's no burning pain to press on.
- **BAB (Before–After–Bridge)** — paint the current state, the desired state, then the bridge (your content/product). Natural fit for transformation content and testimonials.
- **4 U's for any headline/hook line**: Useful, Urgent, Unique, Ultra-specific. Score drafts 0–4; publish nothing under 3.

### 6.4 Halbert's laws, translated to organic

- **The list beats the copy beats the offer** → in creator terms: *audience-topic fit beats packaging beats production.* The right topic for the right audience forgives mediocre execution; the reverse is never true.
- **Write like you talk.** Read every script and caption aloud; anything you wouldn't say to one person across a table, cut. Conversational beats clever.
- **Know the audience's inner monologue** — past demographics, into the actual sentences they say to themselves at 11 PM. Mine comments, DMs, and community posts for verbatim language; the best hooks are the audience's own words played back.
- **Enter the conversation already occurring in the reader's head** (Collier, same lineage): hook onto what they're already thinking about this week, not what you want to say.

### 6.5 CTAs in organic content

- **One CTA per piece.** Multiple asks (like + subscribe + comment + link + join) = zero asks. Pick the one action that matters for this piece's job in the funnel.
- **Match CTA weight to relationship stage**: cold short-form earns a follow or a "watch this next," not a purchase. Asking for too much too early reads as the pushy-infomercial failure mode and suppresses everything.
- **CTA placement**: mid-roll soft mentions outperform end-stacked asks (a large share of viewers never reach the end); the strongest pattern is a one-line contextual CTA at the moment of peak delivered value ("the full template is linked below — back to the third mistake…").
- **Give the CTA a reason** ("comment your biggest sticking point so I make the follow-up on the right one") — reason-why compliance beats bare commands.
- **The next-video CTA is the most underused**: session time is an algorithmic signal; ending by opening a loop that a specific other video closes compounds a catalog.

## 7. Scripting for retention: the playbook

A repeatable process for any long-form script (compress the same steps for short-form):

1. **Package first.** Title + thumbnail sketch approved before word one of the script.
2. **Write the promise sentence.** One sentence: "By the end, the viewer will ___." Everything serves it.
3. **Beat outline with connectives.** One-line beats; write "but"/"therefore" between each pair; rewrite every "and then." Check the ~70/30 conflict/consequence mix.
4. **Loop map.** Mark where each loop opens and closes. Primary loop closes at 80–90%. No orphan loops.
5. **Draft the body first, the intro last.** You can't hook toward a video you haven't written.
6. **Intro pass**: promise (0–5s) → stakes/gap (5–15s) → path preview (15–25s) → hard cut to content (30s). Delete all throat-clearing.
7. **Concreteness pass**: replace every abstraction with a number, name, or image. Curse-of-knowledge audit: would the you-of-three-years-ago follow this?
8. **Read-aloud pass**: cut anything you wouldn't say to one person. Sentences over ~20 spoken words get split.
9. **Pattern-interrupt pass**: mark the visual/energy change every 30–60 seconds (location, b-roll, graphic, zoom, tone shift). Retention bleeds where the screen is static.
10. **CTA pass**: one CTA, placed at peak value, with a reason.
11. **Post-publish**: read the retention curve at 48h; log where exits cluster against the beat map; feed the finding into the next script. If CTR <4% after a week, re-package.

### Short-form checklist (per video)

- [ ] First frame is motion/face/action, not a title card
- [ ] Spoken hook ≤14 words, fires ≥2 cognitive triggers
- [ ] On-screen text adds info, doesn't transcribe the audio
- [ ] Payoff in the final 15–25%, no early spoil
- [ ] Ends on a loop or single CTA
- [ ] Would a stranger know who this is for within 2 seconds?

## 8. Common mistakes

1. **Throat-clearing intros** — branding, greetings, "before we start." Cause of most Cliff curves; the fix is deletion, not compression.
2. **Packaging that overpromises** — inflated CTR, cratered retention, algorithmic suppression, and eroded trust. The extreme claim is only allowed if the video cashes it.
3. **"And then" structure** — information sequenced instead of caused. The script reads fine on paper and dies on the retention graph.
4. **Curse of knowledge** — jargon, skipped setup, answering unasked questions. Expert creators are the most vulnerable.
5. **Speaking product-aware language to unaware audiences** — features and insider framing at the top of the funnel; nobody knows why they should care.
6. **Multiple or missing CTAs** — several asks that cancel out, or a valuable piece that leads nowhere. One piece, one job, one ask.
7. **Copying single packaging elements without the system** — the shocked face and saturated colors without focal discipline, stake, or promise-match; plateaus around 3% CTR.
8. **Title and thumbnail saying the same thing** — wastes half the packaging real estate; they should form one combined loop.
9. **Opening loops that never close** — teased content that doesn't arrive reads as betrayal; kills rewatch, subscribe, and trust.
10. **Judging retention percentage without length/context** — panicking over 35% on a 30-minute video, or celebrating 60% on a 2-minute one.
11. **Polishing production before fixing topic/packaging/hook** — spending on cameras and editors when the constraint is the first 30 seconds of the script.
12. **Self-focused copy** — "we're excited to announce" framing; the audience's problem never appears. Vague, generic, and skippable.
13. **Never re-titling old videos** — leaving the cheapest available views (37–110% CTR lifts from packaging tests) on the table.
14. **Sounding like everyone's AI draft** — generic voice, no verbatim audience language, no opinion. In sophisticated markets, mechanism + personality is the only durable differentiation.

## 9. Questions Carl should ask

**Diagnosing the funnel**
1. What's your CTR by video over the last 10 uploads, and what's retention at 0:30? (Locates the broken gate before any creative debate.)
2. Show me your worst retention curve — where exactly do people leave, and what's happening in the script at that timestamp?
3. Do you write the title and thumbnail before or after making the video?

**Hooks & structure**
4. Read me your last three opening lines. How many words before the promise lands? What trigger does each fire?
5. Take your last script outline — can you put "but" or "therefore" between every pair of beats?
6. What loop does the title open, and at what percentage of the runtime does it close?

**Audience & copy**
7. Which awareness stage is this piece written for, and does the distribution surface match (short-form = unaware/problem-aware)?
8. What sentences do your viewers actually use in comments/DMs to describe their problem? Are those words in your hooks?
9. What's the one action this piece asks for, and where does the ask sit?

**Systems**
10. What are your 5–10 house hook formulas, and when did you last test two openings against each other?
11. Which of your old videos still earn impressions with sub-4% CTR — and why haven't they been re-titled?
12. If I muted your video, would the first 3 seconds still stop a scroll? If I only read the title, would I know exactly who it's for and what they get?

**Relevance notes for this client:** Mayday's YouTube-first model lives and dies at gates 1–2 — packaging-first workflow and intro-deletion are the highest-leverage habits to install. Neptune Performance sells into a *highly sophisticated* market (baseball training claims are saturated), so its organic content should lead with mechanism + proof (measured player results, e.g., before/after exit velo with timeframes) rather than raw promises — the "educational content disguised as a case story" pattern is the natural fit, and parent-targeted hooks are self-relevance plays ("if your 12-year-old…").
