# Community Building & Superfan Cultivation

## TL;DR

- **Community is the only moat a small media company can actually build.** Products, formats, and content styles get copied in weeks; a community with relationships, rituals, and shared identity cannot be cloned. Leaving a community means abandoning social capital — that's emotional switching cost no competitor can undercut (Spinks, *The Business of Belonging*).
- **Don't chase 1,000 true fans at $100 — the modern play is layered: many casual fans at $0, hundreds at $50–100/yr, and a small tier at $500–1,000+/yr.** Real median direct superfan spend is ~$52/yr, not Kelly's $100; but 100 fans at $1,000/yr (Li Jin's "100 True Fans") is achievable when you sell transformation, access, and accountability instead of content.
- **Expect the 90-9-1 rule and design for it, don't fight it.** ~90% lurk, ~9% contribute occasionally, ~1% drive most activity. Spend your energy on the top contributors (they carry the room), lower the participation bar for everyone else, and stop treating lurkers as failure — they're your audience-to-member pipeline.
- **Pick ONE business objective for the community (SPACES model) before choosing a platform or launching anything.** A community that's simultaneously for support, feedback, acquisition, and engagement serves none well.
- **Rituals beat features.** A weekly recurring event people can set their watch by does more for retention than any channel structure. Communities die from inconsistent programming, not bad software.
- **Founder-led everything is the #1 burnout path and the #1 failure mode.** Plan the volunteer moderator/leader pipeline from day one; the goal by month 12–18 is that the community runs a week without you and nobody notices.
- **Health benchmarks to hold in your head:** DAU/MAU ≥ 20% is sticky; monthly churn on paid membership < 5% is healthy, < 2.5% excellent; YouTube subscriber → paid member conversion runs 0.5–2% (3–5% for tight niches); ~83% of new communities are effectively dead within a year — the default outcome is failure, so under-scope and over-program.
- **Monetize commitment, not attendance.** The Commitment Curve says make bigger asks only after smaller ones are accepted. Free community → low-cost membership → high-ticket cohort/access is a ladder, not a paywall slapped on a Discord.

## 1. Why community is a moat (the strategic case)

Three mechanisms make community defensible in a way content never is:

1. **Emotional switching costs.** A viewer can switch channels in one click. A member who has friendships, reputation, inside jokes, and a role in your community loses all of that by leaving. Spinks frames this as community "deepening switching costs by creating social bonds."
2. **Compounding contribution.** Members answer each other's questions, create content, recruit new members, and generate ideas. The CMX/Discourse benchmark world tracks this concretely: mature communities deflect ~25% of support queries and can source 20%+ of product/content ideas from members.
3. **Demand insulation.** Algorithms deprioritize you; sponsors churn; a community is an owned, direct relationship. Kevin Kelly's original condition for 1,000 True Fans still holds: *the model only works if fans pay you directly and you can reach them without a gatekeeper.* A YouTube subscriber count is rented; a Discord/email/membership list is owned.

**Market context:** Goldman Sachs sized the superfan monetization opportunity at ~$4.5B (2024 revision, up from $4.2B in 2023). Platforms (Patreon, Discord, Circle, Skool, Whop) have made direct fan monetization operationally trivial — the constraint is now community design, not tooling.

**The sober counterweight:** up to ~83% of online communities fail within their first year (widely-cited community industry figure). Communities are a high-variance bet whose failure modes are well documented (section 9). The moat only exists if the community survives; treat the first 12–18 months as an active leadership commitment, not a launch.

## 2. Named frameworks (the canon)

### 2.1 SPACES Model (CMX / David Spinks) — what is the community FOR?

Six business value objectives. **Pick one primary; add others only after mastering the first.**

| Letter | Objective | What it looks like | Key metrics |
|---|---|---|---|
| **S** | Support | Members answer each other's questions | Case deflection %, answered-question rate |
| **P** | Product ideation & feedback | Members shape what you build/make | % features/content sourced from community |
| **A** | Acquisition & advocacy | Ambassadors, referrals, word of mouth | Referred members/customers, event attendance |
| **C** | Content & contribution | Members create the content itself (UGC) | Content volume, active contributors |
| **E** | Engagement | Loyalty and retention around shared identity | DAU/MAU, retention, NPS |
| **S** | Success | Members help each other get better outcomes | Customer LTV, retention, satisfaction |

Rule: *let the chosen objective determine the metrics, not vice versa.* For a creator media business the natural primary is **Engagement** (retention/loyalty), with **Acquisition** as the second-order effect. For a training facility the natural primary is **Success** (members getting results keeps them paying) with **Engagement** close behind.

### 2.2 The Commitment Curve (Douglas Atkin → Spinks)

Members move up a curve of increasing commitment. **Match the size of your ask to their position on the curve** — asking a day-one member to lead an event kills the relationship; never asking your two-year veterans to lead anything wastes them.

Ladder of asks (small → large):
1. Read a post / lurk (zero-friction)
2. React / vote / poll answer
3. Introduce yourself
4. Reply to someone else
5. Start a thread / ask a question
6. Attend a live event
7. Share work / post content
8. Help another member unprompted
9. Host or co-host a ritual
10. Moderate / lead a sub-group / recruit

Design explicit on-ramps between rungs. Most communities have a cliff between rung 1 and rung 5 — that cliff is where 90% of members stall permanently.

### 2.3 The 90-9-1 rule / participation inequality (Jakob Nielsen, NN/g)

- ~90% lurk, ~9% contribute intermittently, ~1% account for most activity. The distribution is a Zipf curve; exact numbers vary (blogs run 95-5-0.1; Wikipedia is 99.8-0.2-0.003) but the shape is universal and has held since Usenet.
- **Implications:**
  - Your community's "voice" is the 1% — it is *not representative* of your membership or audience. Don't redesign your product/content around the loudest 10 people.
  - You cannot equalize participation; you can only shift the ratio slightly. Nielsen's levers: lower contribution barriers (reactions vs. essays), make participation a side effect of normal use, offer templates to modify rather than blank pages, reward quality contributors visibly.
  - Spinks' corollary: **prioritize the top 20% of contributors over converting lurkers.** The creators carry the room; lurkers still get value (and still convert to buyers) from watching.

### 2.4 The 7 Ps of community design (CMX)

Define these before launching any community experience: **People** (who exactly), **Purpose** (why gather), **Place** (platform/venue), **Participation** (what members do), **Policy** (rules/norms), **Promotion** (how they find it), **Performance** (how you measure). A one-page 7Ps doc is the minimum viable community strategy.

### 2.5 Get Together framework (Richardson/Huynh/Sotto, Stripe Press)

Core philosophy: **"Build your community WITH people, not FOR them."** Three stages:
1. **Spark the flame** — rally the first people. Start with who's already showing up passionately (your "hand-raisers"), not a cold launch. Do things that don't scale: personal invites, small gatherings.
2. **Stoke the fire** — get people talking to *each other* (not just to you), create shared identity, attract authentic new folks.
3. **Pass the torch** — develop member-leaders, distribute ownership, expand. This is the anti-burnout stage most founders never reach.
Case studies span Instant Pot, Twitch, Rapha Cycling Club, TED Translators — the pattern is identical across a kitchen-appliance Facebook group and a global cycling club.

### 2.6 The Orbit Model (funnel alternative)

Instead of pushing people down a funnel toward a transaction, your brand is a **center of gravity** and members occupy orbits of varying closeness. Members drift closer (higher "gravity": more love + more reach) or farther over time; your job is to increase gravity, not force conversion. Useful mental model for a creator: casual viewers = outer orbit, subscribers = mid, community members = inner, superfans/leaders = core. Every piece of programming should be designed to pull someone one orbit inward.

### 2.7 1,000 True Fans → 100 True Fans (Kevin Kelly 2008 → Li Jin/a16z 2020)

- **Kelly's original math:** 1,000 fans × $100/yr direct spend = $100K living. Conditions: direct payment, direct reach, you must *create* enough to absorb $100/yr per fan.
- **2026 reality check:** measured median direct superfan spend is ~$52/yr (merch + membership + live + direct purchases), so a pure Kelly model needs ~2,000 true fans or higher ARPU offers.
- **Li Jin's inversion (100 True Fans @ $1,000/yr):** works when you shift from a *donation* model ("support me") to a *value* model ("this changes your outcomes"). Her four enablers of high price points:
  1. Premium content with **no substitutes** (exclusive access/community)
  2. **Tangible results** (skill, fitness, income, transformation)
  3. **Accountability** (paying more = committing more; the price IS the product)
  4. **Access & status** (proximity to the creator, recognition, exclusivity)
- Evidence from her piece: Teachable art instructor $110K from 76 students ($1,437 avg); physio instructor $141K from 61 students ($2,314 avg); Patreon $1,200+/yr patrons grew 21% in two years.
- **Practical synthesis: run both simultaneously as a pyramid.** Broad free audience → $5–15/mo membership (Kelly tier) → $500–3,000 high-touch cohort/coaching/access tier (Jin tier). The high tier often out-earns the low tier with 5% of the members and 20% of the support load.

### 2.8 Community-Led Growth — the Figma playbook (First Round Review, Claire Butler)

Best-documented CLG case study; the five phases generalize far beyond SaaS:

1. **Stealth → buzz:** embed in *existing* communities before building your own; run informal demos; watch for the momentum signal (users pushing you aside to try it themselves).
2. **Launch via existing networks:** map the influence graph of your niche (Figma literally scripted the "design Twittersphere"), recruit respected advocates, publish *deeply technical/insider* content — "a lot of technical audiences do not want how-to content."
3. **Power the community with evangelists:** hire/anoint advocates from within (Figma found their first via a 10-person pizza night); create spectator-friendly rituals (Pixel Pong — livestreamed design competitions with audience voting).
4. **Introduce pricing without breaking the magic moment:** Figma's first paywall blocked the collaboration magic and failed; the fix gated *quantity* (files) not *magic* (collaborators). Lesson: never paywall the thing that makes people fall in love.
5. **Move upmarket via champions:** find the internal superfan and give them a "gentle assist," rather than selling top-down. (Creator translation: your community superfans are your route into brand deals, venue partnerships, and B2B opportunities — arm them.)

## 3. Platform strategy: Discord vs. Circle vs. everything else

**The platform question is downstream of the purpose question.** "What's the core purpose of your community?" decides it — tools don't build community, design does.

| Dimension | Discord | Circle (& similar: Skool, Mighty Networks) |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | Free (Nitro/server boosts optional) | ~$39+/mo and up |
| Structure | Real-time chat channels, voice, stages | Threaded posts, spaces, courses |
| Vibe | Energetic, chaotic, always-on | Calm, organized, professional |
| Monetization | Bolt-on (third-party bots, external payments, server subscriptions) | Native (Stripe, tiers, gated spaces, courses) |
| Branding | Discord's brand everywhere | White-label, your brand |
| Discoverability of old content | Terrible (chat scrolls away) | Good (threads persist, searchable) |
| Email reach to members | None | Built-in broadcasts |
| Best for | Fandoms, gaming, live-event energy, younger audiences, "momentum, movement, connection" | Courses, coaching, transformation programs, paid memberships, "educate, transform, guide" |

**Decision heuristics:**
- Audience already lives on Discord (gaming, youth, fandom) → Discord. Fighting platform gravity is a losing war.
- Revenue model is paid membership/courses → Circle-class tool; native payments + email + gating pays for itself.
- **Hybrid pattern (common for creators):** free Discord as the top-of-funnel hangout + paid Circle/Skool as the premium layer. Costs: two surfaces to program. Only do this once the free layer is genuinely alive.
- **Chat vs. forum by size (Spinks):** chat platforms feel dense and alive for *small* groups but become noise at scale; forums feel dead when small but compound value at scale. A 50-person chat feels like a party; a 50-person forum feels like a ghost town; at 5,000 the polarity flips.
- **Don't forget the boring options:** an email list + monthly Zoom + occasional IRL meetup is a legitimate community stack with near-zero platform risk. Platform choice is the *least* important decision on this page.

**Platform-risk note:** everything on Discord is one ToS change from disruption, and you can't email your members. Whatever the hangout platform, *always* capture emails at the membership layer.

## 4. Benchmarks & numbers (memorize these)

**Health / engagement**
- DAU/MAU (stickiness): **≥ 20%** is the standard healthy bar for a community.
- Lurker/contributor split: expect **~90/9/1**; a community at 70/20/10 is exceptional.
- ~80% of content comes from ~20% of members (Pareto, per Spinks).
- Circle's 2025 Community Trends Report: 75% of community managers track **total active members** as their #1 metric.
- Sense-of-community failure base rate: **~83% of communities fail within year one.**

**Paid membership / monetization**
- Subscriber → paid member conversion (YouTube memberships): **1–2% standard**, 3–5% for engaged niche channels (finance, education, coaching), 0.5–1% for broad entertainment. Education/tutorial channels: 2–4%.
- Most common membership price point: **$5/mo**. Multi-tier creators (with at least one premium tier) earn **~17% more revenue** on average.
- Fee math on $10K/mo membership revenue: YouTube Memberships nets ~$7,000; Patreon Pro nets ~$8,700–9,200 → ~$20K+/yr difference in Patreon's favor at that scale. (YouTube's counterweight: zero-friction conversion inside the watch page.)
- Median direct superfan spend: **~$52/yr**; Kelly's assumption was $100; Jin's high-tier model targets $1,000+.
- Documented outcomes: one channel made YouTube Memberships **60% of total revenue within 6 months** by treating it as a product; a top channel cleared **$217K/yr from memberships alone**; syncing Patreon + YouTube memberships drove +24% revenue in two months for another.

**Churn / retention (paid community)**
- Monthly churn **< 5% = healthy**, **< 2.5% = excellent**, > 25% = crisis (Recurly/industry benchmarks).
- Day-one churn is the biggest leak: like consumer apps, most members who go inactive do so in the first week. Onboarding is the highest-ROI intervention (section 6).

**Support / value metrics (mature stage, from Discourse's 20-metrics framework)**
- Case/support deflection target: ~25% of queries answered by community.
- NPS target: > 70 for a strong community.
- "Disappointment score" (Sean Ellis-style): aim for **≥ 40–60% "very disappointed if this community disappeared"** — the single best one-question health check.
- Community-driven initiatives: ~5 member-led events/quarter is a good "torch has been passed" signal.

## 5. Engagement rituals: the retention engine

A **ritual** = a recurring behavior with an event attached, part of a program, that members come to expect. Rituals are how a community develops a heartbeat independent of any single piece of content.

**Cadence architecture (Spinks):** design deliberate touchpoints at every timescale:
- **Daily:** a prompt, standup thread, or question-of-the-day (low bar; carried by the 9%)
- **Weekly:** the anchor ritual — live show, co-working session, game night, film-room breakdown, weekly wins thread. *This is the one that matters most; protect it above all else.*
- **Monthly:** deeper event — AMA, guest expert, member spotlight, challenge kickoff, competition
- **Quarterly:** milestone — awards, tournament, cohort graduation, big challenge finale
- **Annually:** the pilgrimage — IRL meetup, summit, anniversary event. Even 20 people at an annual IRL event creates a mythology that fuels 12 months of online engagement.

**Ritual design rules:**
1. **Same time, same place, every time.** Predictability > production value. A scrappy weekly event that never misses beats a polished monthly one that slips.
2. **Member-facing, not creator-facing.** The best rituals put members on stage (spotlights, show-and-tell, competitions with audience voting — see Figma's Pixel Pong). The creator hosting a lecture every week is content, not community.
3. **Balance sync + async (Spinks #11).** Async (threads, prompts) gives breadth and timezone coverage; sync (live events, voice) creates the actual bonds. You need both; async-only communities never gel, sync-only communities exclude most members.
4. **Rituals create identity artifacts.** Names, inside jokes, recurring segments, badges for streaks — the vocabulary of belonging. Instant Pot owners calling themselves "Potheads" is worth more than any feature.
5. **Retire dead rituals fast.** A ritual limping at 10% of its former attendance drags the whole community's perceived vitality down. Kill it publicly, replace it.

**Investment cycles (venture twins):** engineer regular moments where members *put something in* — monetary (paywall, dues) or non-monetary (monthly assignments, event attendance requirements, intro homework). Out-of-Pocket requires monthly assignments and quarterly event participation as a condition of membership. Skin in the game predicts retention better than any onboarding flow.

## 6. Playbooks

### 6.1 Zero → alive: launching a community (first 90 days)

1. **Write the 7Ps one-pager.** Especially Purpose: finish the sentence "This is the place where [specific people] [do specific thing] together." If the answer is "hang out with fans of X," that's an audience, not a community — find the member-to-member value.
2. **Recruit 10–30 founding members by personal invitation.** Hand-pick from your most engaged audience (top commenters, repeat buyers, people who already DM each other). Do NOT open the doors publicly yet. Signal to look for: are these people already self-organizing (making Twitter lists, starting group chats)? That's product-market fit for a community.
3. **Pre-seed before anyone arrives.** Empty rooms repel. Have 2 weeks of conversations, intros, and one ritual already running before wave two joins.
4. **Establish the anchor weekly ritual in week one** and never miss it for the first six months.
5. **Onboard every member personally** at this stage: a DM welcome, a personal intro post that tags 1–2 members they should meet (The Generalist's approach), and one small first ask (rung 2–3 of the Commitment Curve).
6. **Open the doors in waves, not a flood.** Each wave should be absorbable by the existing culture (rough rule: don't grow > 30–50% per wave early). Quality of early members sets the culture permanently.
7. **Lead by example for ~18 months.** Communities don't lead themselves in the formative stage; expect to model every behavior you want repeated. Budget founder/team time honestly — 5–10 hrs/week minimum early, or don't start.

### 6.2 Alive → self-sustaining: passing the torch (months 6–24)

1. **Identify emerging leaders from behavior, not applications:** who answers questions unprompted, welcomes newcomers, keeps a cool head in disputes? Check their history.
2. **Ask small first** (Commitment Curve): "Can you kick off Tuesday's thread this week?" before "Will you be a moderator?"
3. **Formalize a tiered leadership program:** e.g., Helper → Event Host → Moderator → Community Lead. Each tier gets defined responsibilities, real perks (early access, free membership, merch, direct access to you, first question at AMAs, revenue share for big roles), training, and a named person they report to.
4. **Write the moderation playbook:** rules, escalation paths, ban criteria, tone guide, response-time expectations. Volunteer coverage is uneven and burnout runs high — rotate duties, cap shifts, create a private mod channel for venting/support, and give explicit permission to step back.
5. **Ratio heuristics:** roughly 1 active moderator per 500–1,000 active members for chat communities; forums need fewer. Add mod capacity *ahead* of growth spurts, not after the first incident.
6. **Success criterion:** the community runs for a full week with zero founder input and activity doesn't dip. Then, and only then, is it a moat.

### 6.3 Free → paid: converting community to revenue

1. **Sequence: value first, ask second.** Run the free layer well for 3–6 months so the paid offer sells against demonstrated value, not promises.
2. **Choose the revenue architecture (pick per SPACES objective):**
   - **Membership/subscription** ($5–15/mo): recurring access, bonus content, member-only rituals, early access. The volume tier. Expect 1–2% of engaged audience.
   - **Premium tier** ($30–100/mo): small-group access, monthly calls with you, feedback/critique, priority.
   - **High-ticket** ($500–3,000): cohorts, coaching, masterminds, done-with-you. Li Jin's four enablers must ALL be present (no substitutes, tangible results, accountability, access/status).
   - **Adjacent monetization the community amplifies:** merch (community = design input + launch army), live events/experiences, sponsorship (an engaged community is a premium sponsor asset — engagement rates, not just reach), affiliate/product sales.
3. **Never paywall the magic moment** (Figma's lesson). Gate quantity, depth, and proximity — not the core thing that makes people love you. For a creator: the free community keeps the hangout; paid gets closeness and outcomes.
4. **Price the middle tier at the impulse threshold ($5–10)** and always offer at least one premium tier (multi-tier = ~17% more revenue). Anchor high.
5. **Launch to the commitment-curve top first:** offer founding-member pricing to your 1% before public launch; their testimonials and presence sell the tier.
6. **Retention > acquisition once paid:** target < 5% monthly churn. Levers: the weekly ritual (habit), visible progress/status (levels, streaks, badges), personal touch at cancellation-risk moments (30 days inactive → personal check-in), annual pricing (pre-paid commitment).
7. **Watch the fee stack:** platform fees range ~3–13% (Patreon) to 30% (YouTube memberships). At meaningful scale, owning the billing relationship (Circle+Stripe, Memberful) is worth the migration pain.

### 6.4 Community-led growth loop (turning members into acquisition)

1. Make membership *visible* externally: member wins shared publicly, member content credited, community moments in your main content ("as someone in the Discord said…").
2. Give superfans shareable artifacts: badges, merch, clips, referral links, "I built this in the community" outputs.
3. Run spectator-friendly rituals (competitions, showcases) that non-members can watch — the event is the ad.
4. Track referral rate (target: growing ~15%/quarter early) and % of new members from member invitations. When members recruit members, you have community-led growth; until then you have content-led growth with a community attached.

## 7. Application notes: small media company + training facility

**For a YouTube-first media company (Mayday-type):**
- The community's honest primary SPACES objective is **Engagement** (superfan retention) with **Content** second (community as clip source, idea source, comment army that boosts early video velocity).
- Conversion math: a channel with 100K engaged subscribers should expect 500–2,000 paying members at $5/mo = $2.5K–10K/mo — meaningful but rarely the top revenue line. The larger value: community engagement data makes sponsorship inventory more valuable, and superfans buy merch at multiples of casual viewers.
- Discord fits a fandom-style audience; but capture emails via the membership layer regardless.
- Best creator ritual pattern: weekly members-only live hangout + watch-alongs of your own uploads + member inside-jokes seeded back into main content (the feedback loop makes members feel like co-authors).

**For a training facility (Neptune-type):**
- Community IS the retention product. Fitness-industry research consistently ranks community as the top retention lever; group programming, challenges, member events, and social nights measurably reduce churn. A facility member with friends at the facility does not comparison-shop on price.
- The SPACES objective is **Success**: members visibly improving (velo boards, PR walls, progress spotlights) is both the ritual and the marketing.
- Local flavor: pop-up events, partner nights with local brands, parent community (for youth athletes, the *parents* are the real community and the real buyers), season send-offs and draft-day watch parties.
- Hybrid online/offline: a facility with a members' group chat/Discord extends the community between sessions and is nearly free to run.
- 1,000 True Fans maps cleanly: ~200 committed athlete families at $2,000–4,000/yr is a real business — this is the 100-True-Fans value model (tangible results + accountability + access) in physical form.

## 8. Measurement stack (what to instrument, by stage)

**Stage 1 — Formation (0–6 mo):** weekly active members, ritual attendance, % of members who posted at least once, day-7 new-member activity rate. Vanity trap: total member count.
**Stage 2 — Growth (6–18 mo):** DAU/MAU (≥20%), referral/invite rate, contributor count (the 9%+1%), event attendance trend, first revenue conversion rate.
**Stage 3 — Maturity (18 mo+):** paid conversion %, monthly churn (<5%), NPS (>70), disappointment score (≥40–60% "very disappointed"), % member-led initiatives, support/idea contribution if relevant.
**Always:** one metric as the north star tied to your SPACES objective; review monthly; resist dashboard sprawl (Discourse's advice: focus on metrics aligned with goals, ignore the rest).

## 9. Common mistakes

1. **Launching a container, not a community.** Spinning up a Discord and inviting everyone = "zombie community": thousands of members, perma-muted, glorified group chat. Purpose and programming must precede the platform.
2. **Building FOR people instead of WITH them.** Deciding structure, rules, and rituals unilaterally, then wondering why nobody owns anything. Co-create from member one.
3. **Optimizing for member count.** More members = more noise; large inactive communities actively damage retention (spam, dead channels signal decline). Curation and waves beat open floodgates.
4. **Skipping onboarding.** Most churn happens in the first days. No welcome, no intro, no first small ask = permanent lurker or silent exit.
5. **Fighting the 90-9-1 instead of designing for it.** Guilt-tripping lurkers, engagement-bait polls, and "why is nobody talking??" posts read as desperation. Serve the 1%, lower barriers for the 9%, let the 90% watch happily.
6. **Founder as single point of failure.** Carrying all energy yourself is the fastest road to burnout and the most common death: server admins who never develop other animators, then quietly quit. Pass the torch on a schedule, not when you're already fried.
7. **No rituals / inconsistent rituals.** Communities without a heartbeat decay to ghost towns; nothing kills faith faster than the weekly event silently not happening.
8. **Duplicating Facebook/Reddit.** If your space does nothing a member can't get elsewhere, they won't add another app to their life — people meaningfully sustain only 2–3 communities (attention budget). Differentiation = access to you, to each other's specific expertise, or to outcomes.
9. **Paywalling the magic moment.** Gating the very thing that creates love (Figma's failed first pricing). Gate depth and proximity instead.
10. **Monetizing before trust.** Slapping a paid tier on a two-month-old community converts nobody and taints the free layer. Value first, ask second.
11. **Treating moderation as an afterthought.** No written rules, no escalation path, no mod support → first big conflict does permanent culture damage. Also: unsupported volunteer mods burn out and take institutional knowledge with them.
12. **Letting the loud 1% steer the business.** Community feedback is systematically unrepresentative (NN/g). Weigh it, verify with data from the silent majority.
13. **Measuring nothing, or everything.** Either flying blind or drowning in 20 dashboards. One north-star metric per SPACES objective.
14. **Ignoring platform risk.** No email capture, everything inside Discord's walls. Own the relationship layer.

## 10. Questions Carl should ask a client

**Purpose & fit**
1. Finish this sentence: "This is the place where ___ do ___ together." Can you? Is the value member-to-member, or is it all radiating from you?
2. Is your audience already self-organizing anywhere (group chats, comment friendships, meetups)? If not, why do you believe they want each other and not just you?
3. Which single SPACES objective is this community for? What business number moves if it works?

**Capacity & sustainability**
4. Who is spending 5–10 hours/week on this for the next 18 months? What happens to it when that person has a bad month?
5. What's the plan for the community to run one week without you? By when?
6. Who are your three most likely first volunteer leaders, by name, based on behavior you've already seen?

**Design**
7. What's your anchor weekly ritual? Would it survive you being sick for two weeks?
8. What's the new member's first 48 hours, step by step? What's their first small ask?
9. What can members do or get here that they cannot get from your public content, Reddit, or a Facebook group?
10. Chat or forum — and does that match your community's size and your audience's native platform?

**Economics**
11. What percentage of your engaged audience do you assume converts to paid, and is it under 2%? (If the model needs 10%, the model is broken.)
12. Which tier is your Kelly tier ($5–15/mo volume) and which is your Jin tier ($500+ transformation)? Does the high tier have all four enablers: no substitutes, tangible results, accountability, access/status?
13. What exactly is free vs. paid — and did you accidentally paywall the magic moment?
14. What's your monthly churn, and what happens on day 30 of a member going quiet?
15. Do you own your members' emails, or does Discord/Patreon/YouTube own the relationship?

**Health check (existing communities)**
16. What's your DAU/MAU? What % of members posted in the last 30 days?
17. If the community shut down tomorrow, what % of members would be "very disappointed"? Have you asked?
18. When did a member last run an event without you? When did a member last recruit another member?
19. Which rituals are actually dying, and are you keeping them on life support out of sentiment?
20. Is your product/content roadmap being steered by the loudest 1%? How do you hear from the silent 90%?
