# Sports & Athlete-Creator YouTube Case Studies

Who actually wins in sports/athlete-creator YouTube, exactly how they do it, and what transfers to a retired-MLB-pitcher channel. Tactical execution reference — for algorithm mechanics, packaging fundamentals, and retention theory see the strategy layer (../../Carl/organic-marketing/01-youtube-growth-strategy.md).

## TL;DR

- **The winning athlete-creator format is "access + stakes + compression," not vlogs.** Bryson DeChambeau's rule: golf is 4 boring hours — his job is condensing it to a watchable hour with a challenge frame (Break 50). Trevor's equivalent: compress a pitching lab session, a facility build, or a "can I still..." challenge into a stakes-driven 15–25 min video, never a diary.
- **The credibility arbitrage is the moat.** Non-athlete baseball channels (Jomboy, Foolish Baseball, Baseball Doesn't Exist) win with editing and research; an actual retired MLB pitcher can do what none of them can — demonstrate, decode, and get access. Every format should exploit something only a former big-leaguer can do.
- **Repeatable named series beat one-offs in every case studied.** Jomboy's "Breakdowns," Foolish Baseball's "Baseball Bits," Bryson's "Break 50," Jesser's "Pros vs. YouTubers" — the series name IS the packaging system. Trevor needs 2–3 named formats per channel, run until the audience stops clicking.
- **Cadence sweet spot for solo/small-team athlete channels: one long-form every 7–14 days.** DeChambeau ships one challenge video roughly every 2 weeks and that's called "textbook" cadence by industry analysts (as of 2025). Jomboy-style daily output requires a staff; don't imitate it without one.
- **Collaboration is the single fastest growth lever in this niche.** Grant Horvat built 1.5M subs almost entirely on a collab ladder (Rick Shiels → Bryan Bros → Good Good → Tiger Woods). Golf proved guests-with-audiences compound; baseball's collab web (Jomboy, Baseball Is Dead pod, MLB creator programs, other ex-players) is far less saturated.
- **Content is the wedge, commerce is the business.** Good Good: $40M+ revenue, ~75% from apparel/products, not AdSense (as of late 2025). For Trevor, the YouTube channels are top-of-funnel for Neptune Performance, training products, and sponsorships — optimize videos for the audience that buys, not just raw views.
- **Retired > active for content flexibility, but retired athletes must out-produce.** A 3,306-video academic study found retired athletes skew educational (50.2% lessons/info) and stay closer to their sport (87.6% on-sport), yet active athletes out-engage them — retired athletes must add entertainment value on top of instruction, not rely on name recognition.
- **Leagues now pay creators, not the reverse (as of 2025–2026).** MLB bought a minority stake in Jomboy, runs Creator Class, opened highlight/archive licensing via TikTok deals, and launched MLB Players Studio. A former player with a growing channel is exactly who these programs want — this is free distribution and IP access waiting to be claimed.

## 1. The landscape: five archetypes that win in sports YouTube

Every successful sports/athlete channel studied fits one of five archetypes. Knowing which archetype a video (or channel) belongs to dictates its packaging, length, and cadence.

| Archetype | Exemplars | Core engine | Typical length | Cadence | Team size |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Breakdown/reactor** | Jomboy Media | Speed + unique lens on live moments (lip-reading, rules) | 4–12 min | Daily-ish, news-driven | Staff (50+) |
| **Documentary essayist** | Baseball Doesn't Exist, Foolish Baseball | Research + narrative on evergreen stories | 15–30 min | Every 2–4 weeks | 1–3 people |
| **Athlete challenge/access** | Bryson DeChambeau, Erling Haaland, Josh Kerr | Elite skill + stakes + celebrity access | 20–60 min | Every 1–2 weeks | Small crew |
| **Crew entertainment** | Good Good, Bob Does Sports, Dude Perfect | Personality chemistry + competition formats | 20–40 min | Weekly+ | Full production co. |
| **Athlete talk/podcast** | Gil's Arena (Arenas), Funky Friday (Cam Newton), Talkin' Baseball (Plouffe) | Unfiltered insider takes, clip machine | 60–120 min + clips | 1–5x/week | 2–6 people |

**Trevor's mapping:** Trevor May Baseball = documentary essayist × athlete challenge/access hybrid. More Mayday = crew entertainment × athlete talk. The "Mayday! with Trevor May" podcast = athlete talk archetype (clip-farm it — see §6).

**The fame-alone caveat:** mega-fame converts to subscribers automatically — Haaland hit 1.4M subs in 7 months, Lando Norris holds 1.4M off a couple of videos a year, Ronaldo had the fastest launch in YouTube history — but watch time still requires format. Mid-fame athletes (i.e., nearly all retired MLB players) get no automatic spike; they must win the same way media-native creators do, with the credential as a content *input* rather than a growth engine.

## 2. Channel-by-channel breakdowns

### 2.1 Jomboy Media — the distribution-speed machine

- **Origin mechanics:** Jimmy O'Brien was a wedding videographer posting 2 videos/week to a 4K-sub personal channel. Inflection: 2017 MLB rule change allowing creators to reshare official content. He posted real-time Yankees highlights + 1-minute reaction videos to Twitter and made a habit of **being the first reply to the official team's final-score tweet** — parasiting maximum exposure off owned-media traffic. Tactical lesson: find the highest-traffic recurring post in your niche and systematically be the best first reply.
- **Signature format:** the "Breakdown" — unusual moment (fight, ejection, cheating scandal) + lip-reading + nonchalant couch-commentary delivery. Two 2019 breakdowns (Aaron Boone "Savages," 2.6M views; Astros cheating) created **permanent baseline elevation, not spikes**, because volume of back catalog converted one-time viewers into subscribers.
- **Tone doctrine (quote):** "professional by broadcast, amateurish by tone." Production quality rose; couch-fan voice never changed. When a channel professionalizes its *tone* along with its gear, it dies.
- **Audience doctrine:** targeted "the diehard Yankee fan in each friend group" — the person who *relays* content to 10 others. Optimize for the sharer, not the median viewer.
- **Scale & validation (as of 2025):** $10M+ annual revenue in 2024 (up 40%+ YoY, profits +122%); 63% YoY audience growth Q1 2025, 93M social engagements/year, top 4% of digital publishers; ~2M subs main channel; views +76% in the year after the MLB deal. MLB bought a minority stake (June 2025) with **no board seat and no editorial control** — O'Brien kept majority. The stake buys Jomboy: team-logo merch licensing, All-Star/Derby event access, player participation in Warehouse Games. Precedent: leagues will fund creators who prove audience without demanding control.
- **Warehouse Games economics (the owned-league play):** retrofitted Jersey City carpet warehouse with ceiling-mounted cameras; four invented games (Blitzball, ball-in-play, floorball, slapball) with rules tuned for pacing (2-inning games). Budget ≈ **$50K per Blitzball production** — covering play-by-play commentary, stats packaging, in-game interviews, and postgame press conferences. They manufacture the *rituals* of pro sports around a backyard game, and the ritual is what makes it feel like a league. Blitzball Battle 5 set the channel's single-day record (54K views in 24h); the franchise was licensed to Bally Sports across 17 RSNs (June 2024). Lesson: **a fixed, camera-rigged venue you own collapses production cost per episode and creates licensable format IP** — directly relevant to a training facility.
- **Cost of scale (honest number):** in 2019 O'Brien worked 9am–2am for ~$500/month after a $25K investment. Daily-output archetype is a staffing decision, not a solo grind.
- **Transfers to Trevor:** the breakdown format is open to an upgrade only a player can make — *"what the pitcher was actually thinking"* breakdowns (pitch selection, mound conversations, what an ejection feels like from the mound). Jomboy reads lips; Trevor reads minds, because he's been in the moment.

### 2.2 Baseball Doesn't Exist — the documentary essayist

- **Creator:** Joey Duffield, launched Aug 2020, solo-start. Format: 20–30 min documentary deep-dives on baseball's strangest players and scandals (Greinke, Canseco, Bauer, Chapman; frauds and cheating stories). Top videos in the millions of views on a channel measured in hundreds of thousands of subs — **outlier view-to-sub ratio is the signature of great packaging + evergreen topics.**
- **Packaging pattern:** curiosity-gap titles about a person or mystery ("Why Nike Paid $1,000,000,000 For This" style), character-forward thumbnails. Topics chosen for search + suggested longevity, not news cycle — the anti-Jomboy.
- **Expansion path:** co-host of *Baseball Is Dead* podcast; spun up *Podcast Doesn't Exist* clips channel (Mar 2025). Essayists cap out on cadence, so they add a talk product for volume.
- **Transfers to Trevor:** the highest-leverage unclaimed lane — **first-person documentary**. Duffield researches stories; Trevor lived them. "The weirdest teammate I ever had," "What actually happens in a bullpen during a no-hitter," "The pitch that ended my career" — same 20–30 min narrative arc, but with primary-source authority no essayist can match. Story beats and re-hook placement: see Carl 01 §retention (strategy level).

### 2.3 Foolish Baseball — differentiated visual identity + one named series

- **Format:** "Baseball Bits" — sabermetric video essays wrapped in a 16-bit retro-video-game visual system. Every video instantly recognizable in the suggested feed *before reading the title*. ~357K subs, 137 videos, ~66.7M total views (as of 2025) — ~487K views/video average on a small channel: quality-over-volume works when identity is unmistakable.
- **Editorial angle:** makes *unheralded* players (Tim Locastro, Jeff Mathis) fascinating via stats — contrarian subject selection in a niche that over-covers stars. MLB officially endorsed the channel.
- **Tactical takeaways:** (1) a proprietary visual system is a thumbnail strategy — consistent art style = channel-level brand CTR; (2) one named series carried the entire channel; (3) low cadence (roughly monthly) is viable when every upload is an event.
- **Transfers to Trevor:** pick one distinctive, ownable visual/graphic system for Trevor May Baseball analysis videos (e.g., a consistent "pitcher's-eye" telestration style — the Telestrator tool is an in-house asset here) and never deviate. Name the series.
- Contrarian-subject proof point: Bailey's Tim Locastro episode (one of the *least famous* players in MLB) broke 1M views because the framing was a puzzle ("fastest man in baseball who can't play"), not the name. Stated mission worth copying: "simplifying complicated sabermetrics to an audience that might not otherwise encounter it" — the translation seat between the analytics community and casual fans, which an ex-big-leaguer can occupy with more authority.

### 2.3b Pitching Ninja (Rob Friedman) — proof of demand for pitching-craft content

- Not an ex-pro — a lawyer/coach who became MLB media's pitching authority via overlays, pitch grips, GIF appreciation, and pitcher interviews. YouTube format: segmented recap shows — a "blast zone" home-run segment, a league-wide pitching whip-around, a comedic "Moment of Zen" closer — with rapid, irreverent commentary bridging diehards and casuals.
- His edge is **infectious appreciation of craft**: celebrating nasty pitches, not hot takes. NBC/MLB Network/ESPN analyst roles followed the audience, not the reverse.
- **Direct relevance:** Pitching Ninja proves a large audience exists specifically for *pitching* content — built by someone who never threw an MLB pitch. A retired MLB pitcher covering the same beat holds the credential Friedman lacks (what the pitch feels like, how you set it up, what the catcher called, what the hitter's body language told you). The overlays/grips/appreciation format is proven demand; the firsthand layer is open territory.

### 2.4 Trevor Bauer (@BauerOutage) — the direct comp: MLB pitcher as creator

- **Format mix:** "Tips with Trev" instructional playlist, pitch-development vlogs ("I Threw My Brand New Pitch"), in-game decision explainers, daily-life access, merch store. He built the template of *pitcher-documents-his-own-development* — the pitch-design video is inherently serialized (tease → build → test → game debut).
- **What worked:** year-round content (offseason lab work fills the calendar dead zone), the development-arc series structure, and answering audience questions directly. He ran a real operation — agent Rachel Luba + a dedicated production team ("Momentum") — not a phone-and-hope setup.
- **The 85/15 ratio (memorize this):** on Twitter/X Bauer kept ~85% engagement content (fan interaction, league commentary, humor) to ~15% promotion (merch, sponsors, YouTube plugs) — and the 15% converted *because* of the 85%. Result: 475K+ followers, out-drawing bigger stars (Judge, Soto) whose feeds were mostly sponsored posts, plus strong merch sales through his own site. Trust is the conversion engine; promo-heavy feeds kill it.
- **What to learn from cautiously:** Bauer's channel is welded to his playing status and controversy; the durable, brand-safe version of this archetype is the *retired* pitcher who teaches and entertains without needing active-roster footage.
- **Transfers to Trevor:** the pitch-design/dev-arc format works in reverse for a retiree: "Can I get my fastball back to 95 at 36?", "Teaching a 15-year-old my changeup in 30 days," "Rebuilding my mechanics with the Neptune lab tech." Each is a multi-episode arc with a measurable stake — the strongest retention structure in the athlete lane.

### 2.5 Bryson DeChambeau — the athlete-creator archetype, canonical version

- **Numbers (as of mid-2026):** 2.6M+ subs, 100+ recreational videos in ~3 years, one long-form challenge video roughly every 2 weeks — described by SportsPro as "textbook steady release cadence."
- **Stated strategy (his own words):** golf is 4 hours and "quite boring" — the craft is "condensing it down to an hour" that can go viral. Plus: "be yourself, do the content you want to do, and **listen to the comments section** to see what viewers want." He iterates formats directly off comment demand.
- **Signature format:** "Break 50" — a single, permanent, numeric challenge frame with a rotating celebrity guest slot (guests bring their audience; the format guarantees stakes). One format, infinitely repeatable, packaging writes itself: guest face + number. Ceiling proof: the Trump episode drew ~16M views; guests (Steph Curry and up) say yes *knowing* they'll gain subs, invites, or sponsor value — guest quality followed channel performance, not the reverse.
- **Production economics & discipline:** Forbes pegs his production spend around **$800K/yr (as of late 2025)** — multiple shooters, drones, high-fidelity cameras. Detail-level discipline: in the Curry collab he paused conversation until the cart stopped for clean audio. Among athlete channels, competent production alone clears most of the field — but note the spend came *after* the format proved out, not before. The channel also actively **rehabilitated his public image** from analytical slow-play villain to likable goofball: for any athlete with a complicated public perception, the channel is reputation infrastructure, not just revenue.
- **Network play (Apr 2026):** co-launched **Source Golf** with Grant Horvat + Bryan Bros — an aggregation network selling advertising against pooled creator inventory. The next stage of athlete-creator monetization is collective ad sales.
- **Transfers to Trevor:** build one numeric, repeatable challenge format. Candidates: "Strike Out X" (can guest hitter touch Trevor's stuff — MLB pros, college kids, celebrities), a velocity-target arc, or "9 outs" mini-games at the Neptune facility. The guest slot is the growth engine; the number is the stakes.

### 2.6 Good Good Golf — the content-to-commerce flywheel

- **Numbers (as of late 2025):** founded 2020; 15-creator roster; ~3M subs across Good Good, Good Good Girls, Good Good Pros, Good Good Mini Golf; **$40M+ revenue, ~75% from apparel/products**; raised $45M (Mar 2025, Creator Sports Capital + Omaha Productions among 50+ investors); title-sponsors a PGA Tour event (est. $12–15M/yr); reviving *The Big Break* on Golf Channel.
- **Explicit blueprint:** CEO Matt Kendrick copied fishing brand Googan Squad — YouTube channel → merch → e-retailer. Five revenue verticals: YouTube, sponsorships, live events, wholesale, DTC. **AdSense is the smallest lever.** Supporting numbers: 498M lifetime views (+29% YoY), product in 800+ Dick's Sporting Goods locations, a seven-figure Callaway deal, first live event drew 1M+ concurrent viewers. Flywheel mechanics (RockWater's framing): content = zero-CAC audience acquisition → passionate niche fandom → high-margin D2C sales → profits fund better content; marketing becomes a profit center.
- **Team structure & casting note:** ~10 producer/editors behind ~15 on-camera personalities; members keep their personal channels — exclusivity covers *apparel rights only*. Ensemble + individual channels = an audience lattice where each member's channel feeds the mothership.
- **Format doctrine:** team-vs-team competition, challenges (blindfolds, budget clubs), personality chemistry over golf skill; "the 18-hole vlog has gone out the window" — audiences want high-dopamine, compressed competition.
- **Transfers to Trevor:** the flywheel maps 1:1 to Neptune Performance — content builds the brand, the facility/merch/training products capture the value. Also validates sub-brand channel splitting *only after* the flagship is big (Good Good split into Girls/Pros/Mini after scale, not before). Don't fragment More Mayday/TMB further until one of them has escape velocity.

### 2.7 Grant Horvat — the collab-ladder solo creator

- **Path:** college golfer → joined Good Good (2019) → left 2022 to go solo → 1M subs by end of 2023, ~1.5M by 2025 → ~$300–500K/yr from AdSense alone, plus brand deals, **equity instead of sponsorship** (Takomo Golf stake — creator-maturity monetization: own a piece rather than rent your face), and commerce.
- **AdSense reality check for the whole niche:** Rick Shiels at 3.07M subs (June 2026) earns only ~$22K/month in ad revenue (~$3 RPM, ~$250–300K/yr) — instruction-heavy content, lower engagement per sub. At every scale studied, ads are the scoreboard, not the business. One offsetting tailwind: sports YouTube's male 25–54 demo attracts premium advertisers, so entertainment formats in this niche run above-platform RPMs even at modest views.
- **Method:** first solo uploads were **collabs with already-large channels** (Rick Shiels, Bryan Bros) — he never cold-started. Ladder continued to Tiger Woods. Style: cinematic, calm, one-guest-one-round; the guest is the packaging (face + name in title/thumbnail).
- **2026 moves:** Source Golf network; "Your Golf Tour" — a creator-led competitive circuit for 16 YouTube golfers, $1M finale at Wynn Las Vegas. Creators are now building their own leagues rather than waiting for TV.
- **Transfers to Trevor:** Trevor's rolodex is the most under-priced asset he owns. A standing one-guest format ("throw a bullpen with me / hit off me / tour the lab") converts relationships into subscriber-transferring collabs. Book guests with existing YouTube/social audiences first — audience transfer only happens when the guest promotes or their fans search.

### 2.8 Entertainment-crossover: Jesser, Dude Perfect, Deestroying, Bob Does Sports

- **Jesser** (41M+ subs): built on the cultural layer of sports TV ignores — sneakers, video games, driveway challenges. Production doctrine (his words): "putting the right people in the right environment and then not trying to control every second" — plan the structure, keep the mess. Now runs episodic series (*Pros vs. YouTubers*, *Summer of Soccer*) and signed a Tubi original-series deal (Mar 2026). Positioning quote: "Creators are not replacing the game. We are helping bring more people into it."
- **Dude Perfect:** trick shots + comedy since 2009; proof that a repeatable spectacle format (attempt → fail montage → celebration) outlives any single sport's news cycle; now landing YouTube-exclusive original shows (2026). The endgame numbers: 60M+ subs (4th-most-followed sports channel on earth, ahead of every league), $100M+ from Highmount Capital, $50M+ lifetime from the Nerf toy line alone, 50+ sold-out arena tours. **Cadence discipline even at the top: one ~30-minute video every other Saturday**, weeks of production each — quality-per-upload scales; upload count doesn't have to.
- **Deestroying** (ex-college kicker): the athlete-turned-entertainer path — challenge content + athlete guests; Tubi deal for creator-driven sports storytelling (2026). Demonstrates streaming platforms are now acquiring sports-creator formats outright.
- **Bob Does Sports** (1.1M subs): personality-first "golf, but it's a hangout with drinks" — hosted the 2025 **Internet Invitational**: 48 creators, $1M purse, **29M views across 6 episodes**, prize pool rising to $4M for 2026. Creator-run events are the niche's new tentpoles.
- **Transfers to Trevor:** More Mayday is the crossover vehicle. The mechanism that works: take baseball skill out of the stadium and into game-show/challenge structures where non-baseball fans can follow the stakes. Also: get Trevor *into* other creators' events (Warehouse Games, creator tournaments, Internet-Invitational-style events) — appearing in someone else's 29M-view series is worth more than a year of solo uploads.

### 2.9 Athlete talk shows — Gil's Arena, Funky Friday, Talkin' Baseball

- Retired athletes' fastest-scaling format (2024–2026): unfiltered panel/interview shows built for clipping. Gilbert Arenas (Gil's Arena) and Cam Newton (Funky Friday) lead the NBA/NFL versions; Trevor Plouffe went from ex-MLB infielder to Jomboy's *Talkin' Baseball* franchise face — the proof that an ex-player's *opinion + fluency* is a durable daily product.
- Mechanics: long-form episode (60–120 min) → 10–20 vertical clips per episode → clips do discovery, episode does depth, YouTube long-form does monetization. (Shorts↔long-form decoupling, late 2025: clips can't hurt the long-form — strategy level: see ../../Carl/organic-marketing/01-youtube-growth-strategy.md.)
- **Transfers to Trevor:** "Mayday! with Trevor May" should be run as a clip factory against this playbook; the differentiator vs. generic pods is booking guests only Trevor can get and segments only a pitcher can host (e.g., re-living specific at-bats with the hitter who was in the box).

## 3. Cross-cutting packaging/format/cadence patterns

Observed across every winning channel above:

1. **Named, repeatable series carry channels.** Breakdown / Baseball Bits / Break 50 / Pros vs. YouTubers / Talkin' Baseball. Naming a format lets suggested-feed viewers self-serialize. Target: 2–3 named formats per channel; retire or replace ones that decay two uploads in a row.
2. **The guest is the thumbnail.** In athlete/collab formats the packaging formula is guest face + host face + stakes text (≤4 words). Golf proved a guest with an audience is worth more than any thumbnail trick.
3. **Compression is the skill.** 4-hour round → 1 hour (Bryson). 3-hour game → 8-minute breakdown (Jomboy). A season-long dev arc → 4 episodes (Bauer). The athlete-creator's editing mandate: cut everything that isn't stakes, skill, or personality.
4. **Cadence by archetype:** news-reactor daily (staff required); challenge/access every 7–14 days; documentary essay every 14–30 days; podcast 1–5x/week with daily clips. Consistency at a sustainable rate beats bursts in every case studied (as of 2026 consensus).
5. **Length norms (as of 2025–2026):** breakdowns 4–12 min; essays 15–30 min; challenge videos 20–60 min; podcasts 60–120 min. The academic study of 3,306 athlete-channel videos found **longer videos got higher engagement** in sports — the genre rewards extended narratives; don't artificially shorten.
6. **Year-round programming beats season-locked.** Bauer filled offseasons with pitch-design content; essayists are season-proof by design. Build an explicit offseason slate (training arcs, facility content, documentary essays) so Nov–Feb doesn't crater the channel.
7. **Comments are the R&D department.** DeChambeau's #1 stated growth tactic. Operationalize: after each upload, harvest the top 20 comments for format requests and guest suggestions; track which get repeated.
8. **Packaging intervention thresholds (practitioner consensus, as of 2026):** >10K impressions with CTR <4% = test new title/thumbnail; <2% = urgent. Documented case: one thumbnail swap on a stalled evergreen video → +42% CTR → impressions and watch time doubled within a month. Sports-specific pattern-interrupt: a bold stat/number in the thumbnail (the tension-triangle split: thumbnail = emotional trigger, title = rational reason, hook = promise fulfilled — if any leg fails, the chain collapses). Full packaging theory: see ../../Carl/organic-marketing/01-youtube-growth-strategy.md.
9. **Event-release beats cadence anxiety at low volume.** Baseball Doesn't Exist averages ~1.4 uploads/month with gaps up to 120 days and still compounds — because every release is packaged as an event and the library is evergreen. A new channel with *neither* cadence nor event-scale packaging gets neither habit nor spike; pick one.

## 4. What transfers to a retired-MLB-pitcher channel — the priority list

Ranked by (evidence strength × fit to Trevor's assets):

1. **Numeric challenge series with guest slot** (Break 50 model) — highest ceiling, uses pitching skill + rolodex + Neptune facility as set. Multi-episode arcs with measurable stakes.
2. **First-person documentary essays** (Baseball Doesn't Exist model, upgraded with lived experience) — evergreen, search-friendly, view-to-sub outlier potential; fills offseason.
3. **Pitcher's-mind breakdowns of current MLB moments** (Jomboy model + player credibility) — news-cadence growth lever; requires speed discipline (publish within 24–48h of the moment).
4. **Development-arc series** (Bauer model, retiree version) — "comeback/rebuild/teach" arcs with a stated goal and deadline.
5. **Podcast clip factory** (Gil's Arena/Talkin' Baseball model) — volume + discovery for "Mayday!"; ex-player-only segments as differentiation.
6. **Crossover challenge entertainment on More Mayday** (Jesser/Bob Does Sports model) — baseball skills in game-show frames legible to non-fans; guest creators over guest players here.
7. **League/creator-program capture** — apply/position for MLB Creator Class adjacency, MLB Players Studio (MLBPA's athlete content platform, launched via Portal A), TikTok/MLB highlight-licensing access. The 2026 expanded MLB×TikTok deal gives select creators access to **current and archival footage** plus creator lounges at MLB Player Houses working directly with players — the first time MLB opened its content vault to outside creators. As of 2025–2026 MLB is actively funding and licensing to creators; a former player is the ideal candidate profile. These programs also solve the highlight-rights problem that constrains every baseball channel — plan each format's rights posture up front (transformed footage / archival fair-use / sanctioned access).
8. **Commerce flywheel** (Good Good model) — treat channels as top-of-funnel for Neptune Performance programs, training products, merch; measure channel success partly in facility leads, not just RPM.

## 5. The credibility-arbitrage playbook (step-by-step)

The structural advantage of an ex-player over every media-native competitor, turned into repeatable production:

1. **Inventory the "only I can" list.** Things only a retired MLB pitcher can do on camera: throw MLB-quality stuff, decode pitch selection in real games, narrate what the mound feels like, call former teammates/opponents, read a bullpen, use pro-grade lab tech credibly.
2. **Attach each item to an archetype.** Throw → challenge series. Decode → breakdowns. Narrate → documentaries. Call → collabs/podcast. Lab tech → dev arcs (and Neptune marketing).
3. **Lead the title/thumbnail with the credential once, then the stakes.** "MLB Pitcher" belongs in channel branding and occasional titles; the *video* packaging leads with the question/stakes ("Can a D1 hitter touch a big-league slider?"). Credential fatigue is real — stakes never fatigue.
4. **Book the collab ladder deliberately.** Sequence: peers with small audiences (practice) → baseball creators (Jomboy ecosystem, Baseball Is Dead orbit, Foolish Baseball) → athlete-creators cross-sport → celebrity/entertainment guests. Each rung should roughly 2–5x the audience exposure of the last.
5. **Convert every appearance into a format.** Guest on someone's show → invite them back onto a named series of Trevor's within 60 days. One-way appearances leak value.
6. **Publish the insider take within the news window.** For breakdown-type content, the moment's search/suggested surge lasts ~48–72 hours; have a lightweight breakdown pipeline (screen-record + telestrate + one-take VO) that can ship same-day.

## 6. Entertainment-crossover plays (getting beyond baseball fans)

- **The Jesser mechanism:** cover the *culture around* the sport (collectibles, gear, challenges, video-game versions) — the layer casual and non-fans share. Baseball equivalents: card collecting/breaks, stadium food tiers, bat/glove tech, MLB The Show crossovers (game content, not streamer culture).
- **The stakes-legibility rule:** a non-fan can't follow ERA, but can follow "$1,000 if you get one hit off him." Convert baseball skill into universally legible stakes (money, counts, ladders, brackets).
- **The event play:** creator-run tentpoles are the fastest-growing surface (Internet Invitational: 29M views/6 eps; Your Golf Tour: $1M finale; Warehouse Games with MLB players). Path: appear in others' events (2026) → co-host a baseball version at Neptune (2027+). A facility is exactly the venue this niche lacks.
- **The streaming exit (as of 2026):** Tubi buying Jesser/Deestroying originals and YouTube commissioning Dude Perfect shows means proven creator formats now have a second buyer. Format IP (named series with episode structure) is what gets acquired — another reason to build named series, not videos.
- **Crossover guardrail:** the MDPI study found off-sport content is where *active* athletes drift (30%+ off-sport) and it dilutes channel identity; retired athletes who stayed 87.6% on-sport kept clearer audience signals. Do crossover *formats*, keep baseball as the spine — the algorithm needs a consistent audience cluster to recommend into (strategy level: see ../../Carl/organic-marketing/01-youtube-growth-strategy.md).

## 7. What the data says (athlete-channel study, N=3,306 videos)

MDPI/PMC content analysis of 20 top athlete-run channels (10 active, 10 retired athletes; 2020–2021; Korean market but directionally useful):

- Dominant video length: 3–10 min (60.4%); average 624 seconds — but **longer videos correlated with higher user reactions**, contradicting short-form assumptions; sports audiences reward extended narrative.
- Retired athletes: 50.2% lessons/instructional content (vs 18.7% active), 87.6% on-sport, 25.7% of videos carried paid ads (vs 9.1% active) — retirees monetize harder via sponsorship, and brands accept it.
- Active athletes out-engaged retired across views/likes/comments (p < 0.001) — relevance decays post-career; a retired athlete's channel must win on *format quality*, not name.
- Highest-engagement content categories: eating/daily-life/entertainment > pure instruction — even instructional channels should wrap teaching in entertainment framing (challenge, story, guest).
- Average views 289,910 (range 740–19.4M) — massive variance = idea/packaging dependence, same as general YouTube (strategy level: Carl 01, "the idea sets the ceiling").

## 8. Common mistakes (observed failure modes in this niche)

1. **The retirement vlog trap.** Defaulting to day-in-the-life content because it's easy to film. The data says retirees win on structured instructional/entertainment formats; unstructured vlogs are the lowest-performing athlete content and are algorithmically illegible (no consistent audience signal).
2. **Assuming the name carries the channel.** Active-athlete engagement premium disappears at retirement; channels that don't replace "watch because I'm famous" with "watch because the format is great" decay within 18 months.
3. **Copying Jomboy's cadence without Jomboy's staff.** Daily news-reaction requires a team; a solo operator attempting it burns out (O'Brien: 9am–2am, $500/month year). Match cadence to archetype and headcount.
4. **One-off videos instead of named series.** Every winner in this file runs named formats. Unnamed one-offs can't compound in suggested traffic.
5. **Professionalizing the tone along with the production.** Jomboy's "professional by broadcast, amateurish by tone" is the rule; channels that start sounding like a network show lose the parasocial edge that beat networks in the first place.
6. **Guest collabs with no audience transfer.** Booking impressive names who have zero online audience and don't promote. A 100K-sub creator guest usually outperforms a famous-but-offline Hall of Famer for growth (book the HOFer for the podcast, the creator for the challenge video).
7. **Season-locked programming.** Channels that only work April–October lose 5 months of momentum yearly; the algorithm's returning-viewer signals decay in the gap.
8. **Skipping the commerce layer.** AdSense-only athlete channels leave 75%+ of the business on the table (Good Good ratio). The facility/products funnel should be designed into content from episode one, not bolted on.
9. **Fragmenting into sub-channels too early.** Good Good split only after flagship scale. Splitting audience before either channel has escape velocity slows both.
10. **Ignoring league creator programs.** MLB is actively distributing money, IP rights, and event access to creators (Creator Class, Jomboy stake, TikTok archive licensing, MLB Players Studio) — as of 2026 this is the cheapest distribution in baseball media and most athlete-creators haven't applied.

## 9. Questions Ashley should ask

- Which 2–3 named series are we committing to per channel this quarter, and what's the kill criterion (e.g., two consecutive uploads below channel median views)?
- What is the "only Trevor can do this" element of this video idea? If none, why are we making it instead of an essayist who out-edits us?
- Who is the guest, what audience do they bring, and have they agreed to promote? What rung of the collab ladder is this?
- What's the numeric, legible stake in this video ("Break 50" test: can a non-fan state what winning looks like in one sentence)?
- Is this idea compressible to its stakes — what's the ratio of footage shot to runtime, and what gets cut?
- What's the offseason slate? Which arcs are banked for Nov–Feb?
- How does this video feed Neptune Performance or a product (link, CTA, on-screen facility presence), and are we measuring leads, not just views?
- Have we harvested the last upload's comments for format/guest demand? What did viewers ask for twice?
- Is this a news-window breakdown (must ship in 48h) or evergreen (can wait for polish)? Are we resourcing it accordingly?
- What's our status with MLB Creator Class / MLB Players Studio / MLBPA content programs, and who owns that relationship?
- If this format works 5 times in a row, what's the event/commerce/licensing version of it (Internet Invitational test)?
